# Chapter Ten — The Match

*Cal*

The fixture was the biggest of the season — a regional final, scouts in the stand, the sort of game that the people who decided futures came to watch decide them. Cal's father drove up from the south for it. He always did. He sat in the same seat he always sat in, third row behind the posts, and he did not cheer, because cheering was for people who were spectating, and Gareth Vance was not spectating, he was *invested*, which was a colder and heavier thing.

Cal hadn't told Ella about the match. This was a thing he'd done without deciding to do it, the way he did most of his self-protective things — he'd simply not mentioned it, kept the two worlds in their two boxes, the way he'd kept them his whole life. But Tom told Priya, and Priya told Ella, and so on the day of the regional final Cal jogged out onto the pitch in front of the biggest crowd of his life, looked up into the stand on instinct to find his father's cold invested stillness, and found, two rows behind him and four seats over, Ella Savage-Oluwa, wrapped in a scarf in his club's colours that Tom had clearly procured, sitting next to a beaming Priya, watching him with an expression he couldn't read from forty metres but which made his chest do something complicated.

Both worlds. In one stand. His father and his — whatever the word was, the word he hadn't let himself use. Four seats apart and neither knowing who the other was.

He played the worst first half of his life.

He couldn't find the stillness. The counting wouldn't come. Every time he had the ball he was aware of being watched by the two people in the world whose watching meant the most and meant the most *opposite* things — his father, who needed him to be only ever the fly-half, the nameable thing, the safe son who hadn't been wasted; and Ella, who knew exactly how much of him this performance left out, who'd lain with her head in his lap while the buried track played, who knew about Jordan. He threw a pass into touch. He missed a tackle. He kicked the restart dead. At half-time Marsh took him aside and said, low and furious, "Where the hell are you, Vance? You've left your head somewhere. Go and *get it.*"

And Cal stood in the cold with steam coming off him and understood the thing he'd been refusing to understand for weeks, which was that the boxes didn't work anymore. He'd built his whole life on keeping the worlds apart, on being a different complete person in each, and it had worked because no one stood in more than one room at once. But Ella stood in all of them. She was in the stand watching the fly-half and she was the only person alive who also knew the producer and the brother and the furnace under the lid, and you could not, it turned out, perform half a self for someone who had seen the whole thing. The performance had nowhere to hide. That was why he was falling apart. Not because she was watching. Because in front of her he could no longer *not* be all of it at once, and he'd never in his life been all of it at once on a pitch in front of his father.

So he stopped trying to be only the fly-half.

He went back out for the second half and he played the way he made music. He stopped calculating the odds and started feeling the shape of the game the way he felt the shape of a track — where it wanted to rise, where it wanted to hang back half a beat, where the small heartbreak of the drop went. He played *loose.* He played like Jordan heard things, like Ella saw things. The first time he got the ball he didn't think about it and the pass left his hands soft and weighted and hung in the cold air for exactly as long as it needed to, the one Marsh always said was different, and the winger ran onto it and went under the posts, and the stand came up roaring, and Cal didn't look at his father.

He looked at Ella.

She was on her feet with everyone else, scarf and all, and she wasn't doing the polite thing or the impressed thing, she was *grinning*, the real one, the one she only did when she'd forgotten to manage it, and she had her hand pressed flat to her chest in a gesture he recognised because it was the one she made when a thing he'd produced did the thing it was supposed to do, the *that's it, that's the centre* gesture, and Cal understood that she was watching him produce a rugby match, that she could see the music in it, that she was the only person in forty thousand who knew she was watching all of him at once and was not asking him to be less.

He played the second half of his life. They won by twenty. The scouts wrote things down. And when the final whistle went and the crowd poured down and his teammates piled onto him, Cal extracted himself and walked toward the stand, toward his father, because that was the ritual, that was what you did, you went to get the nod.

His father was waiting at the rail. Gareth Vance looked at his son for a long moment, and the cold invested stillness cracked, just slightly, into something that might have been, in another man, joy.

"You found it," his father said. "Second half. Whatever that was. Where'd that come from?"

And Cal, high on the win, raw from the realisation at half-time, with Ella four seats over watching, almost said it. *It came from the music, Dad. It came from playing like Jordan. It came from a girl who knows all of me. That thing you've been trying to coach into me for sixteen years, the thing that makes the pass different — it's the same thing that made Jordan hear what no one else heard, it's the same thing you called a waste, it's been in me the whole time and I've been hiding it from you because I thought it would kill you to see it.*

He almost said it. The truth was right there, the lighter-even-when-it-goes-badly truth, and his father was looking at him with something open in his face for the first time in five years.

But then his father said, "Don't lose it. That's the level the academy contract's at. That's the level that gets you capped. Whatever you did, do it every week. This is everything we worked for, son. *Everything.*" And he gripped Cal's shoulder, hard, the grip that had run Cal's whole life, and the openness in his face resolved back into the old thing, the invested thing, the *one of you is going to be the safe son and it's going to be you* thing, and the moment closed.

Cal gave him the nod. The small one. "Yeah, Dad," he said. "Every week."

And he turned away with the truth still locked in him, and across the emptying stand he found Ella, who had watched the whole exchange from four seats over, close enough to see the shape of it and too far to hear the words, and who knew him well enough to read the shape, and her grin was gone. She'd seen it. She'd seen the door open in his face and seen him close it. She'd seen him have the truth in his mouth and swallow it.

That night, in the studio, she didn't congratulate him on the match. She let him come down from it, fed him, sat with him, and only much later, with the city dark below and Jordan's four notes playing low, did she say, carefully, the way she said heavy things:

"You almost told him. At the rail. I could see it."

"Yeah."

"What stopped you?"

Cal looked at the dark window. "Same thing that always stops me. If I tell him, I lose him. He'll hear it as me choosing the thing that killed Jordan. He'll hear it as losing the last son too."

"Or," Ella said, very gently, "he'll hear that the thing he's been calling a waste for fifteen years is the exact thing that made the pass different today. The thing he was so proud of he drove up from London for. You played like that *because* of the music, Cal. You said so yourself. Maybe the truth isn't 'I'm choosing the thing that killed Jordan.' Maybe the truth is 'the thing you loved in me today and the thing you grieved in Jordan are the *same thing*, and you've spent five years trying to cut it out of the one son you've got left.'"

Cal didn't say anything. The track played. Climbed, and fell.

"I'm not telling you to tell him," Ella said. "I told you, I'd never. Visibility isn't free, I know what it costs, you know what it costs. I'm just saying —" she put her hand flat on the desk next to his, the gesture, on his side "— you keep thinking the truth is a bomb. And maybe it is. But you've been carrying a live bomb for five years so it wouldn't go off near anyone, and I've watched what carrying does to a person, and I don't want to watch it do that to you for the next fifty years. That's all. That's the only thing I'll say about it."

She didn't say anything else about it. She kept her word, the way she always kept her word, which was the thing about her that had undone him from the start.

But Cal lay awake that night, after she'd gone, in the dark studio with his brother's voice buried in the speakers, and for the first time in five years he let himself imagine saying it out loud. The whole thing. To his father. The truth as a fact in the world instead of a bomb in his chest. And it terrified him so completely that he understood, finally, how much he'd come to want it — because you only get that scared of the things you've decided, somewhere underneath, that you're actually going to do.
