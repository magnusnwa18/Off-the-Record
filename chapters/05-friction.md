# Chapter Five — Friction

*Ella*

The deal lasted about nine days before it tried to kill them both.

The trouble was that art direction and production weren't two jobs done in two rooms. They were one argument conducted in one room over many weeks, because the sound and the world had to agree, and Knox — generous, chaotic, gone half the time chasing features and freestyles — had effectively handed his project to two people who'd met as adversaries and left them to build it together.

"It's too clean," Ella said, for the third time, on the fourth session.

"It's not finished," Cal said, for the third time, not looking up from the desk.

"I'm not talking about finished, I'm talking about *intent*. The single is a 3am sound. The interlude you just played me is a 3am sound. But this —" she gestured at the monitors, where the second single sat, glossy and assured "— this is a *Saturday afternoon* sound. It's confident. It's having a nice time. It doesn't match the world I'm building."

"The world you're building." He turned in the chair, finally, and there it was, the attention, the full beam of it, except now she'd learned that the detachment was a setting he chose rather than a thing he was. "The world serves the music. Not the other way round."

"The world serves the *story*. The music's half the story. The other half is everything around it, and right now you're telling me one story with your ears and a different one with —" she stopped. "Play me the one you took the vocal out of."

He went still. "How do you know about that one."

"Because you keep almost playing it and not playing it. You did it Monday. You did it just now, your hand went to it and stopped. You've got a track you think is the real one and you're scared of it." She folded her arms. "I can't build the world right if you're hiding the centre of it from me. You hired me to see. So let me see."

The silence in the studio went on long enough that the city filled it — the shop shutter below, a bus, gulls who'd come too far inland. Cal looked at her with something she couldn't read, which was rare, because she could usually read everyone, that was the whole skill, and the fact that she couldn't read *him* half the time was a problem she had not yet admitted she found interesting.

He pulled up the track. He played it. The one with the chopped vocal taken out, then put back, then taken out — she could tell, now, that he'd left it out. And it was — God. It was the inside of something. Two minutes and forty seconds with no words, just drums that didn't want to arrive and a synth like weather and underneath it all a chopped voice, in fact, but buried so deep you felt it more than heard it, a person trying to say something through a wall.

When it ended Ella didn't say anything clever. That was the discipline of it. She'd learned, doing this for free for two years, that the moment you reach for a clever thing to say about someone's most naked work, you've told them it's a performance, and then they never show you the real thing again.

"That's it," she said simply. "That's the centre. Everything builds out from that."

"It's not for the project. It's just a —"

"I know what it is." She did. She'd built worlds for enough artists to know the difference between a song someone wrote and a song someone bled. "It's not for the project. But the project should *know it exists*. The whole identity should feel like it's circling that, even if no one ever hears it. That's the secret that got out." She looked at him. "Who's the voice? Buried in it?"

"No one."

"Cal."

"It's a sample," he said, in a voice that closed a door, and she let it close, because she'd pushed exactly as far as the work allowed and one inch past that was none of her business and she knew, the way she knew most things, that whatever was behind that door was not a thing he'd survive having forced.

So she changed the subject, and they worked, and that was the day the friction turned into something else.

---

It happened in increments, the way the only real things did. She started staying later than the session needed. He started having food there, good food, the proper Vietnamese from the place on the corner, which she pretended not to notice was always the thing she'd mentioned liking once. They argued constantly and she came to understand that the arguing *was* the work — that Cal didn't trust an idea until it had survived being attacked, and that he attacked hers because he respected them, which was a love language she recognised because it was hers too, and which no one had ever matched her at before. Priya argued to win. Her mother argued to be right. Cal argued the way Ella argued, which was to find out what was true.

"You do this thing," she told him one night, three weeks in, both of them slumped at opposite ends of the battered sofa under the high window, a single light on, the city dark and wet below. "When you're listening to something and it's good. You go completely still. Everyone else nods along or talks. You stop. Like you're —"

"Counting."

"Like you're counting." She turned her head against the sofa back to look at him. "I noticed it at the party. Before I knew it was you. You were standing in the loudest room in Fallowfield being the only still thing in it."

"You were the other still thing."

"I was leaving."

"You stayed forty minutes."

"You said forty minutes. I left at thirty-eight, to spite you."

He laughed, the real one, the one that took ten years off him, and for a second neither of them said anything and the launderette-warm hum of the studio's old heater filled the gap and Ella was aware, with the part of her that read rooms, that the room had become small and that she was choosing to stay in it.

"Why styling," he said. Not a deflection. A real question, the kind he didn't ask often, because asking gave something away. "You're — I've watched you in that meeting with Sasha. You could run anything. You could be in any room you wanted. Why this one. Your mum know you're doing this instead of —"

"Don't." It came out sharper than she meant. He raised a hand, retreating, and she sighed and softened, because he'd given her a true thing nine days ago and the ledger between them had been running ever since. "Sorry. It's a — my mum thinks this is a hobby I'm calling a career. She wants me on a graduate scheme. Law, finance, something I can say at a dinner party so the aunties nod. And the worst thing is I understand why. They came here with nothing. They built a whole life out of being twice as good and half as visible and they want me *safe*. And I keep choosing the most visible, least safe thing there is, which to her looks like — spitting on the climb."

"And to you?"

Ella looked at the dark city. No one had asked her that. Priya cheered her on; her mother grieved her in advance; no one had asked her what it *was*.

"To me it's the only thing I've ever done," she said slowly, "where I'm not performing being fine. Everywhere else I'm the strong one. The eldest. The one who's got it handled, who carries it, who never gets to fall apart because there's no one underneath me to catch the pieces." She felt the truth of it arrive even as she said it, surprised by it. "When I'm building a world for a sound, I'm not holding anything up. I'm just — seeing. It's the only place I get to put the weight down." She glanced at him. "You'd know. It's what the studio is for you. Isn't it. The one room where nobody's watching you on a touchline."

Cal went very still. The counting stillness. She watched him decide something.

"Yeah," he said, low. "That's exactly what it is."

And they sat there, two people who'd each spent their whole lives being the version of themselves that other people needed, in the one room where neither of them had to, and the heater hummed, and the rain came down on the Northern Quarter, and Ella thought: *this is a problem.* She had a deadline and a deal and a mother at six o'clock and a future she was trying to force into existence by sheer will, and the very last thing she could afford was the slow, dangerous comfort of a boy who saw exactly what she was carrying and didn't ask her to put on a brave face about it.

She stood up. "I should go. It's late."

"Yeah." He stood too, fast, and for a second they were close, the way they'd been close at the party, near enough that she could smell the cedar under the studio's coffee and electronics, and something in the air between them tightened like a held breath.

She left first. She made sure of it. Looking away first was an apology and leaving first was a victory and she needed the victory more than she needed to know what would have happened if she'd stayed.

On the stairs that smelled of old paper she stopped, one flight down, out of his sight, and pressed her hand flat against the cold wall and breathed.

"It's a problem," she told the wall.

The wall, like the rain, declined to argue. Above her, through the floor, she heard him play the buried track again — the one with the voice in it, the secret that got out — and she stood in the stairwell of the unmarked studio listening to the centre of Cal Vance through the ceiling, and she did not go back up, and she did not leave either, not for a while.
