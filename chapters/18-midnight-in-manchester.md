# Chapter Eighteen — Midnight in Manchester

*Cal & Ella*

### Cal

The launch was in a converted chapel off the top of Oldham Street, all stripped brick and stained glass and a sound system that cost more than the building, and by ten o'clock it was full of the entire scene — artists, A&Rs, the blogs, the bloggers from the blog, Sasha in understated black working the room with a smile that had gone brittle at the edges because the night had slipped her grip and she knew it. Knox owned the room the way he owned every room, a weather system in a good coat. And somewhere in the crowd, Cal knew, was a screen, and on the screen at midnight a film was going to play that a girl he'd knifed had built about him without his permission and with his whole heart in it.

He'd come. That was already the thing, the impossible thing — he'd come, hood down for once, his actual face in a room full of people who now knew his actual name, the fly-half who was secretly the ghost, and he'd felt every eye clock him and catalogue him, the way he'd spent his whole life cataloguing everyone else, and it was as horrible as he'd always known it would be and also survivable, which he hadn't known.

He hadn't replied to her text. Four days he'd had it. *I built you a door. Whether you walk through it is the only part I can't build.* He'd read it on Jordan's floor and he'd read it on the train back north and he'd read it standing outside the unmarked studio that wasn't safe anymore, and he hadn't replied, because his mother was right that a reply was just more words from the boy who'd used words to hurt her, and because he was building something too, and you don't announce the thing you're building, you build it.

He found Ella across the chapel at eleven. She was working — of course she was working, this was her night as much as Knox's, she'd built the whole world the room was standing in — and she looked exhausted and luminous and she had not seen him yet, and Cal stood in the stripped-brick dark and watched the only person who'd ever seen all of him, and let himself feel the whole size of what he'd thrown away and the whole size of what she'd built anyway, and then he did the thing he'd come to do, which was not cross the room to her.

It wasn't time yet.

First he had to do the harder thing. Because his mother had said *you'll have to do what your brother never got the chance to do, show him the music isn't the road that kills,* and you can't show someone that from a film at a launch they're not at. So Cal had made one more phone call, the call he'd been not-making for five years, and he'd said eleven words into his father's silence — *Dad, there's a film on Friday. About Jordan. Please come* — and then he'd hung up before his father could say don't, because some doors you build and then you don't get to control whether they're walked through, he understood that now, Ella had taught him that without meaning to.

And at quarter to midnight, into the converted chapel off Oldham Street, in a coat too heavy for the room and a face Cal had inherited half of, walked Gareth Vance.

### Ella

She saw the old man before she saw Cal, and she didn't know who he was, except that she did — she knew that face, she'd seen the shape of it at a rugby final from four seats over, the cold invested stillness, except it wasn't cold now, it was lost, an old man in a heavy coat standing at the back of a room full of a world he didn't understand, looking for someone.

And then she saw Cal, and her whole body did the thing it always did, the room rearranging, except this time she let it, because she'd run out of reasons not to.

He was watching his father. And his father was watching the crowd. And the two of them stood at opposite ends of the chapel, twenty metres and five years and one dead brother apart, and Ella understood in a single read what Cal had done — *he made the door for his father too. He invited the one person who told him don't come to the house. He built a room for the ghost AND the man who made him a ghost.* — and she understood that the film she'd built was about to do something far larger and more dangerous than she'd designed it to do, and there was no stopping it now, the lights were already going down.

The chapel hushed. Knox stepped up, mic in hand, and said only: "Before the album. Somebody made something. Watch it." And he stepped back, and the screen lit, and Ella's film began to play in a converted chapel for four hundred people and two men who weren't speaking.

The orange light. The wrong film stock. The night-bus ache. And under it, Cal's music — the buried track, the real one, Jordan's four notes climbing and falling, pitched so deep you felt it more than heard it. And the voiceover, anonymous, true: *There's a producer in this city who buries his name in other people's songs. People think it's mystery. It's a grave...*

Ella didn't watch the screen. She'd watched it a hundred times. She watched the old man in the heavy coat.

She watched Gareth Vance hear his dead son's melody come out of a sound system that cost more than his house, buried in the music his living son had made, and she watched him not understand, and then begin to understand, and then understand completely — *every track he's ever made has a voice in it, and the voice belongs to someone he lost, and he hides his name so that the only person who'd be hurt by the music never has to hear it* — and she watched a hard old man who poured grief into projects because he couldn't feel it find out, in a room full of strangers, that his surviving son had spent five years keeping his lost son alive in secret, in sound, building him a grave that his father could never accidentally visit and be destroyed by, *out of love, and he has never once been thanked for it,* and she watched the cold invested stillness break, all the way, finally, the thing eighteen years of marriage and two sons and one wet road had frozen, and Gareth Vance put one hand over his mouth in the dark of the chapel and his shoulders went, silently, the way his son's had gone on a desk in an unmarked studio, the same grief in the same shape passed down the same broken line.

And then the screen went black, and the white text came up, and four hundred people read it at once:

*He can step out of the corner whenever he's ready. Some of us have always been able to see him.*

The room was silent. The kind of silent Cal went when something was good. Four hundred people holding still.

### Cal

He didn't decide to do it. That was the thing he'd tell her later, the thing that finally proved his mother right — he didn't run the odds, didn't calculate, didn't read the room. The text faded on the black screen and the silence held and somewhere in it his father was coming apart and the girl who'd built the door was standing very still not looking at the screen, looking at his dad, *looking after his dad sideways the way she looked after everyone,* and Cal Vance found that his feet were already moving.

He crossed the room.

Not to Ella. First — and he'd think about why first for the rest of his life, and the answer was that she'd built it so he could — first he crossed to his father. Through the parting crowd, four hundred faces turning, the whole scene watching the ghost walk into the light with his actual face, and he reached the old man in the heavy coat and his father looked up at him with his hand still over his mouth and his eyes wrecked and wet and open, fully open, for the first time since a phone rang on a wet night five years ago.

"That was Jordan," his father said. Not a question. "In the — that was his. The bit at the end. He played me that bit once. I told him —" the old man's voice failed "— I told him there was no living in it."

"There wasn't," Cal said. "Not for him. Because you couldn't see him. So I made sure half a million people would hear him without knowing, so he'd be — so he wouldn't be gone. That's what the music is, Dad. It was never the thing that killed him. It was the only thing that kept any of him here. And it's the same thing that made the pass different. The second half, the final, the thing you drove up to watch — that was Jordan. That was the music. You've been trying to cut it out of me for five years and it's the best thing in me and it's the best thing that was in *him* and it's the same thing, Dad, it was always the same thing, and I'm done burying it. I'm done being a ghost so you don't have to feel it. Feel it. He was your son. *Feel it.*"

And Gareth Vance, who had not held his living son since his dead one died, who had poured eighteen years of unbearable feeling into the project of keeping one boy safe, reached out in a converted chapel in front of the entire Manchester music scene and pulled Cal into the heavy coat and held on, shaking, the dam finally broken, and said into his son's shoulder the thing he'd been unable to say for five years, *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I couldn't look at it, I'm sorry,* and the room, four hundred strangers and a brittle-smiled fixer and a generous chaotic artist, looked away to give them it, all except one person.

### Ella

She watched the ghost cross the room. She watched him choose his father first, and she understood why, and it broke her heart and mended it in the same motion, because *of course* he went there first — she'd built the door for exactly this, she'd built it true enough that the truest thing would happen, and the truest thing was a son and a father five years too late and just in time.

And then it was done, the held thing held, and Cal stepped back from his father with his own face wet and unhidden in front of everyone, the cool man's lid gone for good, the furnace turned all the way to warmth, and he turned, and he looked across the converted chapel, across the parting crowd, across the twenty metres and the leak and the knife and the cruelest text chopped into the cruelest order, and he found her.

He crossed the room.

The second time was harder, his mother had said, the party one was easy, this is the brave version, everyone's watching and you've already wronged her and there's no guarantee. And there was no guarantee. Ella stood her ground and made him cross all of it, because looking away first was an apology and walking toward him was a kind of looking away, and she'd spent her whole life crossing rooms to make other people comfortable and she was not going to cross this one. He had to come the whole way. He had to be the one, for once, to not stay in the corner.

He came the whole way.

He stopped in front of her, close, the way he'd stopped in front of her in the loudest room in Fallowfield, and the whole scene was watching, and Sasha was watching with her story gone completely now, taken from her and given to the only two people it ever belonged to, and Cal Vance said, low enough that only she could hear it, the way he'd said the first thing he ever said to her:

"You didn't leak me."

"No."

"I knew. The whole time. I knew it the second I sent the text and I sent it anyway because hurting you was faster than waiting to be hurt. I did the family thing. I pointed it at the one person who'd stay." His voice was wrecked and even. "You told me you wouldn't be the place I put the thing I'm too scared to feel. And I made you it anyway. And then you went and built me —" he gestured at the dark screen, at his weeping father, at the whole impossible night "— you built me the door back to my own life. While I had a knife in you. While you thought I'd never even — Ella. You turned down the offer. Knox told me. You turned down everything you've wanted your whole life so you wouldn't build a career on my worst day, and you didn't tell me, you didn't use it, you just —"

"I didn't do it for you," Ella said, and it was the truth, and she needed him to have it. "I did it because I found out who I am, and who I am doesn't get to be switched off because you stopped being able to see me. I'd have built that film if you'd married someone else. I'd have built it for the principle of it. The film's not an *I love you*, Cal. The film's an *I refuse to let your story be told by people who don't love you.* Those are different things."

"And the *I love you*?"

Ella looked at him, at the ghost who'd crossed two rooms, at his father wiping his face twenty metres back, at the world she'd built standing all around them holding still the way he went still when something was good.

"The *I love you* I'm keeping," she said. "For now. Because you knifed me in the softest place I've got, and a film fixes the story but it doesn't fix *us*, and I'm done being the strong one who forgives fast so everyone can be comfortable. You don't get the *I love you* tonight. You get the door. You walked through it. That's enough for tonight. The rest you have to earn the slow way, the boring way, the way you earned the pass — ten thousand reps of not pointing it at me when you're scared. And if you do that —" her voice finally went, one clean fracture, the strong-one cracking on purpose, for him, where he could see it "— if you do that, then there's an *I love you* at the end of it the size of the one I've been not-saying since a chip shop in Mayfield. But you have to do the reps. I'm not handing it over because you cried in a chapel. I've handed everything over my whole life. This one you work for."

And Cal Vance — who had spent his entire life calculating the odds, who had read every room he'd ever stood in, who had made himself a ghost so he'd never have to wait to be left — looked at the one person who would not let him have the easy version, who made him earn the realest thing the slow honest way, and understood that she was, once again and finally, completely right, and that being made to work for her was the first time in his life anyone had loved him enough to not just *give in.*

"Ten thousand reps," he said.

"Ten thousand."

"Starting when?"

"Started already," Ella said. "You crossed the room. That's rep one." And she let herself smile, the real one, the one she only did when she'd forgotten to manage it, except she hadn't forgotten, she'd *chosen*, which was different and better. "Take your dad home, Cal. He's a mess and he's yours and he came. The reps can wait till tomorrow. Go be somebody's son."

He went. He looked back once — and she let him, because looking back wasn't an apology, it was a promise — and then he crossed the room the third time, to his father, to take the old man home, and the film played again on a loop on the screen, the buried voice climbing and falling, and the album launched, and the scene talked about it for a year, *the night the ghost crossed the room,* and nobody ever again called Cal Vance mysterious, because mystery is just a grave with the lights off, and he'd turned the lights on, in a converted chapel, at midnight, in Manchester.

### Ella

She stayed till the end. She always did. She watched the room she'd built do its work, watched Sasha leave early with her story gone, watched Knox own the night, watched the city's whole scene fall in love with a film she'd made on no sleep for an audience of one ghost who'd shown up after all.

And at three in the morning she walked home through the wet glittering city, alone, the way she'd walked home from the loudest room in Fallowfield a hundred years ago, and it was raining, the fine orange rain under the lamps, the exact colour she'd built two whole worlds out of, and her phone buzzed in her pocket.

Cal. One line. The same number of lines they always sent each other.

*Got him home. He played me Jordan's records till 2. First time he's said his name in five years. Thank you for building the door.*

And then, a minute later, because he'd learned, because the reps had already started:

*Rep two: I'm not going to say I love you tonight either. You said work for it. So I'll say it the slow way. Goodnight, Ella. The catch can have everything else. It's not having tomorrow.*

Ella stood in the orange rain on the empty street and read it twice, and she did not text back *I love you,* because she'd meant it, he had to earn it, that was the whole point.

But she smiled the real one, alone, in the rain, where no one was watching, which meant it was only for herself, which was a thing she was learning to allow.

And she texted back one word, which was not *I love you* and was the beginning of it:

*Tomorrow.*

Then she put the phone face down, the way he always did, and she walked home through midnight in Manchester, the strong one who'd finally put the weight down in the middle where someone could see it, the eye who'd built a hundred worlds for other people and had, at last, started building one for herself — and somewhere across the wet gold city a ghost sat in a flat in Catford that wasn't his, listening to his brother's records with his father, fully visible, fully seen, doing rep two of ten thousand, and meaning every one.
