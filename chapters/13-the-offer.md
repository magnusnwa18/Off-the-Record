# Chapter Thirteen — The Offer

*Ella*

The offer came on a Thursday, which Ella would later appreciate as the universe's idea of a joke, because everything that mattered in this story happened on a Thursday, and she should have learned by now to be afraid of them.

It came from a woman named Imogen Clarke-Bayo, who ran the most respected new creative studio in London — the kind that did the album campaigns and the brand films and the fashion-house collaborations, the kind whose work Ella had been screenshotting into reference folders since she was sixteen. Imogen had seen the Knox campaign. Imogen had, specifically, seen Ella's name in the third paragraph of the culture-site piece, and had then gone and found everything Ella had ever made, the free work, the reimaginings, the degree-show teasers, and Imogen had emailed her personally, and now Ella was sitting in a too-expensive coffee place in Soho on a day-return train ticket she could barely afford, across from a woman who held her entire future in a flat white.

"You've got the rarest thing," Imogen said. "You can hear. Most people in this industry can only see — they make things look right. You make things *sound* right, visually, which makes no sense and is the whole job. The Knox work isn't styled, it's *scored.* I want you." She said it plainly, the way powerful people who don't need to perform power say things. "Junior creative director. Not an intern, not a runner — I've got six of those. A real seat. You'd start in September, after your show. You'd work on the campaigns you've been screenshotting. I'll pay you properly, which no one in this industry does for someone your age, and I'm only doing it because I've watched twenty years of agencies let people like you walk out the door because they couldn't see what you'd be in five years. I can see it. So." She slid a folded thing across the table. "That's the offer. It's good. Take a week."

Ella looked at the folded offer and did not unfold it, because she could feel her hands wanting to shake and she would not let them shake in front of Imogen Clarke-Bayo.

It was everything. It was the entire argument she'd had with her mother, won, in writing — *the thing you're good at IS a thing you can build a life on, here is a respected woman paying you properly to prove it.* It was the answer to six o'clock. It was the validation she'd been clawing toward for two months of polite nos. It was, in a sentence, the thing she'd come to Manchester at eighteen to make possible.

"Why now," Ella heard herself say. "Why this week. The album's not even out. You could have waited for the full campaign."

Imogen smiled, and it was a real smile, but there was an edge of the industry in it, the thing Ella was learning to read. "Honest answer? Because the Knox project is about to get very loud, and I want you signed before it does. There's a — your name's attached to a story that's about to break. The producer. The mystery one, V. Everyone in London's heard the reveal's coming — Sasha Reyes has been seeding it for a fortnight, she's good, she's got three outlets circling. And when that breaks, the whole campaign goes supernova, and you, the eye who built the ghost's world, you become the person everyone wants, and I won't be able to afford you." Imogen tilted her head. "So I'm getting you a week early. Before the explosion. While you're still a secret too." A pause, lighter, but not light at all. "Between us — do you know who he is? V? Because if you did, and you wanted to tell me, off the record, it wouldn't change the offer. The offer stands either way. But I won't pretend it wouldn't be useful to know what's coming. Forewarned, and all that."

And there it was again. The same line, in a more expensive coffee shop, from a kinder mouth. *Off the record. It'd be useful. The girl who unmasked the ghost.* Sasha had seeded it so well that it had grown into the soil of Ella's actual dream job, and now the two things were tangled together at the root, and Ella understood, sitting in Soho with the offer folded in front of her, the true shape of the catch at last.

It wasn't that the reveal would expose Cal. It was that *everything Ella wanted was now downstream of the reveal.* The album going supernova, this offer existing at all, her career launching the way Imogen described — all of it ran through the explosion of Cal's secret. She could keep his secret and watch the project stay smaller and the offer maybe quietly evaporate when the supernova didn't come. Or the reveal could happen — on Sasha's terms, in the worst version, the headline version, the version that would reach Cal's father as a betrayal — and Ella's career would launch on the back of the single most destructive day of the person she loved.

She couldn't win clean. That was the catch. There was no version where she got the thing she wanted that didn't cost Cal the thing he was most afraid of. Sasha had built it that way on purpose, the way Ella built worlds, with total command of how every element served the story — and the story Sasha was telling was one where Ella's ambition and Cal's secret were set against each other so that *someone* would crack and the reveal would come, and it didn't matter to Sasha which of them broke, because either break made her story.

"I don't know who he is," Ella said, and it was the first time she'd said the lie to someone she actually liked, and it cost her something. "I just made the outside of the door look nice."

Imogen looked at her for a long moment, the way Sasha had looked at her, except where Sasha's look was inventory, Imogen's was almost — respect. "You know," she said, "I believe you don't want to tell me, which isn't the same as you not knowing, but it's a better answer than most people would give me, because most people would lie *enthusiastically.* Loyalty's rare in this business. It usually gets people nowhere." She stood, gathered her coat. "Take the week. The offer's real. And Ella — whatever the thing is that you're protecting? I hope it's worth the supernova passing you by. Because it might. I'm being straight with you: if the reveal doesn't happen, the project stays a cult thing, and a cult thing is a lovely line on a CV but it's not the thing that makes me unable to afford you. The offer stands either way. But the *size* of your start — that's downstream of the story breaking. So you should know exactly what the loyalty's costing you before you spend it." She put a card on the table next to the folded offer. "Most people never find out who they really are because they never have to choose between what they want and who they want to be. You're about to. Lucky you. It's horrible. Call me."

---

Ella didn't unfold the offer until she was on the train, north, through the dark, and when she did the number on it was more than her parents had made between them most years she was growing up, and she sat with it in the rattling carriage and let herself, for one stop, just *want* it. Want it cleanly, the way she never let herself want anything. The seat, the money, the campaigns, the answer to six o'clock, the proof. She'd earned it. God, she'd earned it. She'd been the strong one her whole life and here, finally, was the world saying *yes, the thing you are is a thing you can build a life on, here is the life, take it.*

And all she had to do to make it as big as it could be was let the worst day of Cal's life happen.

Or all she had to do to protect Cal was let the biggest chance of her life shrink to a cult line on a CV.

She thought about her mother at the kitchen table at midnight, not crying so the children wouldn't see. She thought about Cal with his head on the desk, *you can't bury a man who's already buried himself.* She thought about the toothbrush and the second mug and the morning the catch wasn't allowed to have. She thought about Sasha's smile with no eyes in it, and how she, Ella, had been outplayed by someone who built stories the way Ella built worlds, someone who'd put the two people she'd boxed against each other and was waiting, patient, for one of them to make the reveal inevitable.

And somewhere outside Stockport, in the dark, with the offer in her hands and the lights of the towns going by like a sound she couldn't quite hear, Ella Savage-Oluwa stopped trying to win clean — because there was no clean win, Sasha had made sure of that — and started, instead, to think about how you change the game when someone else wrote the rules. Not how to choose between what she wanted and who she wanted to be.

How to refuse the choice itself. How to take the story away from the person telling it.

She got out her phone. She did not text Cal. She texted Sasha, three words, the first move of a game she was going to write the rules of herself this time:

*Let's get coffee.*

Then she folded the offer back up, and put it in her bag, and watched the dark country go by, and for the first time in the whole story she was not the one being read. She was the one doing the reading. And she had finally, after twenty-one years of carrying, found a weight she was glad to pick up: the weight of fighting for someone else's right to be seen instead of just protecting her own.
