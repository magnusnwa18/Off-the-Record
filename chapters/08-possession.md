# Chapter Eight — Possession

*Cal*

The problem with finally wanting something, Cal discovered, was that it didn't arrive alone. It dragged the whole rest of him in behind it — the parts he kept locked, the intensity he'd spent years pouring into a sport and a sound so it wouldn't have anywhere else to go. He'd thought he was a cool man. He'd built a cool man, carefully, over years. It turned out the cool man was a lid, and Ella Savage-Oluwa had taken the lid off, and underneath it Cal Vance was not cool at all. Cal Vance, underneath, was a furnace.

He noticed it first at a thing of Knox's — a low-key album playback in a bar off Stevenson Square, industry only, the kind of room Sasha curated. Ella was there in her professional capacity, working the room, and she was *good* at it, better than good, she moved through the industry crowd like she'd been doing it for years, and Cal stood in his corner being V, the silent magic, and watched a man he half-recognised — a manager, slick, older — put his hand on the small of Ella's back to steer her toward someone, and Cal's vision did a thing he didn't like.

It went very clear and very narrow. The counting stillness, except not calm. He was across the room before he'd decided to move, and he caught himself two steps in, and stopped, and stood there with his heart doing something violent and ridiculous, appalled at himself.

Ella, of course, handled it. She stepped neatly out from under the hand without seeming to, redirected, kept her smile, gave the man nothing — she didn't need rescuing, had never needed rescuing, would have been *insulted* by rescuing. And later, when she found Cal in his corner, she read his face in the way she read everything.

"You went somewhere just now," she said. "When Declan put his hand on my back."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, and I want to be really clear about something." She kept her voice low and even, and there was no anger in it, which was worse than anger, because it meant she'd thought about this before it happened. "I will not be a thing you protect, Cal. I've spent my whole life being the strong one and I am not trading one version of carrying for another. If you go full caveman every time a man stands near me, this ends. I mean it. I need a partner, not a bodyguard, and definitely not an owner."

It was the exact right thing to say and it landed exactly where it should have, in the part of him that was his father's son, the part that confused intensity with control. He took a breath. The narrow vision widened back out.

"You're right," he said. "I'm sorry. It's not —" he struggled, because he wasn't built to explain himself, he was built to *not*. "When I want something, I want it like a problem I have to solve. I get — I lock onto it. It's how I am with rugby, with the music, I go all the way in, there's no setting between off and everything. And I've never pointed it at a person before. So I don't know how to — aim it. Without it becoming the thing you're describing. I'll learn. But you'll have to tell me when I get it wrong, because I won't always see it. That's the deal I can actually make. Not that I'll never get it wrong. That I'll listen when you tell me I have."

Ella looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face softened, because that, too, was the right thing to say, the realer thing — not a promise to be perfect but a promise to be corrigible, which was the only promise worth anything.

"Okay," she said. "That's a deal I'll take." Then, because she could never let a moment stay heavy: "Caveman."

"I didn't even do anything."

"You did the *face*. You've got a face. It says 'I have considered three ways to remove this man's hand and I'm choosing the merciful one.'"

"Two ways," Cal said. "I'm not as scary as you think."

"You're exactly as scary as I think," Ella said, "which is why I like you, which is a flaw I'm working on," and she went back to the room, and he let her, and that was the lesson, the whole lesson: the wanting didn't get to be a leash. He could feel the furnace and not feed it into someone else. It was the hardest thing he'd ever practised, harder than the pass, and he practised it the same way, ten thousand reps, and got it wrong a hundred times, and she told him every time, and he listened every time, and slowly, over weeks, the furnace learned to be warmth instead of fire.

---

They didn't tell anyone. That was the other thing about it — the secrecy that had been Cal's whole life now had a second floor, because if the scene found out V was dating the project's art director it became gossip, became a story, became exactly the loose thread Sasha was looking for, and if the rugby world found out Cal was *anything* in the music scene the whole edifice came down. So they were careful. They were so careful. And the carefulness, perversely, made it more — every stolen hour at the unmarked studio more charged, every glance across an industry room more loaded, every late walk home through the wet city more theirs precisely because no one else could have it.

He learned her. This was the part that undid him, the part the cool man could never have survived. He learned that she ran on three hours' sleep when she was building something and then crashed for twelve. That she called her mother every day at six and put on a voice for it and was wrung out for an hour after. That she'd been the strong one so long she didn't know how to be looked after and flinched from it like a hand near a burn, so he learned to look after her sideways — the right food appearing, the heater already on, the deadline he quietly took thirty minutes of pressure off by handling a thing she didn't know he'd handled. Never announced. Never a debt. Just the weight, briefly, off, the way she'd taken his weight that night without trying to lift it.

"You're doing the thing," she said one night, three weeks into the secret, lying with her head in his lap on the studio sofa while he worked one-handed on a mix, the other hand in her hair. "The sideways looking-after thing. The Vietnamese was here when I got here. I didn't order it."

"Don't know what you mean."

"I mean I notice. I notice everything, it's my whole job. I just — let you. Which I don't do. I don't let people." Her eyes were closed. The track played low. Jordan's four notes were in it somewhere, buried, climbing and falling. "It's the most frightening thing I've ever done, letting you. I wanted it on the record."

Cal looked down at her, at the one person who'd ever walked into the room where he kept the only true thing about himself and decided to stay, and he felt the furnace turn all the way to warmth, no fire in it at all, and he understood that he'd been wrong his whole life about what wanting was.

"It's on the record," he said softly, and kept his hand in her hair, and let the city tumble on below, and for a little while two people who'd spent their whole lives being seen by everyone and known by no one got to be, in one unmarked room, fully and dangerously and entirely known.

He should have known the bill always comes for that. In his experience it always did. But he was happy, for the first time he could remember, properly happy, and happiness made even Cal Vance — who calculated everything — forget, for a few weeks, to count the odds.
