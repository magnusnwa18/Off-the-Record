# Chapter Seven — Late Nights

*Ella*

The shoot for the first single happened in a railway arch in Mayfield that someone knew someone to get the keys to, and it ran from nine at night until the sky over the city went the colour of a bruise healing, and it was, Ella would think later, the night the deal finally died for good.

She'd built the world and now she got to watch it come true, which was a feeling she'd chase for the rest of her life. The wrong film stock. The orange streetlight she'd specified, achieved with gels and a fog machine and a borrowed sodium lamp that made everyone look like a memory of themselves. Knox in the centre of it, electric, doing take after take, and the camera loving him, and the whole sound she'd promised — *the inside of a night bus at 3am, everything beautiful and slightly ruined* — assembling itself in front of her in real time.

Cal wasn't supposed to be there. Producers weren't, usually, and a producer who was secretly a rugby player definitely shouldn't have been, but he came anyway, hood up, hanging back in the dark beyond the lights, and Ella clocked him the second he arrived because she'd developed, to her private alarm, an instinct for it. The room would rearrange and she'd know before she turned around that he'd walked into it.

"Your producer's here," murmured Sasha, materialising at her shoulder with two coffees, handing her one. Sasha did this — appeared, gave you something, watched you. "The mysterious V. He never comes to shoots."

"Maybe he likes the world I built him," Ella said lightly, taking the coffee, giving nothing.

"Maybe." Sasha's smile was warm and her eyes were doing inventory. "You two have a real rapport. It's good. The work's better for it, you can hear it." A sip. "Funny thing — I've been trying to work out who V is for two years. Whole scene wants to know. Producer that good with no face, no name, no socials — that's not shyness, that's a *strategy*. There's a story there. Stories are my whole job." She let it sit. "If you ever wanted to tell me, off the record — it'd be very good for you. The girl who unmasked the ghost. That's a launch."

And there it was, laid on the table as smoothly as Ella had laid out Cal's own moves at the party. Ella kept her face exactly where it was, the professionalism welded on, and felt something cold settle in her stomach that she'd remember later as the first sight of the cliff.

"I don't know who he is," Ella said. "Genuinely. He's a closed door. I just make the outside of it look nice."

Sasha looked at her for a beat too long. "Sure," she said, pleasant, and drifted off to do inventory on someone else, and Ella stood holding a coffee she hadn't asked for and understood that the danger in this project had a name and the name was not Cal's father.

She found Cal in the dark beyond the lights.

"Your fixer just offered to make me famous," she said, low, "if I tell her who you are."

Cal didn't react the way she expected. He didn't tense or pale. He just turned his head and looked at her, the counting stillness, and said, "Are you going to?"

"No."

"Why not? She's right. It'd be a launch. The girl who unmasked the ghost. You'd never have to find a brand again, they'd come to you." His voice was perfectly level, the dangerous level, and she realised he was *testing* it, the door he'd given her the key to, finding out whether she'd open it the second it became useful. "You told me you're only keeping the secret because it's useful. Well. It just stopped being useful and started being expensive. So."

Ella looked at him in the orange light, at the boy who'd told her about Jordan with his head on the desk, who'd built his brother a grave out of buried sound, who'd handed her the one key that could destroy him and then spent three weeks watching to see if she'd use it.

"Cal," she said. "I lied to you. That first day. When I said I was only keeping it because it was useful."

Something moved across his face.

"I kept it because you came over anyway," she said. "At the party. You knew I'd see through you and you came over anyway, and that's the realest thing anyone's done for me in about three years, and I've been pretending it didn't land because landing is dangerous, because I've got a deadline and a deal and a mother who calls at six o'clock, and I cannot afford —" her voice did something and she hated it "— I cannot afford to want a thing I can't have, I have spent my whole life not wanting things so that I could carry the things I had to, and you are extremely inconvenient, Callum Vance, you are the single most inconvenient thing that has ever happened to me, and I'm not going to tell Sasha who you are because the secret isn't useful to me anymore, it's *yours*, and yours matters to me more than mine, which is the most terrifying sentence I have ever said out loud."

The fog machine hissed. Knox laughed at something across the arch. The sodium lamp made them both into memories.

Cal said, "Come outside."

---

The street outside the arch was empty and wet and the rain had stopped and the air smelled of iron and the city. They walked without deciding to, away from the lights, past the dark hulk of the old station, and neither of them said anything for a while, and the not-saying was loud.

"I've never told anyone about Jordan," Cal said eventually. "Not Tom. Not anyone. Five years."

"I know."

"I keep trying to work out why it was you. And I think it's because you're the only person I've ever met who's also two people. The one everyone sees and the one carrying it. You knew where the door was because you've got the same door." He stopped walking. They were under a streetlight, the orange she'd built the whole world out of, and it occurred to her that she'd specified this exact light because somewhere she'd already known it was the colour of this. "I'm not good at this. Whatever this is. I read rooms, I read odds, I don't —" he exhaled. "I don't know how to do a thing I can't calculate. And I can't calculate you. I've tried. You keep coming out as the same answer."

"Which is?"

"Worth it." He said it like a man stepping off something. "You keep coming out as worth it."

They walked the long way, the way you walk when neither of you wants to arrive anywhere, down through the dead industrial dark toward the canal, past the backs of buildings that had been mills and were now apartments and bars and the kind of nothing that Manchester did better than anywhere, the beautiful ruined nothing she'd built a whole campaign out of.

"Tell me something true," Ella said. "Since we're off the record. Something you've never said to anyone in daylight."

Cal was quiet for a while, their footsteps the only sound. "I'm scared all the time," he said finally. "That's the thing. Everyone thinks I'm the calm one, the still one, the one who's never rattled — Tom calls me a robot, affectionately. And the truth is I'm scared every single minute. Scared the pass won't come. Scared someone'll find the studio. Scared my dad'll find out. Scared I'll lose the music or lose the rugby or lose —" he stopped. "I made the stillness so no one would see the scared. That's all the stillness is. It's not calm. It's a man holding his breath so the fear doesn't show in his shoulders. I've been holding my breath since I was sixteen." He glanced at her. "Your turn. Something true."

"I don't know how to be looked after," Ella said. "I know how to look after. I'm world-class at it, I could do it in my sleep, I've been doing it since I was nine. But when someone tries to do it back — when you put the heater on before I get there, when the Vietnamese is already here — there's a half-second where it feels like a *threat.* Like being handed something I'll have to pay for. Like a trap. Because in my experience the only reason anyone gives you something is so you'll owe them, and I've spent my whole life making sure I never owe anyone anything, because owing is how you end up carried, and I'm the one who carries." She watched the black canal water. "So when you look after me sideways, the way you do, where there's no debt in it — I have to physically stop myself flinching. Every time. It's the hardest thing I do all week. And you've never once made me feel like I owed you for it. That's the true thing. I don't know what to do with kindness that doesn't come with an invoice. I've never had any."

Cal stopped walking. The canal lay flat and black beside them, the city's orange light broken on its surface.

"Then I'll keep doing it," he said. "The no-invoice kind. Until the flinch stops. However long that takes. You can owe me nothing for the rest of your life and I'll keep the heater on. That's not a trap, Ella. That's just — that's the only thing I've ever wanted to do with the scared. Point it at keeping one person warm instead of at holding my own breath."

And that, more than the kiss that came after, was the moment Ella stopped being able to pretend the deal was just a deal — because no one in twenty-one years had offered her a single thing without a price, and here was a boy offering her warmth with no invoice attached, and the no-invoice was so foreign to her that it undid her more thoroughly than any grand declaration could have.

There was a chip shop three doors down, the only lit thing on the street, open absurdly late, the kind of place that existed entirely to catch people at the exact moment Ella and Cal were having, and later neither of them could explain why they'd ended up in it except that the moment had been too big and they were two people who'd learned to manage big moments by getting smaller and more practical, so they bought chips. Chips at three in the morning, in polystyrene, drowning them in vinegar, sitting on the cold step outside while the city's last buses went past empty and gold.

"This is so romantic," Ella said. "Vinegar. A bin. The bins of Mayfield."

"You built a whole world out of beautiful and slightly ruined," Cal said. "This is just product testing."

She laughed, properly, the way she only laughed when she'd forgotten to manage it, and he watched her do it with the counting stillness, and when she turned to say something else he was very close, closer than the step required, and the laugh died in her throat, not unpleasantly.

"I'm going to kiss you," Cal said, "unless you tell me not to. And I want you to know I have run the odds on this and they're terrible. The deal, the project, your deadline, your mum at six, Sasha doing inventory on us all night, my dad, all of it. The odds are terrible."

"They're catastrophic," Ella agreed, not moving away.

"Catastrophic. So I want it on the record that I'm not doing this because I think it's a good bet." His eyes did the thing, the full beam, the measuring that had stopped being detached weeks ago. "I'm doing it because it's the only thing I've wanted in five years that's just for me and not for anyone watching. You can decide what that tells you."

"It tells me," Ella said, and closed the distance herself, because looking away first was an apology and waiting was a kind of looking away, and she was done apologising for wanting things, "that you finally learned to stop reading the room."

She kissed him on a chip-shop step in Mayfield at three in the morning, vinegar and cold and the iron smell of the city, under the exact orange light she'd built a whole world out of, and the kiss was not careful, and it was not calculated, and somewhere behind them the last gold bus went by empty, and for once in both their lives neither of them was performing being fine, because they weren't fine, they were something much more dangerous than fine, and for the length of that kiss neither of them cared who was watching, because for the first time the answer was no one, and the only person who could see them was the only person either of them had ever wanted to be seen by.
