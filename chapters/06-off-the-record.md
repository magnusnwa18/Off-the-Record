# Chapter Six — Off the Record

*Cal*

He told her about Jordan on a Tuesday, which he hadn't planned, which he'd in fact spent five years planning never to do, and the way it happened was so small he didn't see it coming until it had already happened.

They were working late, too late, the session overrunning because Knox had sent eleven voice notes of new ad-libs and Cal was chopping them and Ella was three feet away cross-legged on the floor building the single's cover in real time, her laptop screen throwing blue light up onto her face, and she was humming. Under her breath. Not to anything. Just a melody, idle, the way people do when they've forgotten they're not alone.

Cal's hands stopped on the desk.

"What's that," he said.

"What's what?"

"What you're humming."

Ella looked up, surprised, not having noticed she was doing it. "I don't — nothing. It's just a thing. My brother used to —" she frowned. "It's nothing. A nursery thing."

But Cal had already gone somewhere else. Because the melody she'd been humming, idle, to no one, was four notes that climbed and one that fell, and it was the bones of the buried track. The one with the voice in it. Not the same — she couldn't have known — but the same *shape*, the same reluctant rise and the small heartbreak of the drop, and the coincidence of it, the awful intimate coincidence of this girl idly humming the architecture of the thing he'd built around his dead brother's voice, undid something in him that had been holding for five years.

"Cal?"

"The voice in the track," he heard himself say. "You asked who it was."

She closed the laptop. The blue light went out. She didn't say anything. She'd learned, he'd noticed, when not to say anything, which was a skill almost no one had.

"It's my brother," Cal said. To the desk. To the monitors. To the dark window. "Jordan. He's — he was. He was older. Six years. He's the reason I make this stuff, he's the reason all of it, he used to make beats in his room when I was a kid and I'd sit on his floor while he did it, and he was — he was so much better than me, you have to understand, I'm good but Jordan was — he heard things no one else heard." Cal's voice was very level, the dangerous level, the one that meant the opposite of what level usually meant. "And my dad — my dad's a rugby man. Played second row, broke himself doing it, decided his sons were going to do it properly. And I was good at it, the rugby, I was *good*, so I got the nod. But Jordan wasn't. Jordan was good at the wrong thing. The thing you can't put on a touchline. And my dad —" he stopped.

"You don't have to," Ella said quietly.

"I do, actually." And he found that he did, that the door he'd kept shut for five years had been waiting for someone who'd hum the right four notes by accident, someone who'd already taken him apart once and put him back gently. "My dad called it a waste. To his face. For years. *You're wasting yourself, there's no living in that, look at your brother.* Look at me. The good son. The one who picked the nameable thing. And Jordan — he didn't fall apart loud. That's the thing people don't get. He fell apart quiet. He got smaller. He stopped playing me stuff. And then one night he —" Cal's hands were flat on the desk and he was looking at them like they belonged to someone else. "He'd been driving back from a session in Leeds. Late. Tired. They said tired. The road was wet. I was sixteen."

The studio was completely silent. Even the heater seemed to hold its breath.

"And after," Cal went on, because if he stopped now he never would, "my dad — he doubled down. On me. Like if he could just make *one* of us into the safe thing, into the nameable thing, then Jordan being gone would — mean something. Be worth something. And I let him. I gave him the nod back. I became Cal Vance, the fly-half, the one they come to watch, full-time, all daylight, no exceptions." He finally looked at her. "And the music — Jordan's thing, the only thing me and him ever had that was *ours* — I buried it. Took my name off it. Made it a ghost. Because if my dad ever found out I was still doing the thing that he thinks killed his other son, it'd —" He shook his head. "I'm not protecting a career, Ella. I'm not being mysterious for the aesthetic. I bury my name because the day my dad hears it on a track is the day I lose the only parent I've got left. That's the secret. That's the whole secret."

Ella didn't move toward him. He noticed that, and was grateful for it, because if she'd hugged him in that moment he'd have come apart, and instead she did the exact right thing, the thing almost no one knew how to do, which was stay where she was and let the weight of it sit in the room without trying to lift it off him too soon.

"The buried track," she said. "With his voice in it."

"It's the last thing he ever sent me. A voice note. 'Listen to this, Cal, listen to the bit at the end.' I never — I was sixteen, I was at training, I didn't reply for two days. Two days. And then —" He shrugged, a terrible small movement. "So now I build everything around it. Everything I make has a bit of that voice note in it, buried so deep no one can hear it. I've put my brother in a quarter of a million people's headphones and not one of them knows they're listening to him. It's the only grave I could give him that my dad couldn't visit."

For a long time neither of them spoke. The city tumbled on below, indifferent, full of people each carrying their one unbearable thing.

"Cal," Ella said at last. "Thank you. For trusting me with that."

"I didn't decide to. You hummed his melody. It just —"

"I know. But you kept going. That was the decision." She uncrossed her legs and got up, slowly, and crossed the room, and she didn't hug him. She put her hand flat on the desk next to his, not touching, just there, a thing in the world that was on his side. "I'm not going to tell you to put your name on it. People are always telling me to be louder about who I am and they mean well and they don't understand that visibility isn't free. You know exactly what it costs. So I'm not going to do that to you." She paused. "But I'm going to ask you one thing, and you can say no."

"Ask."

"Let me hear the bit at the end. The bit he wanted you to listen to."

Cal looked at her. Five years he'd carried the voice note. Five years he'd played the buried version, the encrypted version, the one where Jordan was hidden so deep he was safe and also gone. No one had ever asked to hear the actual thing. No one had known to.

He pulled out his phone. His hands weren't quite steady. He found it — *Jords*, the message thread he'd never been able to delete and never been able to open — and he played the last voice note his brother ever sent him, out loud, in the one room in the world where he didn't have to be anyone, for the one person in the world he'd decided to trust with it.

A laugh, first. Jordan's laugh, bright and unguarded. *"Cal, bro, listen — listen to this bit at the end, I think I cracked it, tell me I cracked it."* And then the music — four notes that climbed and one that fell, the same shape Ella had hummed by accident an hour ago, raw and unfinished and so full of someone who thought he had all the time in the world.

It ended. The silence after it was a different silence than before.

"He cracked it," Ella said softly. "Tell him he cracked it."

And Cal Vance, who had not cried in front of another human being since he was sixteen years old, who had built an entire self out of stillness so that no one would ever see the rooms inside him, put his head down on the desk in the unmarked studio above the vintage shop, and Ella Savage-Oluwa stood beside him with her hand flat on the wood, not touching, just *there*, and let him.

She left an hour later, and neither of them had touched the other, not once, and it was the most intimate night of Cal's entire life.
