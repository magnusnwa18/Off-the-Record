# Chapter Three — The Brief

*Ella*

The flat Ella shared with Priya was a two-bed above a launderette in Hulme, and the rent was due on the first, and the launderette's dryers ran a heat up through the floor that made the front room the warmest place Ella had ever lived and the loudest, a permanent low tumble like the building had a heartbeat. She'd come to love it. She'd come to love a lot of things she'd had to learn weren't beneath her, which was a sentence she'd never say to her mother.

It was a Tuesday and she was sitting cross-legged on the floor in the heat surrounded by the carcass of her final project, which was — she was prepared to admit at three in the morning to no one — not working.

The brief, set by her course, was open to the point of cruelty: *Develop and execute a complete visual identity for a real or fictional brand, artist, or campaign.* Most of her cohort were doing fictional. Safe, controllable, no client to disappoint. Ella had decided in September, with the specific arrogance of someone who needs to prove something, that fictional was cowardice. She was going to do *real*. She was going to find a real artist or a real label and art-direct a real thing into the real world, and she was going to put it in front of the external assessors and the recruiters who came to the degree show and she was going to make it impossible to file her under *nice eye, shame about the practicality*.

She'd been pitching for two months. She had a folder of polite nos and a larger folder of no replies at all. She had a moodboard practice that she was, objectively, very good at — she could take a sound and build it a world, a palette, a typeface, a set of references that made A&Rs go quiet on calls. She'd done it for free for two local artists already, just to have the work to show, and the work was *good*, and good wasn't translating into the one thing she needed, which was a yes attached to a name big enough to matter.

Her phone buzzed. Six o'clock. She knew before she looked.

"Hi, Mum."

"Eleonora." Her mother's voice came down the line with the full weight of Lagos and Turin and Lewisham folded into it, three accents that had never quite agreed to merge. "You sound tired."

"I'm fine. I'm working."

"On the school project." A pause that Ella could have written the subtitles for. "You know the deadline for the Hargreave scheme is the fifteenth. I sent you the link. Your cousin Tobi did it and he's at the firm now, you remember Tobi, he was always —"

"I remember Tobi, Mum."

"It's a graduate scheme, Eleonora, not a prison sentence. Two years. You do the two years, you have a name behind you, *then* if you want to do —" the word came out of her mother as though held with tongs "— *styling*, fine, you do styling, but from a position of *something*. Not from nothing. Your father and I did not come here so you could do *nothing* and call it freedom."

There it was. The thing under the thing. *We crossed oceans for your safety and you are spending it on prettiness.* Ella had heard it her whole life, in a hundred forms, and the worst part — the part that kept her up — was that she understood it. She wasn't a fool. She knew exactly what her parents had given up and exactly what they were afraid of, which was the specific terror of people who'd clawed up a slope to watch their daughter stand at the top of it and look back down at the climb like it might be interesting to descend.

"I'm not doing nothing," Ella said, quietly. "I'm building something. You can't see it yet. That doesn't mean it isn't there."

"That's what your uncle Femi said about the restaurant."

"I'm not Uncle Femi."

"No," her mother agreed, and softened, which was harder than the hardness. "No, you're better than all of us, that's the whole problem, *omo mi*. We just want you to be safe. Is that a crime, to want you safe?"

"No," Ella said. "It's not a crime." She closed her eyes. The dryers tumbled beneath her. "I'll look at the link."

"That's all I ask. Eat something. You're too thin, you stylists, you all look hungry."

"Bye, Mum. Love you."

She sat with the phone in her lap for a while after, in the warm and the tumbling, and let herself feel the full shape of it — the exhaustion of being the eldest, the one who had to be the proof, the one who couldn't fall apart because there was no one underneath her to catch the pieces, only siblings looking up. She'd been the strong one since she was nine and her father lost the first job and her mother sat at the kitchen table at midnight not crying so that the children wouldn't see, and Ella had seen anyway, and had decided, the way children decide enormous things in single moments, that she would never make anyone carry her. She would carry. That was her job. She was good at her job.

She was so tired of her job.

Priya came home at half seven, dropping bags, radiant, talking before the door was shut. "Okay don't be weird but I have a thing for you and you have to not be weird about it."

"I'm never weird."

"You're the weirdest person I know, I love you. Sit." Priya sat opposite her on the floor, which she only did for major announcements. "So Tom knows everyone, right, because rugby boys somehow know the whole city. And one of Tom's mates is mates with Knox. *Knox*, Ella. Marcus. The 'Lowlight' one."

Ella's whole body went still. "*That* Knox."

"That Knox. He's making a proper project, like a real album, and the label's behind it now, and they want — Priya did jazz hands — *creative direction*. A visual world. Someone with vision who isn't some forty-year-old man who thinks Black British music is a costume. And Tom mentioned you, and Knox's people looked at the stuff you put up, the Lowlight reimagining you did for fun, and —" Priya was vibrating again, the way she had at the party "— they want to meet you. Thursday. At the studio. As a *real candidate* for the *real job*."

The dryers tumbled. Ella didn't say anything for so long that Priya's face started to fall.

"Say something. Is it not — I thought —"

"Priya." Ella's voice came out strange, too careful, the voice she used to hold something heavy without dropping it. "Do you understand what this is."

"It's a job?"

"It's *the* project. It's a real artist, with a real label, a real release — if I art-direct Knox's album I don't have to find a brand for my final project, the project *is* the final project, it's the thing I put in the degree show, it's the thing I put in front of the assessors, it's —" she pressed her hands to her face "— it's the thing I show my mum. With a name on it she's heard of. It's everything I've been trying to build for two months landing in one afternoon."

"So that's good?" Priya ventured. "That's the good face? It's hard to tell with you."

"It's the good face." Ella lowered her hands and she was, she realised, grinning, properly, the first uncomplicated grin in weeks. "Priya. I could *kiss* Tom. I could kiss his enormous stupid head."

"Please don't, he's mine." Priya launched across the carcass of the project and hugged her, hard, and they sat there in the warm tumbling dark, laughing, and for once Ella let herself be held without doing the calculation of what it cost.

"Thursday," Ella said into Priya's shoulder. "Where's the studio?"

"Northern Quarter. Some unmarked place above a vintage shop, very mysterious, very 'we're too cool for a sign.' Tom doesn't even know exactly, the producer's apparently this total ghost, nobody knows who he is, he just makes everything sound incredible and then disappears."

Something moved at the back of Ella's mind, a small dark fish under ice, and was gone before she could name it.

"A ghost producer," she said. "Of course it is. The whole scene's built on men who want credit for being mysterious."

"You'll eat him alive," Priya said proudly.

"I won't have to. I'm there for the art direction. I'll never even meet him probably." Ella was already on her feet, already moving, already opening the laptop, the tiredness burned clean off her by the only fuel that had ever really worked on her, which was a chance. "I need to build a deck by Thursday. A real one. Not free-work, a *pitch*. I need to listen to everything he's ever made and find the world inside it and walk in there Thursday with the world already built so they can't imagine the project without me in it."

"It's eight o'clock at night."

"Then I've got till Thursday minus eight o'clock at night." She looked up, and Priya saw the face, the real one, the one Ella only ever showed the work. "I'm not going to be safe, Pri. I'm going to be *good*. And I'm going to be so good they can't say no."

She did not sleep much that week. She built a world. She did not, in all the hours of building it, spare a single thought for the boy from the loudest room, because she had no reason to, because the studio above the vintage shop was just a room, and the ghost behind the boards was just a man with a complex, and Thursday was just the day her real life started.

She was wrong about exactly one of those things.
