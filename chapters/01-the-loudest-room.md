# Chapter One — The Loudest Room

*Ella*

The house on Egerton Road had been sweating since ten o'clock, and by midnight the walls had given up pretending to be walls. Bass came up through the floorboards and into the soles of Ella's boots, into her teeth, into the hollow under her ribs where she kept the things she didn't say out loud. Somebody had hung fairy lights from the picture rail and somebody else had pulled half of them down, so the front room flickered like a signal going out.

"You're doing the face," Priya shouted into her ear.

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing the face that says you're already writing the text you're going to send me tomorrow about how you should have stayed in." Priya pressed a warm can into Ella's hand and beamed at her, lipstick immaculate, eyeliner immaculate, the whole of her assembled with a confidence Ella envied and had styled half of. "One hour. Give me one hour of being a person and then you can go home and be a genius."

"I'm always a person."

"You're always a *project manager*. There's a difference."

That landed harder than Priya meant it to, the way the truest things from your best friend always did, casual as a dropped glass. Ella looked away so it wouldn't show on her face. The room was full of people she half-knew — second-years from the union, a boy from her seminar who'd once spent forty minutes explaining her own course to her, a knot of girls she recognised from the fashion society reposting each other's stories in real time. She catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, by silhouette and palette and the small tells of who they were trying to be. It was the only way she knew how to be in a room. Watch it. Style it in her head. Stay a step outside.

"One hour," she said. "Then I'm gone and you don't get to guilt me."

"Deal." Priya was already turning, already gone, swallowed by the crowd toward the kitchen where the rugby lot had set up like an occupying army.

Because that was the thing about this particular party. It was a *crossover*. Priya had started seeing one of them — Tom, a lock the approximate dimensions of a wardrobe, who laughed like a faulty boiler and who, to be fair, looked at Priya like she'd personally invented the sun. And where Tom went, the rest of the academy boys went, filling doorways, drinking with the focused efficiency of professional athletes on their one night off the leash, taking up more room than physics should have allowed.

Ella had opinions about them. She'd had the opinions for three years without needing to meet a single one. Boys who'd been told they were special since they were eleven. Boys who walked through the world like it was a pitch and everyone else was the crowd. She'd grown up around enough certainty to recognise the manufactured kind.

She found a wall and made it hers, can sweating in her hand, and let the party happen at her like weather.

That was when she clocked him.

It wasn't that he was loud. That was the first thing that snagged, actually — that he wasn't. The kitchen was loud, the boys around him were loud, a great churning engine of noise and shoulder-slaps, and at the centre of it he was *still*. Leaning against the counter with a bottle he wasn't drinking from, head tipped, listening to something one of his mates was bellowing, and his eyes were going round the room the whole time. Slow. Methodical. The way Ella's own eyes went round a room. Reading it.

He had the kind of face that the camera was going to love and probably already did — sharp at the jaw, a nose that had been broken at least once and set just imperfectly enough to be interesting, dark hair pushed back off his forehead and curling at the nape where he'd let it grow past regulation. Tall. Built like he'd been assembled for a specific purpose. He wore a plain black tee and dark jeans and absolutely nothing else that asked for attention, which was its own kind of asking, and Ella's stylist brain noted it before her better judgement could intervene: *he knows exactly how he looks and he's chosen to look like he hasn't thought about it.*

His gaze finished its circuit of the room and arrived at her.

She didn't look away. She'd learned a long time ago that looking away first was a kind of apology, and she was done apologising for taking up space. So she held it, eyebrow lifting a fraction, *and?*, and watched something flicker across his face — surprise, maybe, that she hadn't done the thing girls apparently did, and then, infuriatingly, the slow arrival of a smile. Not a wide one. A private one. Like she'd confirmed something he'd been wondering.

He pushed off the counter.

"Oh, no," Ella murmured to her can.

"Don't move," Priya hissed, appearing at her elbow from nowhere, vibrating. "Don't you *dare* move, that's Cal."

"That's a sentence that assumes I know who Cal is."

"Callum Vance. Fly-half. *The* fly-half. Tom says scouts come to watch him. He's going to be —" Priya did a complicated gesture meant to convey enormity. "He never talks to anyone at these things. He just stands there being —"

"Aloof?"

"I was going to say *fit*, but —"

He arrived. Up close he was worse, which Ella registered with a flat sort of dismay, because up close she could see that the stillness wasn't an act, or wasn't only an act; there was a real attention behind his eyes, a focus that he turned on her now with an intensity that felt less like flirting and more like being *measured*.

"You're not having a good time," he said.

No hello. No name. Just that, delivered low enough that she had to lean in half an inch to catch it over the bass, which she suspected was the point.

"I'm having a fine time."

"You're having a *fine* time against a wall, drinking something you don't like, leaving in —" he glanced at nothing, at the air "— forty minutes, because that's the deal you made with her." He tilted his head at Priya, who had developed an expression of such delighted, transparent matchmaking that Ella wanted to fold her into a small box. "Am I close?"

It was a good trick. She'd give him that. The kind of cold reading that worked because it was ninety percent flattery dressed as insight: *I see you, you're complicated, no one else here gets it.* She'd had it tried on her before, usually by men who wanted credit for noticing she was a person.

"That's a lot of certainty," Ella said, "for someone who's known me eleven seconds."

"I'm good at reading rooms."

"You're good at reading the *odds*. There's a difference." She let that sit, watched it land, watched the smile change. "You picked the one girl who isn't already looking at you. Low risk. If I tell you to get lost, you've lost nothing — you didn't want me, you wanted the read to be right. And if I don't, you get to be the man who saw something everyone else missed. It's a clean bet either way."

For a second nothing happened. The party roared on around the small pocket of stillness the two of them had made. Priya had gone very quiet in the way of someone watching a car approach a cliff in slow motion.

Then Cal laughed — a real one, surprised out of him, short and low and warmer than she'd expected, and it changed his whole face for half a second into someone younger and less guarded before the shutter came back down.

"All right," he said. "Fair."

"That's it? Fair?"

"You're right. So — fair." He set his untouched bottle down on the windowsill behind her, deliberate, and for one absurd moment they were close enough that she could smell him under the spilled-beer fug of the room — something clean and warm, cedar maybe, expensive. "But you got one thing wrong."

"Did I."

"It wasn't low risk." His eyes did the thing again, the measuring, except this time there was nothing detached in it at all, and Ella felt it go through her like the bass through the floor, unwelcome, undeniable. "I knew you'd see right through it. I came over anyway." A beat. "You can decide what that tells you."

And then — and this was the part that kept her up later, replaying it with a kind of furious admiration — he didn't wait. He didn't lean in for the kill or ask for her number or do any of the things the read had been building toward. He just nodded, once, like she'd taught him something, and turned and went back to the kitchen, back into the noise, back to the boys who immediately swallowed him into their orbit, and he didn't look at her again.

Not once. She watched the back of his head for a full minute to confirm it.

"Okay," Priya breathed. "*Okay.* What was that."

"That was a boy who's used to the last move working." Ella's heart was doing something irritating and she resented it specifically and personally. "And who just changed strategy when it didn't."

"That sounds like you liked it."

"It sounds like I noticed it. They're different things."

"You're doing the face again."

She was. She turned it toward the window so the streetlight could have it instead, and out on Egerton Road the rain had started, fine and orange under the lamps, and she thought: *Manchester*. She'd come here at eighteen because it was far enough from South London to be hers and near enough that her mother could still phone every evening at six to ask whether she'd applied for the graduate scheme yet. Three years she'd spent assembling a self out of this grey, glittering, rain-soaked city — the markets and the warehouse nights and the studios where people made things with their hands, the whole alternate universe to the one her family had mapped out for her in straight lines. *Law. Or finance, if you must be difficult. Something you can name at a dinner.*

She had four months left. One final project. One shot at proving — to a course, to a future employer, to her mother at six o'clock — that the thing she was good at was a thing you could build a life on. She did not have time for a rugby player who read rooms like he was solving them.

"I'm going home," she told Priya. "You stay. Be a person. I love you."

"You're so *boring*," Priya said, with enormous affection, and hugged her hard, and let her go.

The night air outside was a relief, cold and wet and honest after the furnace of the house. Ella pulled her coat tight and started the walk down toward the curry mile, neon bleeding into the puddles, and she did not think about Callum Vance. She thought about it so deliberately that it amounted to thinking about him constantly, which she noted, and filed, and refused.

*I came over anyway. You can decide what that tells you.*

"It tells me you're trouble," she said out loud, to the rain, to nobody.

The rain didn't argue. Behind her, three streets back, the loudest room in Fallowfield kept on roaring, and somewhere inside it a boy who never talked to anyone was standing very still, not drinking, watching the door she'd left through for longer than he'd admit to anyone, including himself.
