# Chapter Sixteen — Silence

*Cal*

He went home anyway. Not to the house — *don't come to the house* still rang in him like a struck bell — but to the south, to London, on a train he didn't remember booking, because the ghost had run out of places to be in Manchester and there was one room left in his life he hadn't been thrown out of, and it was Jordan's.

His mother had kept it. That was the thing nobody talked about in the Vance house, the thing under the thing: that Cal's mother, who'd left when Cal was fourteen, unable to live inside Gareth's coldness, had nonetheless been the one to keep Jordan's room, in her small flat in Catford, exactly as it had been — the decks, the foam tiles half-falling off the walls, the milk crate of records, the spider plant somehow still alive. She'd kept it the way Cal kept the voice note. Two graves for the same brother, neither of them telling the other they had one.

"You look like him," his mother said, opening the door, and then she saw his face and didn't say anything else, just stepped back and let him in, the way Ella had let him in, the way the people who actually loved him always knew not to crowd the wound.

He sat on the floor of Jordan's room, where he'd sat as a kid while his brother made beats, and his mother sat in the doorway, and the spider plant did its slow green thing in the window, and Cal told her. All of it. The studio, the ghost name, the quarter of a million streams, the buried voice note in every track, *the only grave I could give him your father couldn't visit.* The match, the second half, the truth in his mouth at the rail and swallowed. Ella. The girl who saw all of him at once. The leak. *Don't come to the house.* And the text he'd sent Ella, the cruelest order he could chop the truth into, the ghost thing, leaving first so he couldn't be left.

His mother listened the whole way through, the way you listen to someone confess to a crime they committed against themselves. When he finished, the room was quiet, Jordan's room, full of his brother's gear and his brother's absence.

"You've made yourself into a ghost," she said finally. Not a question. "You think I don't recognise it? Cal. I'm the original ghost of this family. I left because your father turned grief into a *project* — he couldn't feel it so he managed it, he took the one son who was good at the safe thing and he poured all of it into you so he'd never have to sit on this floor and feel what we lost. And I couldn't watch him do it, so I left, and leaving was its own ghost thing, the same thing you just did to that girl — *leave first so you can't be left.* It runs in us." She looked at him. "But here's what twenty years of being the family ghost taught me, baby. It doesn't work. You can't get out ahead of loss by leaving first. All you do is lose people while they're still alive. I left your father and he was *still there to lose*, I just lost him on purpose so it would hurt on my schedule. That's not safety. That's just grief you administer to yourself in advance, every day, forever. It's the worst deal there is and I signed it for twenty years."

Cal put his head back against Jordan's wall.

"The girl," his mother said. "Did she leak you?"

"The timing — everything she wanted was downstream of —"

"I didn't ask about the timing. I asked what you *know.* You've told me about her for an hour without meaning to — the food that appears, the second mug, the hand flat on the desk not touching, the way she let you cry without lifting it off you too soon, the way she told you 'it wasn't me' before you'd accused her of anything. You read rooms for a living, Cal, it's the family curse, your brother heard things and you read things and I see things. So *read it.* Not the odds. The girl. Did she leak you?"

And Cal, sitting on his dead brother's floor in his exiled mother's flat, finally stopped reading the odds and read the girl, and the answer was so obvious and had been so obvious the whole time that the obviousness of it was its own indictment.

"No," he said. "No. She'd never. She lied to Imogen Clarke-Bayo's face about who I was. She texted me *it wasn't me* before — because she knew, she knew the second it broke that I'd do exactly this, that I'd do the ghost thing and point it at her, she knew me well enough to predict the cruelty before I'd even —" His voice broke. "She said she had a plan. *I think I know how to fix it, please call me.* She was going to fix it. And I texted her *congratulations, you win clean.* I took the thing she's most scared of — that she's secretly just ambitious, that the loyalty's a performance — and I put the knife exactly there. On purpose. Because hurting her was the fastest way to make her leave so I wouldn't have to wait to be left." He looked at his mother, horrified. "I did the thing. The family thing. I did it to the one person who ever —"

"Yes," his mother said gently. "You did. We do." She came and sat beside him on Jordan's floor, the first time she'd touched him in he couldn't remember how long. "The question isn't whether you did it. You did. The question is whether you're going to be the kind of ghost who knows he's a ghost and does nothing, like me for twenty years, or the kind who finds out he's a ghost and decides to come back to life, which is harder and more humiliating and the only thing worth doing. You crossed a room once, you told me. At a party. You knew she'd see through you and you crossed it anyway. That's the least ghost thing a person can do, baby. You've got it in you. You just have to do it again, except this time everyone's watching and you've already wronged her and there's no guarantee, and that's the actual brave version, the party one was easy."

Cal looked at the milk crate of records, his brother's records, the spider plant, the foam tiles. "What about Dad."

His mother was quiet for a moment. "Your father said don't come to the house. He didn't say don't ever speak to me. He said it from inside the worst sound I've heard him make since the night Jordan — and Cal, listen to me, because I was married to him for eighteen years and I know the difference: that sound wasn't betrayal. You heard it as betrayal because you've spent five years braced for betrayal. But I heard it. That was *fear.* He's not afraid the music will waste you. He's afraid the music will *take* you, the way it took Jordan, because in his head music is the road his son died on. *Don't come to the house* isn't 'I'm ashamed of you.' It's 'I can't look at the thing that killed your brother living in the face of the son I've got left, not yet, give me a minute, I'm drowning.' He's a coward about feeling things, your father, the worst coward I ever met, but he's not a man who stops loving. He just can't say it. He pours it into projects instead. He poured eighteen years of not-being-able-to-feel into making you safe." She squeezed his hand. "If you want him back, you can't wait for him to cross the room. He doesn't know how. You'll have to do what your brother never got the chance to do, which is show him the music isn't the road that kills. Show him it's the thing that makes the pass different. You already know it does. You told me. The second half. You played like Jordan and your father drove up from London to watch you do it and didn't know that's what he was watching."

"Play me something of yours," his mother said, when the worst of it had passed and the room had gone soft and late. "Not Jordan's. Yours. I've never heard a single thing my son made and apparently a quarter of a million strangers have. I'd like to be ahead of strangers, for once, on the subject of my own child."

So Cal got out his phone and played her the thing he'd made the morning after the party — the still thing in the middle of the loudest room, the date-and-a-kick-drum, the one Ella had read off him in a single listen — and his mother sat on Jordan's floor and closed her eyes and listened the whole way through, and at the end she didn't say *that's nice* or *clever* or any of the things that would have told him she was performing interest. She said: "There's someone in it. Not Jordan. Someone new." She opened her eyes. "Your brother's in the bones of it, I can hear his — the way he hung the drums back, you do that, you got that off him. But there's someone *new* in this one. In the top of it. The bright part. Who's the bright part, Cal?"

And Cal, who had told this woman everything in the last two hours, found there was still one thing left, the brightest and most buried thing, and he said it: "Her. It's her. I made it the morning after I met her and I didn't even — I called it a date so I wouldn't have to call it her name."

His mother smiled, the original ghost, the one who'd administered her own grief in advance for twenty years and knew exactly what it cost. "Then you've already done it once," she said. "Buried someone alive in a song so you wouldn't have to feel them in the daylight. Jordan because he was gone and you couldn't bear it, and now this girl because she's *here* and you can't bear that either — that there's someone in the daylight worth losing again." She reached over and turned his phone face down, the way he always did, the way Ella had learned to do. "Stop burying the living, baby. You did right by Jordan, in the end — you kept him, the only way you could. But she's not dead. You don't have to encode her into a beat so it's safe to love her. You can just — go and love her. In the daylight. Out loud. With her actual name on it. *Produced by Cal Vance. Loved by Cal Vance.* Both names. Spelled right."

It was the second time in a week someone had told him to put both names on the truth, spelled right, and he thought of Ella's father wanting the Savage and the Oluwa both, and he understood that this was the whole lesson of every person who'd ever actually loved him: that hiding your name off the thing you love doesn't protect it, it just makes you a ghost at your own life, and that the brave thing — the only thing — is to sign your work, all of it, the music and the grief and the girl, *by Cal Vance,* and let the daylight have it.

Cal sat with that for a long time, on the floor of the room that was one of his brother's two graves, and slowly, the way the frost burned off the pitch at six a.m., the ghost thing started to lift off him — not gone, never gone, it ran in the family — but lifting enough that he could see, underneath it, the shape of what he'd thrown away in a single Wednesday: the girl who built rooms, the father who couldn't feel, the brother who'd cracked it and never got told, and the one version of his own life where he stopped administering grief to himself in advance and actually, finally, came back from the dead.

"I have to call her," Cal said. "Ella. I have to —"

"No," his mother said. "You have to do better than call her. You knifed her in the softest place she's got. A call doesn't fix that, a call is just more words from the boy who already used words to hurt her. You built a wall and gave her the only key and then accused her of using it against you. You don't get to fix that with a phone call, baby. You broke it in public — well, in the place that's public to the two of you — and you have to mend it somewhere she can actually see it cost you something." His mother smiled, sad and knowing, the original ghost passing something on. "She builds rooms. You said. She builds worlds so people can be seen safely. So build her one back. Build her the thing she's been building everyone else her whole life and never once getting for herself. Stop being the ghost in the corner. Cross the room where everyone can watch you do it. That's the only apology that's the same size as the wound."

And in the silence of Jordan's room, with the spider plant doing its slow green thing in the Catford window, Cal Vance — who had organised his entire life around never being seen, never being left, never crossing a room he might lose — began, for the first time, to plan how to be loud.
