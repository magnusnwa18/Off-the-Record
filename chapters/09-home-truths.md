# Chapter Nine — Home Truths

*Ella*

She went home to South London for her mother's fiftieth, because there were things in life you did not get to skip, and a Savage-Oluwa milestone was the most non-negotiable of them, and she took the train down from Piccadilly with a knot in her stomach that had nothing to do with the party and everything to do with the fact that she'd spent the journey deciding, finally, to tell her family the truth about what she was building. The real version. Not the graduate-scheme-adjacent version she'd been feeding them for months.

She'd practised it on Cal the night before, in the studio, pacing.

"I'm going to tell them I'm not applying for the Hargreave scheme. That I've got a real project, a real client, a real release coming, and that I'm going to walk into the degree show with the most ambitious final project on the course and I'm going to use it to start a career, not a hobby. And that I love them and I see what they gave up and I'm not spitting on it, I'm — I'm spending it. The way it was meant to be spent. On a life that's mine."

"That's good," Cal had said. "That's the truth."

"It's going to go terribly."

"Probably." He'd pulled her in. "But you'll have said it out loud. That's the thing about saying the true thing out loud. It stops being a secret you're carrying and starts being a fact in the world. It's lighter, even when it goes badly. I'd know."

She'd held onto that on the train. *It's lighter, even when it goes badly.*

---

The house in Lewisham was full. It was always full — aunties and uncles who were not technically aunties and uncles, cousins, the pastor, neighbours, the whole architecture of a community her parents had built out of nothing over twenty-five years, jollof and small chops and a cake her sister Tioluwa had been guarding from the children all day, her mother resplendent in deep green and gold, queenly, fifty and unbowed, working the room like — Ella realised with a small shock — like Ella herself worked an industry room. *Oh,* she thought. *That's where I got it.*

It went beautifully for three hours. She let it. She held her mother and meant it, she danced with her father who still danced like it was 1991, she let Tioluwa — seventeen, sharp, watching everything the way Ella had watched everything at seventeen — interrogate her about Manchester. And then, when the crowd thinned and it was just family in the kitchen, the washing-up and the leftovers and the particular late-party tiredness that makes people honest, her mother said, "So. The Hargreave scheme. The deadline passed. Tobi's mother asked me at church and I had to —"

"I didn't apply, Mum."

The kitchen didn't go silent — there were too many people, the tap still running, Tioluwa's music still playing from her phone — but a particular silence opened up in the middle of it, the family silence, the one Ella had grown up navigating like weather.

"You forgot the deadline," her mother said. Carefully. Giving Ella the exit.

"I didn't forget it." Ella turned off the tap. Her hands were wet. She looked at her mother in her green and gold, fifty and magnificent and afraid, always afraid, the fear that had crossed two oceans. "I didn't apply because I'm not going to do it. I've got a real project, Mum. A real artist — you've heard of him, the kids have, ask Tio. A label's behind it. A proper release. I'm art-directing the whole thing, the whole visual world, and I'm going to put it in my degree show and I'm going to start a career out of it. A real career. Not a hobby. The thing I'm actually good at."

"Eleonora —"

"Let me finish. Please." She kept her voice level, the strong-one voice, except she let something through it that she usually didn't, which was the cost. "I know what you and Dad gave up to get us here. I know it better than Tio does, better than the boys do, because I was old enough to see it. I saw you at this table at midnight not crying so we wouldn't know. I was nine. I decided that night I'd never make anyone carry me, that I'd be the one who carried, and I've done it, Mum, I've carried, I've been the strong one my whole life and I'm *tired* —" her voice cracked and she let it, for once, in the warm crowded kitchen "— I'm so tired, and the only place I'm not carrying anything is when I'm building these worlds, it's the only place I get to just *be good at something* without holding the whole family up while I do it. And I'm not spitting on the climb. I'm spending it. You climbed so I could stand somewhere high and *choose*. So I'm choosing. And I need you to — I'm not asking permission. I'm twenty-one. But I'm asking you to be proud of me for the thing I actually am, instead of grieving the daughter who'd have done law."

The tap dripped. Tioluwa had turned her music off without anyone noticing. Her father, by the back door, had gone very still in a way that Ella recognised from the mirror.

Her mother set down the dish she'd been holding. For a long moment she didn't say anything, and Ella prepared herself for the disappointment, the scripture, the comparison to Uncle Femi and the restaurant, the whole familiar architecture of *we want you safe.*

"Show me," her mother said instead.

"What?"

"The work. This — world you've built. You think I haven't heard of —" her mother's mouth twitched, almost "— I am fifty, Eleonora, I am not dead. Tioluwa plays his songs in this house every day. *Knox.*" She said it with the tongs, but the tongs had loosened. "Show me what my daughter made for Knox. If you are going to throw away the Hargreave scheme, the least you can do is let me see what you threw it away *for.* I am not agreeing to anything. I am saying — *show me.*"

Ella's hands were shaking. She got out her phone. She showed her mother the world — the orange light, the wrong film stock, the type that looked left out in the rain, the single cover she'd built on the studio floor with Cal three feet away. She talked her mother through it the way she'd talked the label through it, *this is a secret that got out, this is homesick for now,* and her father came over to look too, and Tioluwa, and the not-quite-aunties drifted back in, and somewhere in the explaining the kitchen stopped being a tribunal and became a room full of people looking at a thing their girl had made.

"This is on the radio?" her father asked. "This song? The one with this — picture?"

"It will be, Dad. Next month. The single. With my name in the credits. *Creative direction, Eleonora Savage-Oluwa.* Your name. On a thing the whole country's going to see."

Her father looked at the phone for a long time. He was not a man who said things easily; he'd spent twenty-five years saying things with his hands instead, with the jobs he worked and the table he provided and the dancing. "Our name," he said finally, quietly. "On a thing the whole country sees." He nodded — and it was, Ella thought with a jolt, the same small nod Cal had described his father giving, the earned one, the one that ran a whole life, except this one she had earned by refusing to do the safe thing instead of by doing it. "Good. That's — good, omo mi. Make sure they spell it right. The whole name. The Savage and the Oluwa. Both."

It was not approval, exactly. Her mother said, going back to the dishes, that she still thought a graduate scheme behind her would not have hurt, and that creative people died poor, and that Ella should at least keep the option open, and Ella let her have it because she'd won the only thing that mattered, which was *show me* instead of *no.*

It was Tioluwa who came to find her, later, when the kitchen had emptied and Ella was sitting on the back step in the cold with a plate she wasn't eating. Seventeen, sharp, watching everything the way Ella had watched everything at seventeen — except Tio had grown up watching *Ella*, which Ella had never once considered, that she'd been someone's load-bearing wall the whole time she'd thought she was just surviving.

"You scared me in there," Tio said, sitting down beside her, pulling her sleeves over her hands against the cold. "I've never seen you — you don't crack. Ever. You're the one who doesn't crack. And you cracked on purpose, at the sink, in front of everyone. To get Mum to say *show me.*" She looked at her sister with something between awe and accusation. "Was that real or was that a move?"

"Both," Ella said, surprised into honesty. "That's the thing nobody tells you about being the strong one, Tio. After a while you can't always feel the difference between what you mean and what you're performing, because you've been performing fine for so long that fine became a skill instead of a feeling. Tonight it was real *and* it was a move. I cried because I meant it and I let everyone see because I needed Mum to —" she shrugged. "Both. That's not lying. That's just being someone who learned to use her own true things as tools because no one was ever going to use them *for* her."

Tioluwa was quiet for a while. The garden was dark, the party-noise muffled behind the kitchen door, the South London sky orange the way every sky Ella loved was orange.

"I'm not going to be the strong one," Tio said eventually, fierce, decided. "You did it so I don't have to. You know that, right? You went first, you took the law-versus-art fight and you *won* it tonight, sort of, and now when I do something mad in three years Mum's going to be — she's going to have a script for it. Because of you. You're like the —" she searched for it. "You're the one who walks into the minefield first so everyone behind you knows where the mines are."

Ella looked at her little sister and felt something shift in her chest, an old weight redistributing. Because she'd spent her whole life thinking the carrying was a tax, a thing done to her, the eldest's unfair sentence — and here was Tioluwa telling her it had *bought* something, that the strength she'd resented had cleared a path, that the wall she'd been for the family had been load-bearing in the good sense too, holding up a roof under which her siblings got to grow into people who didn't have to crack on purpose at sinks.

"Don't walk into minefields," Ella said, and put her arm around her sister, and Tio let her. "But — yeah. I know where some of them are now. Ask me first. That's allowed. You're allowed to ask me first."

"Is he nice?" Tio said, into her shoulder.

"Is who nice?"

"Whoever you keep checking your phone for. You've checked it eleven times since dinner. You think I don't watch you? I learned watching off you." Tio grinned up at her, wicked, seventeen. "Is he nice?"

"He's complicated," Ella said, which was the truest answer, and Tio said "that means yes and Mum's going to hate it" with enormous satisfaction, and the two of them sat on the cold step under the orange London sky and laughed, two sisters, one who'd gone into the minefield and one who got to walk the cleared path, and it was, Ella thought, the lightest she'd felt at this house in years.

On the train back to Manchester the next day she texted Cal: *I told them. It went — not terribly. My dad wants them to spell the whole name right.*

He texted back immediately, which he never did, which meant he'd been waiting: *Both names. Savage and Oluwa. I'll make sure. Proud of you.*

And then, a minute later: *It's lighter. Even when it doesn't go badly. Especially then.*

Ella sat on the train as it carried her north through the dark, back to the grey glittering city she'd built a self in, back to the unmarked studio and the boy in it, and she felt — for the first time in as long as she could remember — like she wasn't holding anything up. Like she'd set the weight down and it hadn't crushed anyone. Like the climb her parents made had finally bought the thing it was for.

She didn't know, yet, that Sasha had spent the weekend doing inventory. She didn't know the bill was already being drawn up. She had one good train journey, one weekend of lightness, and she let herself have it, because she'd earned it, and because some part of her — the part that read rooms, that read odds, that had learned the same hard lessons as the boy waiting for her up north — knew that lightness this complete never lasted long.
