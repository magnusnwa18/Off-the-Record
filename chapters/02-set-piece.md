# Chapter Two — Set Piece

*Cal*

Six a.m. and the pitch was the colour of a bruise, frost burning off it in the floodlights, and Cal Vance was running the same line for the fourth time while his father stood at the touchline with his arms folded and said nothing, which was worse than anything he could have said.

"Again," called Marsh, the academy backs coach, but Cal was already turning, already resetting, because the thing about being the one they came to watch was that the watching never stopped. He took the ball flat off the imaginary nine, drifted, fixed the imaginary defender with his eyes, and put the pass into the space where the winger would be — soft, weighted, hung in the cold air for exactly as long as it needed to hang. The kind of pass that looked like nothing and was the entire game. He'd thrown ten thousand of them. He could throw it asleep. He sometimes did, in dreams, the ball leaving his hands and never landing.

"Better," Marsh said. "That's the one. Why's that one different?"

"Didn't think about it," Cal said.

"Then stop thinking about the other ones."

Easy to say. Cal jogged back, breath smoking, and let his eyes go to the touchline for half a second — just half — and his father gave him the nod. The small one. The one that had run Cal's entire life since he was six years old and first understood that the nod was a thing you earned and the absence of it was a thing you survived. Gareth Vance had played eleven seasons in the second row for clubs that didn't pay enough and broke him in places that still ached when it rained, and he had decided, somewhere in the wreckage of his own career, that his younger son was going to have the one he didn't. He'd decided it the way other men decided to build a house. Brick by brick. No days off.

*Younger son.* Cal shut that down before it could open. Not at six a.m. Not on the pitch. There were rooms in him he kept locked during daylight, and that was one of them, and he'd gotten very, very good at the locking.

They ran for another hour. By the end of it the sun was a grey suggestion behind the stand and Cal's hamstrings were singing and three of the younger lads were watching him the way he used to watch the seniors, with that hunger, that *how do I get to be that*, and he gave them the version of himself they needed to see — easy, unbothered, a word for each of them, a hand on a shoulder. *You're good, you're getting there, watch your support line.* It cost him nothing and it bought him everything, which was the whole economy of being Cal Vance in the daylight. Give them the swagger. Let them think it's free.

"Pub Thursday," Tom said, falling into step beside him as they came off, steam rising off both of them. Tom Bryce was the only one of them Cal would have called a friend without an asterisk — too big to be threatened by anyone, too kind to bother with the politics, the rare lad who'd worked out that you could just *like* people. "Priya's lot are coming. The fashion girls."

"I'm working Thursday."

"You're always working Thursday. You don't even have a job." Tom said it without heat, just baffled, the way he'd been baffled for two years by the fact that Cal disappeared after dark and came back with shadows under his eyes and no explanation. "Where do you even *go*, man?"

"To bed early. Like a professional." Cal toweled his face. "Some of us are trying to make it."

"Aye, and some of us are trying to have a life." Tom grinned. "The one from the party was asking about you. Well. Not asking. Priya said her mate said your name with a *tone*."

Cal kept his face exactly where it was. This was a skill. He'd built it the same way he'd built the pass — ten thousand repetitions until it looked like nothing. "Which one."

"Don't do that. The one you talked to. Tall, the stylist, did Priya's whole look for the formal. Ella." Tom watched him with the dim, delighted cunning of a large man who has spotted something. "You *talked* to someone, Cal. At a party. Voluntarily. I nearly called your mum."

"I read the room wrong," Cal said, which was true, and which was the most honest thing he'd say all day.

He'd gone over to Ella Savage-Oluwa for the reasons she'd laid out to his face, more or less — because she was the one not performing, and he was tired, bone-tired, of rooms where everyone was performing, himself most of all. He'd expected to do the bit. The read, the flattery, the soft landing. He was good at the bit. And she'd taken it apart on the table in front of him, calm as a surgeon, named every move before he made it, and instead of the irritation he should have felt he'd felt — *relief*. Like setting something down. Like being caught. For one second in that disgusting kitchen-adjacent corner he'd wanted to say something true to a stranger and the wanting had scared him enough to make him leave.

He hadn't stopped thinking about it. He was thinking about it now, in the showers, in the car, which was a problem, because Cal Vance did not have spare attention to spend. His attention was a budget and the budget was fully allocated and had been for years.

---

The studio was above a vintage shop on the edge of the Northern Quarter, up a staircase that smelled of damp and old paper, behind a door with no sign on it because the whole point was that there was no sign. Cal had taken the lease under a name that wasn't his — well, a name that *was* his, the one he used for the only work that had ever felt like his own. He paid for it in cash he made from the work. It was the one clean loop in his life: the music paid for the room where he made the music. Nobody's nod required. Nobody watching from a touchline.

He let himself in at nine and the city was already going about its business below, delivery vans and the metal shutter of the shop rattling up, and he sat down at the desk in the dark and didn't turn the big lights on, just the lamp and the glow of the screen, and exhaled for what felt like the first time since the alarm.

Here was the thing nobody knew, the thing he'd guarded like a vault since he was seventeen: Cal Vance made beats. Not as a hobby. Not as a laugh. He made the records that other people's faces went on. Three tracks on a mixtape that had done a quarter of a million streams. A whole EP for an artist out of Moss Side that the blogs had lost their minds over, *who produced this*, and the answer was a credit that read *V.* and nothing else, because that was the deal, because Cal Vance the fly-half could not also be Cal Vance who made the kind of woozy, broken, late-night sound that the rugby world would have read as a distraction at best and at worst as — what his father would call it. *A waste.* The thing that had ruined Jordan.

The room locked again. He let the beat do what the locking couldn't, which was hold the shape of the feeling without letting it out. He pulled up the session he'd been building for three weeks — a thing for Knox, the only artist he worked with face to face, the only one who knew his real name. It was nearly there. A drum pattern that hung back half a beat, a chopped vocal he'd pitched down until it sounded like a memory of itself, a low synth that moved like weather. He'd been trying to make it sound like the inside of his own head and he'd nearly done it, which was the trouble. He played it back and it sounded like six a.m. on a frozen pitch and a girl taking his moves apart on a table and the locked room and Jordan, all of it, somehow, in two minutes and forty seconds with no words in it at all.

He took out the chopped vocal. Put it back. Took it out.

His phone lit on the desk. Knox: **studio thurs? bringing the team. label want a moodboard person, some art direction girl, she's apparently sick. proper vision. you'll like the sound of her stuff.**

Cal turned the phone face down.

He worked until the light through the high window went from grey to gold to grey again, until his stomach reminded him he hadn't eaten, until the budget of his attention was finally, blessedly spent and there was nothing left over to spend on remembering. He saved the session under its non-name. He turned off the lamp. He stood at the high window for a minute looking down at the wet street and the people who got to be one person each, and then he went back out into the daylight to be Cal Vance, the one they came to watch, and he was so good at it that no one ever asked who was watching him.

Not until Thursday.

But he didn't know that yet. He pulled the studio door shut behind him, and it locked, the way everything did, and he went down the stairs that smelled of old paper and out into the rain that South London girl had walked off into three nights before, and for no reason he could have defended to anyone, he found himself almost looking forward to a moodboard.
