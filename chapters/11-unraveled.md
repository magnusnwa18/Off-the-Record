# Chapter Eleven — Unraveled

*Ella*

For five weeks they were happy, and Ella learned that happiness was harder to write down than misery, because misery had edges and happiness was just a series of ordinary mornings that you'd have died to keep.

The single came out. *Creative direction, Eleonora Savage-Oluwa* — both names, spelled right, Cal had made sure — and it did the thing. The world she'd built went out into the world and people reposted it and a culture site ran a piece on "the visual language of Knox's new era" with her name in the third paragraph, and her course leader emailed her to say the external assessors had been asking about her, and her mother sent a screenshot of the culture-site piece to the family group chat with the caption *MY DAUGHTER* in capitals and seventeen of the not-quite-aunties had reacted, and Tioluwa had texted Ella privately, *you're so sick, I'm telling everyone at school you're my sister, can you get me into something,* and Ella had cried in the launderette-warm flat over a screenshot, which she'd have been embarrassed about if Priya hadn't cried too.

And Cal was there for all of it. Sideways, careful, secret, but there. He was the first person she called and the last person she saw. They built a life in the cracks of two impossible schedules — his rugby, her degree show looming, the both of them stealing hours in the unmarked studio that had become, without either of them saying so, *theirs*, the one room in the city where they got to be whole.

She moved a toothbrush in. He noticed and said nothing and bought a second mug, the kind she liked, heavy, and said nothing about that either, and she found it one morning and stood holding it in the grey light and felt the specific terror of being looked after, the burn-flinch, and made herself not flinch, made herself let it land, because letting him was the bravest thing she did all week and she did it every week.

"I keep waiting for it to go wrong," she told him, one of those ordinary luminous mornings, the both of them on the studio sofa with the second mug and the first, the city going about its grey business below. "I don't know how to just — have a good thing. I keep checking it for the catch."

"There's a catch," Cal said. "There's always a catch. We both know there's a catch. We're the two most catch-aware people in Manchester."

"That's not comforting."

"I wasn't trying to comfort you. I was agreeing with you." He put his arm around her. "There's a catch and it's coming and we don't have to pretend it isn't. But the catch isn't here yet. It's not in this room. Right now in this room there's just —" he gestured at the mugs, the grey light, her "— this. And I've spent my whole life so busy bracing for the catch that I missed the part before it. I'm not doing that with you. The catch can have everything else. It's not having this morning."

It was, Ella thought, the most romantic thing anyone had ever said to her, precisely because it didn't pretend the world was safe. He wasn't promising her forever. He was promising her *this morning*, which was the only thing anyone could actually promise, and he was the only person who'd ever been honest enough to promise only what was real.

She kissed him over the heavy mugs. "Okay," she said. "The catch can have everything else."

---

There was a Sunday in the middle of those five weeks that Ella would keep for the rest of her life, unspent, the way you keep one note you can't afford to break.

It rained all day, properly, the kind of Manchester rain that isn't an event but a climate, and neither of them had anywhere to be — a rare alignment, his training cancelled for a waterlogged pitch, her deck delivered, the world briefly not asking either of them to be anyone. They didn't go out. They stayed in the studio with the heater on and the high window streaming and made it a whole day, which neither of them had ever done with another person, given a day, a clear one, and just spent it.

He cooked, badly, on the single hob in the corner that was meant for warming takeaway, and she sat on the counter and narrated his failures like a nature documentary, and he retaliated by making her listen to the worst beat he'd ever made, age fifteen, a genuinely catastrophic thing he'd dug out of an old drive, and she laughed so hard she nearly fell off the counter and he caught her, easy, automatic, his hands knowing where she'd be before she fell the way they knew where the winger would be, and they stayed like that, her on the counter, him between her knees, both of them grinning like idiots, and he said, "I've never had a day off in my life. Not really. Even the days off were training for the days on. This is the first day I've ever had that isn't *for* anything," and she said, "Me too," and meant it more than she'd meant most things, because every day of her life had been for something, for the family, for the proof, for the future she was forcing into being, and this one was for nothing, this one was just hers, just theirs, just rain and a bad beat and a boy who caught her without looking.

She fell asleep on the sofa in the afternoon — she who ran on three hours and crashed for twelve, who never let herself be unconscious near anyone because unconscious was undefended and undefended was for people who had someone underneath them — and she woke an hour later under his jacket, which he'd put over her, and he was at the desk working with the volume low so as not to wake her, the lamp on, the city grey and pouring beyond the glass, and she lay there pretending to still be asleep just to watch him, the still one, the magic, working in the half-dark while he thought no one could see, and she understood with a clarity that frightened her that this — not the offer, not the degree show, not the name spelled right — *this* was the thing she'd been trying to build her whole life without knowing it. Not a career. A room where she could be unconscious and undefended and wake up covered.

"You're awake," he said, not turning round. "Your breathing changed."

"You weren't supposed to notice."

"I notice everything. It's the family curse." He turned then, and looked at her under his jacket on the sofa in the grey light, and something moved across his face that she'd never seen on it, something that on a less guarded man she'd have called fear and on Cal she understood was its cousin, *wanting something this much is dangerous, I've made a study of not wanting things this much,* and he said, quietly, "I keep waiting to get bored. Of people. I always get bored. It's why the bit works, the read, the soft landing — I've never met anyone who was still interesting on the second day." A pause. "It's been thirty-five days."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's a *fact*," they said it together, and laughed, and she got up still wrapped in his jacket and crossed the room to him, and that was the other thing she'd keep — that in the studio she was the one who crossed the room, always, that he'd crossed it first at the party and ever after she was the one who closed the distance, because he'd taught her without meaning to that crossing a room you might lose is the bravest thing a person does, and she'd decided she was done being the one who waited to be reached.

But the catch was already in the building, of course. It was wearing box-fresh trainers and an understated coat and a smile with no eyes in it, and its name was the story, and the story wanted out.

It started small. Sasha began appearing at the studio more — not unusual in itself, she was the label's fixer, the rollout was hers to manage. But she started appearing at the *odd* times. The late times. Once when Ella and Cal were alone, working, sitting closer than colleagues sat, and Ella saw Sasha clock it from the doorway before her professional face came down, and felt the cold settle in her stomach again, the first-sight-of-the-cliff cold.

"The single's doing numbers," Sasha said, all warmth, dropping onto the sofa, getting out her phone. "Which means the label wants the album rollout to be *bigger.* Which means they want a *story.* And the story they keep coming back to —" she looked up, pleasant, lethal "— is the mystery producer. V. The ghost behind the sound. They want a feature. A reveal. 'Who is V.' It'd be the biggest press the project gets. Bigger than the music, honestly, which is depressing but it's the world we're in." She let it sit. "V's resisting. Obviously. But the thing is, when an artist this size has a producer this good and this hidden, the press is going to *find out* eventually whether we manage it or not. Someone always talks. A leak, a session photo, a tagged location." Her eyes moved, briefly, to the toothbrush Ella had forgotten was visible by the little sink. "Better it comes from us. On our terms. With a narrative we control. That's all I keep telling him." She smiled. "And you, Ella — you'd be central to that story. The eye who built the ghost's world. You two are the whole narrative. It'd make your career overnight. I keep saying that too."

The cold spread. Ella understood, with the clean awful clarity she usually reserved for reading a room, exactly what Sasha was doing. She wasn't asking. She was *informing them of the weather.* She was telling them the reveal was coming, one way or another — managed or leaked — and offering Ella a choice that wasn't a choice: be the hero of the story and make your career, or be collateral when the story comes out anyway. And the thing she'd just done with her eyes, the flick to the toothbrush, said the other part, the part she was too smooth to say out loud: *I know there's something between you. I know it's a better story than even the producer reveal. And I will use whichever one I need.*

"V will reveal when V's ready," Ella said, keeping her voice level. "It's his face. His name. Not the label's. Not mine."

"Of course," Sasha said warmly. "Of course it's his choice." A beat. "I'd just hate for it to stop being his choice. Stories have a way of choosing for you, if you wait too long. Anyway." She stood, gathered her understated coat. "Think about it. Both of you. It really would be the making of you, Ella. The girl who unmasked the ghost." She paused at the door, exactly where Ella had once paused, the last to leave. "Funny how that line keeps coming back, isn't it. Almost like the story wants to be told."

And she was gone, down the stairs that smelled of old paper, and Ella stood in the studio that had become theirs with the cold all the way through her now, looking at the toothbrush, at the second mug, at the whole fragile luminous thing they'd built in the cracks, and understood that the catch had arrived, and that it was worse than either of them had braced for — because it wasn't going to make Cal choose between his two worlds.

It was going to choose for him. On someone else's terms. At the worst possible time.

She didn't tell Cal that night. She held it for one more ordinary morning, one more grey luminous morning with the two mugs, because she'd promised the catch could have everything else but not *this* morning, and she gave them one last clean one before the world came in.

It was, she'd think later, the kindest and most cowardly thing she ever did. She let him have one more day of being only happy. She held the weight by herself for one more day, the way she'd held weight her whole life, and she told herself it was a gift, and it was, and it was also the old reflex, the strong-one reflex, the one that said *I'll carry this so you don't have to* — the exact thing she'd promised Cal she wouldn't do to him, and he wouldn't do to her, and which, when the catch finally landed, was going to blow up in both their faces precisely because they were two people who'd never learned that some weights you're supposed to put down between you, in the middle, where both of you can see them.
