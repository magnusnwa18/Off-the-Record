# Chapter Seventeen — Encore

*Ella*

Here is what Ella built, in nine days, on no sleep, in the launderette-warm flat and a borrowed edit suite and a hundred phone calls, while the music press churned through the shallow version of the story and Sasha sent increasingly less-smiley texts trying to regain control of a narrative that had escaped everyone:

She built a film.

Not a campaign. Not a moodboard. A short film, eight minutes, to premiere at the album launch — the launch Sasha had been planning for months as the centrepiece of the rollout, the launch that the leak had turned from a celebration into a vulture-circling, *will V show his face* media event. Ella went to Knox first, because it was his launch and his album and she would not build anything on top of his night without his yes, and she showed Knox the rough cut on her laptop in the back of his car, and Knox — generous, chaotic Knox, who'd known Cal's real name longer than anyone and kept it like the secret it was — watched it twice and then put his hood up and turned his face to the window so Ella wouldn't see, and said, "Yeah. Yeah. We do it. But only if he says yes. It's his grave, Ella. You can't dig it up for him."

"I know. The film doesn't reveal anything. Watch the end again."

Because that was the whole architecture of the thing, the part that had taken her eight of the nine days to get right. The film was about V — about the ghost, the producer no one could name — but it never named him. It was eight minutes of the world she'd built for the album made into motion: the orange streetlight, the wrong film stock, the night-bus-at-3am ache of it, and over the top of it, Cal's music, the buried track, the real one, the one with Jordan's voice in it climbing and falling — and a voiceover, spoken by an actor, anonymous, telling the true story without a single identifying detail. *There's a producer in this city who buries his name in other people's songs. People think it's mystery. It's not mystery. It's a grave. Every track he's ever made has a voice in it, buried so deep you feel it more than hear it, and the voice belongs to someone he lost, and he hides his name so that the only person who'd be hurt by the music never has to hear it. He made himself a ghost to keep someone else safe. This whole album you're about to hear is a haunted house, and the ghost built it on purpose, out of love, and he has never once been thanked for it.*

And then — this was the part Knox watched twice — the film ended on a black screen, and a single line of white text, and the line was: *He can step out of the corner whenever he's ready. Some of us have always been able to see him.*

"It's an invitation," Ella told Knox. "Not a reveal. The film tells the *true* story — the deep one, the Jordan one, except it never says Jordan, never says Cal, never says rugby, none of the shallow stuff the blog got. It reframes the whole thing. The blog said 'rugby boy has secret hobby, is he even committed to the sport.' The film says 'a man turned his grief into a gift for half a million strangers and asked for nothing.' Same facts. Completely different story. Whoever's telling the story controls what it means, and I'm taking it back from the blog and from Sasha and giving it to —" her voice caught "— giving it to him. So that if he wants to step out, he steps out into *this* version, the true one, the one where he's not a liability or a scandal, he's the realest artist in the room. And if he doesn't want to step out, the film still works, it's still just a beautiful anonymous tribute to a ghost, and his secret's safe, because I never put his name in it. He has the only key. Still. I just built a door worth walking through."

Knox looked at her for a long moment in the back of the car. "You're in love with him."

"Yeah."

"He thinks you leaked him."

"Yeah."

"And you built him *this* anyway. On no sleep. While he thinks you're his enemy. Knowing he might never even —" Knox shook his head. "Ella. There's people who say they'd do anything for someone and there's people who go and *build* it at three in the morning for a person who's not speaking to them. You're the second kind. That's rare, man. That's rarer than V's beats." He put his hood down. "We're doing it. I'll get you the slot at the launch. Sasha's gonna lose her mind because it takes the narrative off her and she can't control what it means anymore, which —" he grinned "— is the best thing I've heard all week, honestly. She's been a vulture for a fortnight. Take it off her."

---

She had one more thing to do, and it was the hardest, and it had nothing to do with the film.

She turned down the offer.

She called Imogen Clarke-Bayo from the flat, at six o'clock, the hour she'd spent her whole life dreading, and she said, "I'm not going to take the September seat. I need to tell you why, because you were straight with me and you deserve straight back."

"Tell me," Imogen said, and Ella could hear her listening properly, the way powerful people who don't need to perform power listen.

"You told me the size of my start was downstream of the story breaking. The supernova. And you were right. And the story did break — the worst way, the leak, the betrayal version. And here's the thing I figured out: if I take the seat now, in September, riding the supernova, then for the rest of my life the biggest break I ever got came from the worst day of the person I love. I'd have built a career on top of his grave. And I can't — I won't be the person who got famous off the ghost. Sasha wanted me to be the girl who unmasked him. I'm not going to be the girl who profited off him being unmasked by someone else either. Those are the same thing wearing different coats." Ella's hands were steady, which surprised her. "So I'm going to do something stupid. I'm going to take the slot at the launch and premiere a film that takes the story back and gives it to him, and that film is either going to be the best thing I ever make or it's going to torch my reputation with the label and with Sasha and with half the people you'd want me to work with, and either way I'll have done it clean, on my terms, the way he should've gotten to come out on his. And then in October, when the supernova's over and nobody's offering me anything, I'd like to come and talk to you again. About a smaller start. One I actually earned with work instead of with someone's worst day."

The line was quiet for a moment.

"You know what's funny," Imogen said. "I told you most people never find out who they are because they never have to choose between what they want and who they want to be. I've made that little speech to a lot of frightened twenty-one-year-olds. You're the first one who chose *who you want to be* in real time, on the phone, turning down the biggest number I've ever offered someone your age." A pause. "The October conversation isn't going to be about a smaller start, Ella. It's going to be about a bigger one. Because the thing I actually can't afford isn't someone the supernova made hot. The thing I can't afford is someone who'll be loyal to the work and the people over their own ambition, because that person is *unbuyable*, and unbuyable is the only thing in this industry that's actually worth money in twenty years." Ella could hear the smile, the real one. "Go make your film. Torch whatever you have to torch. And call me in October. The seat'll be there. It might be a better seat."

Ella put the phone down at four minutes past six, on the worst hour of her life turned somehow into the best, and she sat in the launderette-warm flat with the dryers tumbling, and she did not feel like the strong one carrying the weight for everyone. She felt like someone who had, for the first time, picked up exactly the weight she wanted to carry, for exactly the reason she wanted to carry it, and found it was the only kind that doesn't crush you.

Then she texted Cal. She'd thought for nine days about what to say to a man who thought she'd betrayed him, a man who'd knifed her in the softest place she had, and in the end she didn't defend herself at all, because defending yourself is just more words, and she'd learned from the best that the realest thing is the thing you build, not the thing you say.

She sent him one line, the same number of lines he'd sent her, the cruelest order answered with the kindest:

*There's a film at the launch on Friday. You don't have to do anything. You don't have to come, you don't have to believe me, you don't have to forgive me. But it's about you, and it tells the true version, and I built you a door. Whether you walk through it is the only part I can't build. That part's always been yours.*

She didn't say *I love you.* She didn't say *it wasn't me.* She'd said *it wasn't me* eleven times already and he hadn't believed it, and you can't argue someone into believing the truth, you can only build it where they can see it and leave the last step to them.

She put the phone face down, the way he always did, and she went back to the edit, and she did not let herself wait for a reply, because waiting for a reply was a kind of looking away, and she was done apologising for wanting things, and the thing she wanted now wasn't a reply.

It was to watch a ghost cross a room. One more time. With everyone watching. Even if he crossed it away from her — *especially* then — because she'd finally understood that loving someone isn't building the room they have to walk into. It's building the room and then loving them enough to let them choose not to.
