# Epilogue — One Year On

*Ella*

Parklife, the second weekend of June, Heaton Park gone gold and heaving under a sky that had — miraculously, this being Manchester — decided to behave. Ella stood at the side of the main stage in the dust and the noise with a laminate around her neck that said *CREATIVE DIRECTOR* and both her names spelled right underneath, Savage and Oluwa, and watched a year of her life become true in front of forty thousand people.

Knox's album had gone gold in the autumn. The film she'd built in nine sleepless days had been written up everywhere, *the night the ghost crossed the room*, and it had done exactly the thing she'd told Imogen it might do — torched some bridges, built others. Sasha had moved to a label in London and sent, to Ella's genuine surprise, a single text the week the film went up: *You took the story off me. Cleanly. I respect it. Call me if you ever want to be ruthless for money instead of for love — you'd be frighteningly good at it.* Ella never had. She'd called Imogen instead, in October, exactly as planned, and the seat had been a better seat, and she'd taken it on her own terms, in the cold light of no supernova, earned the slow way.

She freelanced now, mostly. The studio had come to her in the end, the way it does when you stop chasing it and start being undeniable.

Out on the stage, under the gold, Knox was three songs deep and the crowd was a single animal, and at the back of the stage, half in shadow the way he liked it, hood down for once, stood the man working the live production — the one the festival booked specially, the one the artists asked for by a name that wasn't a secret anymore but still wasn't loud, because some people are quiet by nature and not only by wound.

Cal caught her eye across the stage and did the thing. Went completely still in the middle of all the noise. The counting stillness. And then — and this was new, this was the year's ten thousand reps made visible — he smiled, the real one, the unguarded one, and he didn't go back to the corner. He crossed the stage to her, through the cables and the crew, in front of everyone, and put his arm around her, easy, public, a man who had stopped being a ghost.

"You did this," he said into her ear, over the noise. "All of it. The whole world out there. You built it."

"You made the sound."

"I made the sound. You made the *world*. We've been over this. It's the whole argument." He'd said it a hundred times and it never stopped being the thing they returned to, the original friction turned into the foundation. "Your dad texted. He and your mum are watching the stream. Your mum says the staging is —" he checked his phone, deadpan "— 'very nice but you could still have done it from a position of something.' And then a row of hearts."

Ella laughed, the real one, into the gold afternoon.

Things were not perfectly mended, because nothing real is. Cal still pointed it the wrong way sometimes, the intensity, the fear; he'd done it twice in the year and both times she'd named it and both times he'd put it down, faster each time, the reps adding up. His father came to things now — rugby and music both, the two no longer at war in the old man, who'd quietly become Cal's most disconcerting fan and had taken, alarmingly, to using the word "drop" incorrectly in sentences about beats. Cal still played rugby; he'd signed the academy contract in the end, on his terms, with a clause his agent had thought was insane that let him produce, openly, no more ghost. *I'm not choosing between the pitch and the desk,* he'd told his father, and his father, for once, had not made him. Jordan's records lived in Cal's flat now, out of Catford, out of the grave, played loud. His name was on the tracks. *Produced by Cal Vance.* Both worlds in one room at last, because the one person who'd ever stood in all his rooms at once had refused to let him keep them apart.

And the *I love you* — the one she'd kept that night in the chapel, the one he had to earn the slow way?

She'd said it on an ordinary Tuesday in November, in the launderette-warm flat with the dryers tumbling, no occasion, no stage, no forty thousand people. He'd done some small sideways looking-after thing — the right food, the heater already on, a deadline he'd quietly taken thirty minutes of pressure off — and she'd looked up from her laptop and the blue light had been on her face and she'd just said it, plainly, the way the truest things get said, in the warm, to one person, for no audience at all. *I love you.* And he'd gone still, the counting stillness, and then he'd said it back, and it hadn't been a grand gesture or a crossed room or a film at a launch. It had been two people in a small warm flat above a launderette, finally, neither of them performing being fine, because they weren't fine, they were something much better than fine.

Now, on the main stage at Parklife with the city's whole summer roaring out in front of them, Cal pulled her in and said, "The catch can have everything else."

"It's not having this afternoon," Ella finished, and meant it, and leaned into him, the strong one who'd learned how to be held, the eye who'd built a hundred worlds for everyone else and finally one for herself — and for him, the door she'd built that he'd walked through, the only world either of them had ever wanted, which turned out not to be a stage or a studio or a stadium at all.

It was just the one other person who could see you. Standing still in the loudest room. Crossing it anyway.

The sun went down gold over Heaton Park, and Knox hit the song with Jordan's four notes buried in it, climbing and falling, and forty thousand people who'd never know whose voice they were feeling more than hearing put their hands in the air, and two people at the side of the stage stood very still in the middle of all of it, fully visible, fully seen, home.
