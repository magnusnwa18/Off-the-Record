# Chapter Four — Studio Lights

*Cal*

Knox arrived at the studio on Thursday in the way Knox arrived everywhere, which was as a weather system — door banging, hood down, a carrier bag of cans in one hand and his phone held in front of his mouth like a microphone, finishing a voice note to someone, and three people behind him who Cal didn't know and immediately catalogued: a videographer with a camera already half-up, a label A&R in box-fresh trainers, and a woman in expensive understatement who Cal pegged as the fixer before she'd said a word. Sasha, Knox called her. She smiled at Cal with her whole face and none of her eyes and he understood her instantly, which was its own kind of warning.

"V," Knox said, dropping the cans, pulling Cal into a one-armed thing. He was the only one in the world allowed to use the producer name to Cal's face, and he used it like a secret they were both keeping, which it was. "Play them the thing. Play them the thing, man, they don't believe me."

"Sit down. You're making the room nervous."

"I *am* the room." But Knox sat, sprawling across the battered sofa under the high window, and the others arranged themselves, and the A&R said something about scheduling, and Sasha said something smooth about "the bigger picture, the rollout, the *story*," and Cal turned to the desk and did the only thing in his life that quieted everything, which was play them the music.

He played the track he'd been killing himself over for three weeks. The one that sounded like the inside of his head. He played it loud, on the good monitors, and he watched the room the way he watched every room — and he saw it land. Saw the A&R stop scrolling. Saw the videographer lower the camera to just listen. Saw even Sasha's professional smile slip into something briefly genuine. The drums hanging back half a beat. The vocal pitched into a memory. The low synth moving like weather over the rooftops outside.

"That," said Sasha, when it ended, into the quiet. "*That* is the record. Marcus, that's your first single, I don't care what else you've got."

"Told you," Knox said, but he was looking at Cal, and the look was thank you, and Cal gave him the nod — the small one, the one he'd inherited from his father and repurposed into something he could actually afford to give.

"We need to talk about who's making the world for this," Sasha went on, all business now. "The visuals. I'm not putting a sound like that out with a stock-template rollout. We need someone with a real eye who isn't going to cost forty grand. Marcus has a candidate."

"My boy Tom knows her," Knox said. "Did a thing for 'Lowlight' just for the love, put it online, it's —" he kissed his fingers. "She gets it. She's coming now. Should be —"

The buzzer went.

Cal got up to hit the door release the way he always did, half-listening, his mind still down in the mix wondering if the chopped vocal wanted to come back in the second eight, and he pressed the button and heard the street door go and feet on the stairs that smelled of old paper, and he turned back to the desk to pull up the session for her to hear, the art direction girl, *some art direction girl, she's apparently sick, proper vision, you'll like the sound of her stuff* —

The studio door opened.

Ella Savage-Oluwa walked in out of the grey Northern Quarter light with a leather folder under her arm and a face composed into pure, expensive professionalism, *good morning, thank you for seeing me,* already scanning the room the way she'd scanned the party, reading it, and her eyes went round — the sofa, Knox, the A&R, Sasha, the camera — and arrived at the desk.

At him.

Cal watched it happen in real time, the recognition, the world reorganising itself behind her eyes, and to her enormous credit not one muscle in her face moved. The professionalism stayed welded on. But he knew her now, he'd spent a week not thinking about her with the dedication of a man thinking about someone constantly, and he saw the thing she did at the party, the holding of the gaze instead of the apology, and underneath it, fractionally, the same thing he was feeling, which was: *oh no.*

"Ella!" Knox was up off the sofa, all warmth. "Yes. You found it. No sign on the door, right, V likes it mysterious. This is V — well, that's the only name you get —" Knox laughed "— he's the magic. He made the thing you're about to hear. V, this is Ella, the one I told you about, the eye."

There was a beat. A small, vertiginous beat in which the entire shape of both their lives wobbled on a point.

"We've met," Ella said.

It was the perfect move, Cal thought, with the helpless admiration he'd felt at the party. Truthful — so she couldn't be caught in a lie later. Vague — so it surrendered nothing. And it threw the next word to him, made him choose how much to spend, in a room full of people who could not, under any circumstances, learn that "V" was a fly-half whose father stood on a touchline at six a.m.

"At a party," Cal said. Even. Easy. The daylight voice. "Friend of Tom's, right."

"Right." Ella crossed the room and shook his hand, firm, brief, her eyes telling him a whole paragraph that her mouth wasn't: *I don't know what you are, but I need this room more than I need to expose you, so let's both be very careful.* Her hand was warm. He let go a half-second after he should have.

"Small world," Sasha said pleasantly, watching them both with the patient attention of someone whose whole job was noticing what people tried to hide. Cal added a line to her file. *Dangerous.*

"Manchester's a village pretending to be a city," Ella said smoothly, and turned to Knox, to Sasha, to the actual room, and dropped Cal entirely, which was the right thing to do and which stung in a way he had no business being stung by. "Can I play you something? I built a deck. But honestly the deck's secondary. I'd rather you heard the world first and then I'll show you how I'd build it. Can I —" she gestured at the monitors, at the desk, at *him* "— can we play the single again, and I'll talk over it?"

"You haven't heard the single," the A&R said.

"I don't need to have heard it to know what it's doing," Ella said. "I've heard everything else he's made. May I?"

Cal looked at her, and she looked at him, and the room didn't know it was watching the second move of a game whose first move had happened on Egerton Road. He cued the track. He pressed play. And Ella Savage-Oluwa stood in the middle of the studio above the vintage shop and talked over his music — *his* music, the inside of his head, the thing he'd never let anyone describe because no one had ever known it was his to describe — and what she said about it took the floor out from under him.

She didn't talk about it like a stylist selling a service. She talked about it like she'd been *inside* it. "This sound is nostalgic for a present it's still living in," she said, walking the room slowly, eyes half-closed, "it's homesick for now. The drums are late on purpose — they're reluctant. It's the sound of someone who's brilliant at something and resents being watched do it." She wasn't looking at him. She had no idea. She was reading the music the way she'd read the room, the way she'd read *him*, and she didn't know that the man she was undressing in front of a label was standing four feet away. "So the world can't be slick. Slick would be a lie. It has to look like the inside of a night bus at 3am — that orange streetlight, condensation, everything beautiful and slightly ruined. I'd shoot it all on the wrong film stock. I'd make the type look like it's been left out in the rain. The whole identity should feel like a secret that got out."

The room was silent. Even Sasha.

*A secret that got out,* Cal thought, and didn't trust himself to speak.

"That," said Knox, very quietly, "is exactly the thing. That's exactly it." He looked at Sasha. Sasha looked at the A&R. The A&R nodded.

"Show us the deck," Sasha said. "But I think we already know."

Ella opened the leather folder.

And Cal, who had spent five years building a wall between the boy on the touchline and the ghost behind the boards, who had organised his entire existence around the principle that no one would ever be allowed to stand in both rooms at once, watched the one girl who'd ever taken him apart on a table walk straight through the wall and set up her work on the other side of it, and he understood, with the clean cold clarity he usually reserved for reading a defensive line, that he was in an enormous amount of trouble.

She got the job. Of course she got the job. There was never any version where she didn't.

It was only afterward, when the others had gone clattering down the stairs to find food and Sasha had air-kissed the room and Knox had pulled Cal into another one-armed thing and said *we're gonna make something real, man,* that Ella turned at the door, the last to leave, and looked at him properly for the first time since she'd walked in. The professionalism came off like a coat.

"So," she said. "'V.'"

"Ella."

"You're the ghost." She said it slowly, fitting the pieces, and he watched her get there, watched her arrive at the size of it — *the fly-half, the one scouts come to watch, is the producer no one can name.* "Nobody knows. Do they. Not Tom. Not — anyone."

"No."

"Why?"

He could have done the bit. The deflection, the charm, the soft landing. He had ten of them ready. Instead, because she'd already taken every one of them apart once and he was so tired of the wall, he told her the truth, or the first inch of it.

"Because if it has my name on it," Cal said, "it stops being mine."

Ella looked at him for a long moment in the grey light from the high window, the city tumbling on below, and whatever she'd walked in prepared to think about him rearranged itself again behind her eyes.

"Okay," she said finally. "Then I'll keep your secret, V. Because I need this project and you need it quiet, so we have a deal." She put her folder under her arm. At the door she paused, and the corner of her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "But I want you to know I'm only keeping it because it's useful to me. Not because you came over anyway."

And she was gone, down the stairs that smelled of old paper, into the rain, and Cal Vance stood alone in the room where he kept the only true thing about himself and discovered that the wall he'd spent five years building had a door in it now, and that she had the only key, and that — God help him — he'd handed it to her himself.
