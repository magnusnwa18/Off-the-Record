# Chapter Three — Two Cities

> *Long chapter · built to its natural length. Scenes marked with ◆. Two families, two cities, one secret. Ella goes home to South London for her mother's fiftieth and finally tells the truth about her career; Cal is pulled deeper into the rugby/family machine; the big match brings both worlds into one stadium, his father in the stand and Ella secretly watching, and the truth Cal's been holding gets within a single sentence of the light. Absorbs and expands old short-chapters 9–10.*

---

## ◆ i. The Train South

*Ella*

She went home to South London for her mother's fiftieth because there were things in life you did not get to skip, and a Savage-Oluwa milestone was the most non-negotiable of all of them, and she took the train down from Piccadilly on the Friday with a knot in her stomach that had nothing to do with the party and everything to do with the decision she had made, somewhere over the five stolen weeks, to finally tell her family the truth about what she was building. Not the graduate-scheme-adjacent version. Not the *I'm exploring some creative opportunities while I keep my options open* version she'd been feeding them in instalments to manage the disappointment down to a survivable size. The real one.

She'd practised it on Cal the night before, in the studio, pacing the worn boards while he sat at the desk and let her, because he'd learned that when Ella paced she was building something and the worst thing you could do was offer to help build it.

"I'm going to tell them I didn't apply for the Hargreave scheme and I'm not going to," she'd said. "That I've got a real project, a real client, a real release, my name in the credits of a thing the whole country's seen. That I'm going to walk into the degree show with the most ambitious final project on the course and use it to start a career, not a hobby. And that I love them and I see exactly what they gave up and I'm not spitting on the climb — I'm *spending* it, the way it was meant to be spent, on a life that's actually mine." She'd stopped pacing. "And it's going to go terribly."

"Maybe," Cal had said. He didn't do false comfort; it was one of the things she'd come to rely on, that he wouldn't tell her a thing was fine when it might not be. "But you'll have said the true thing out loud. That's the part that matters. A true thing you're carrying is a secret, and it weighs what a secret weighs. A true thing you've said out loud is just a fact in the world. It's lighter. Even when it goes badly. *Especially* when it goes badly, actually, because then at least you're not also carrying the lie on top of the bad thing." He'd looked at her with the thing in his face that she'd stopped being able to misread. "I'd know. I'm a national expert on the weight of the unsaid true thing."

"You should take your own advice. With your dad."

"One reckless honesty at a time, Savage-Oluwa." But it had landed; she'd seen it land; she'd filed it, the way she filed everything, for later.

She held *it's lighter, even when it goes badly* the whole way down, through the long grey middle of England, the towns going past in the dark like a melody she couldn't quite hear. She did not let herself think too hard about the other thing — the thing she also wasn't telling her family, the boy thing, the secret on top of the secret. One reckless honesty at a time. She would give them the career truth this weekend. The boy could wait. The boy *had* to wait, because the boy came with a locked room and a loaded gun and a father at a touchline, and you did not unpack a thing that complicated at a fiftieth.

Her phone buzzed as the train pulled into Euston. Cal: *Text me when you've said it. However it goes. I'll be up. (Match tomorrow, can't sleep anyway.)*

She'd forgotten, almost — the match. The big one, the regional final, the one Priya had mentioned because Tom had mentioned it, scouts in the stand, the kind of fixture that decided futures. Tomorrow, while she was being honest in a kitchen in Lewisham, Cal would be running out in front of the biggest crowd of his life with his father in the stand. Two cities. Two reckonings. The same weekend. She hadn't planned the symmetry and she distrusted it the way she distrusted all neat things, because neat things in her experience were usually a story tightening its grip.

*I'll text you,* she wrote back. *Good luck tomorrow. Play loose.* And then, because the stolen weeks had taught her she was allowed to: *Both of us being brave in two different cities at once. Don't lose it.*

The reply came as she stepped onto the platform into the particular roar of Euston, the south of England closing over her head like water she'd been born in and swum out of: *Both names spelled right. Go and tell them who you are.*

## ◆ ii. The House That Twenty-Five Years Built

*Ella*

The house in Lewisham was full, the way it was always full, the way it had been engineered over twenty-five years to be full — aunties and uncles who were not technically either, cousins to several degrees, the pastor and his wife, neighbours, the whole architecture of a community her parents had built out of nothing and jollof and relentless reliability, the social capital of a family that showed up, every time, for everyone, banked over a quarter of a century and paying out now in a front room packed wall to wall for Folake Savage-Oluwa's fiftieth.

Her mother was resplendent. There was no other word and Ella, who had a great many words, didn't reach for another: deep green and gold, gele architectural and exact, fifty years old and entirely unbowed, moving through the room like — and Ella felt the small shock of it land as she watched from the doorway — like Ella herself moved through an industry room. Reading it. Working it. Knowing where everyone was and what they needed and giving it to them a half-second before they asked. *Oh,* Ella thought, with a lurch that rearranged something. *That's where I got it. The watching. The managing. I thought I invented it in a cold hall at nine. I didn't. I inherited it. She's been doing it for fifty years and I learned it off her the way Cal learned the gap off Jordan.*

It went beautifully for three hours, and she let it. She held her mother and meant it. She danced with her father, who still danced like it was 1991, like the minicab years and the lost first job and the damp-country flat had never happened, joy as its own kind of defiance. She let Tioluwa — seventeen now, sharp, watching everything the way Ella had watched everything at seventeen, the next inheritor of the family gift — drag her into a corner and interrogate her about Manchester, about the project, about the culture-site piece that Tio had apparently shown her entire year group. She was, for three hours, the eldest daughter, home, a credit, the proof the whole room had been built to display.

And then the crowd thinned, the not-quite-aunties drifting off into the London night with foil-wrapped plates, and it was down to family in the kitchen — the washing-up, the leftovers, the particular late-party tiredness that makes people honest — and her mother, drying a serving dish with the methodical care she gave everything, said, without looking up, in the voice that had crossed two oceans, "So. The Hargreave scheme. The deadline passed. Tobi's mother asked me at church and I had to make a face like I knew what was happening with my own daughter, which I did not, which was not a nice face to have to make in front of Tobi's mother."

The kitchen didn't go silent — there were too many people, the tap running, Tio's music still going from her phone on the windowsill — but a particular silence opened in the middle of it, the family silence, the one Ella had grown up navigating like weather.

This was it. The platform. The space the train pulls into.

*It's lighter, even when it goes badly. Go and tell them who you are.*

"I didn't apply, Mum," Ella said. And turned off the tap, so the words would have somewhere to land. "And I'm not going to."

## ◆ iii. Show Me

*Ella*

"You forgot the deadline," her mother said. Carefully. Setting the dried dish down. Giving Ella the exit, the graceful lie, the off-ramp — *you forgot, it slipped, you're so busy, we'll sort it, there's always next year.* Her mother was a master of the merciful exit; she'd spent twenty-five years building exits for people so they could keep their dignity in her kitchen. It was, Ella realised, the same instinct as Cal's no-invoice kindness, the same instinct as her own — the family gift again, the watching, turned to grace.

"I didn't forget it." Ella's hands were wet. She didn't dry them. She looked at her mother in her green and gold, fifty and magnificent and afraid — always afraid, underneath, the fear that had crossed two oceans and never fully set its bags down. "I didn't apply because I'm not going to do it. I've got a real project, Mum. A real artist — you've heard of him, Tio's played him in this house a hundred times. A real label behind it, a real release, out now, doing numbers. I'm art-directing the whole visual world of it, the thing you saw in the piece, *creative direction by Eleonora Savage-Oluwa* — and I'm going to put it in my degree show in front of the assessors and the people who hire, and I'm going to start a career out of it. A real one. Not a hobby I do until the babies come. The thing I'm actually, genuinely good at."

"Eleonora —"

"Let me finish. Please. I've been practising and if you stop me I'll lose it." She kept her voice level, the strong-one voice, except she let something through it that she usually sealed off entirely, which was the cost. "I know what you and Dad gave up to get us here. I know it better than Tio does, better than the boys do, because I was old enough to *see* it. I saw you at this table — not this one, the old one, the flat — at midnight, when Dad lost the first job. I was nine. You weren't crying. You were holding yourself so still and breathing so carefully so that we wouldn't hear, and Dad came and put his hand on the table next to yours, not holding it, just there, and you sat like that over the letters, and I watched from the hall." Her mother had gone very still. Her father, by the back door with a bin bag, had stopped moving entirely. "And I decided that night — nine years old — that I would never be a thing you had to carry. That I'd be the easy one. That I'd handle myself so completely you'd never have to do that midnight maths with me in the sum. And I kept it, Mum. For twelve years. I have been the strong one, the sorted one, the credit, since I was nine, and I am so *tired* —" her voice cracked, clean, and she let it, in the crowded warm kitchen, in front of all of them "— I'm so tired, and the only place in my whole life where I'm not holding something up is when I'm building a world for a sound. It's the only place I get to put it down. And that's not nothing, and it's not a hobby, and it's not me spitting on the climb. You climbed so I could stand somewhere high enough to *choose.* So I'm choosing. I'm not asking permission — I'm twenty-one, I'm past permission. I'm asking you to be proud of me for the thing I actually am, instead of grieving the daughter who would have done law."

The tap dripped. Tio had turned her music off without anyone noticing the moment it stopped. The not-quite-aunties who'd drifted back in for tea had gone quiet by the door. Her father stood with the bin bag in his hand and his face doing something Ella couldn't read, which frightened her, because she could always read him.

For a long moment her mother said nothing, and Ella braced for the whole familiar architecture — the scripture, the comparison to Uncle Femi and the restaurant, the *creative people die poor*, the *we did not cross water for this.*

"Show me," her mother said instead.

"What?"

"The work. This *world* you have built. You think I have not —" her mother's mouth did something complicated, almost a tremor, almost the beginning of a thing "— you think I am fifty years old and dead? Tioluwa plays this boy in my house every single day. *Knox.*" She said the name carefully, like a foreign coin, but the tongs had loosened. "Show me what my daughter made for Knox. If you are going to throw away the Hargreave scheme — and you are, I can see it, you have your father's face when you have decided a thing — then the least you can do is let your mother *see* the thing you threw it away for. I am not agreeing to anything. I am not saying it is wise. I am saying —" and her voice, for one syllable, went somewhere it almost never went, somewhere unguarded "— *show me.*"

Ella's hands were shaking. She dried them, finally, and got out her phone, and she showed her mother the world: the orange light, the wrong film stock, the rain-ruined type, the single cover she'd built on a studio floor three feet from a boy she couldn't mention. She talked her mother through it the way she'd talked the label through it — *this is a secret that got out, this is homesick for now* — and her father set down the bin bag and came to look over her mother's shoulder, and Tio crowded in, and the not-quite-aunties drifted closer, and somewhere in the explaining the kitchen stopped being a tribunal and became a room full of people looking at a thing their girl had made with her own head and her own eye, a thing that existed in the world, that strangers had seen, that had her name on it.

"This is on the radio?" her father asked. He had a way of asking the practical question that was really the emotional one in disguise. "This song. With this — picture you made."

"It will be everywhere, Dad. It already is. The single's out. With my name in the credits. Your name. On a thing the whole country's going to see."

Her father looked at the phone for a long time. He was not a man who said things easily; he had spent twenty-five years saying things with his hands instead — with the jobs and the table and the dancing. "Our name," he said finally, quietly. "On a thing the whole country sees." And he nodded — once, small, downward, and Ella felt the floor tilt, because it was the *exact* nod Cal had described his father giving, the earned one, the one that runs a whole life — except this one she had earned not by doing the safe nameable thing but by refusing it, and the difference between those two nods was the entire distance between her family and his. "Good," her father said. "That's — good, omo mi. Make sure they spell it right. The whole name. The Savage and the Oluwa. Both. People will want to shorten it. Don't let them."

It was not approval, exactly. Her mother, returning to the dishes, said she still thought a graduate scheme behind her would not have *hurt*, that creative people died poor, that Ella should at least keep the option open, leave a door ajar — and Ella let her have all of it, because she'd won the only thing that mattered, which was *show me* instead of *no*, which was her father saying *both names, don't let them shorten it*, which was the climb, finally, paying out the thing it had been for.

## ◆ iv. The Step, Again

*Ella*

It was Tioluwa who found her afterward, when the kitchen had emptied and Ella had taken a plate she wasn't eating out to the back step in the cold, the South London sky orange overhead the way every sky she loved was orange — she'd never noticed before that she'd built a whole aesthetic out of the colour of the sky over the house she grew up under.

"You scared me in there," Tio said, sitting down beside her, pulling her sleeves over her hands. "You cracked. On purpose. At the sink, in front of everyone. To get Mum to say *show me.* I've never seen you do that. You don't crack. You're the one who doesn't crack." She looked at her sister with something between awe and accusation. "Was it real or was it a move?"

"Both," Ella said, surprised into the truth. "That's the thing nobody tells you about being the strong one, Tio. After enough years you can't always feel the seam between what you mean and what you're performing, because you've performed *fine* for so long that fine became a skill instead of a feeling. Tonight it was real *and* it was a move. I cried because I meant it and I let everyone see because I needed Mum to soften and I knew the crack would do it. Both. That's not lying. That's just being someone who learned to use her own true things as tools, because no one was ever going to use them *for* her, so she had to."

Tio was quiet a while, the muffled party-noise behind the door, the orange sky. "I'm not going to be the strong one," she said eventually, fierce, decided, seventeen. "You did it so I don't have to. You went into the minefield first. You took the law-versus-art fight and you basically *won* it tonight, and now when I do something mad in a few years Mum's going to have a — a script for it. Because of you. You're the one who walks into the minefield first so everyone behind you knows where the mines are."

And Ella looked at her little sister and felt something redistribute in her chest, an old weight shifting to a better-balanced place — because she'd spent her whole life experiencing the carrying as a tax, a thing done *to* her, the eldest's unfair sentence, and here was Tioluwa telling her it had *bought* something, that the strength she'd resented had cleared a path, that the wall she'd been for the family had been load-bearing in the good sense too, holding up a roof her siblings got to grow up under without ever having to crack on purpose at a sink.

"Don't walk into minefields," Ella said, and put her arm around her sister, and Tio let her. "But — yeah. I know where some of them are now. So ask me first. That's allowed. You're allowed to ask me first instead of finding them with your feet."

"Is he nice?" Tio said, into her shoulder.

Ella went still. "Is who nice?"

"Whoever you keep checking your phone for. You've looked at it fourteen times since dinner. You think I don't watch you? I learned watching off you, and you learned it off Mum, it's the family curse, you said so yourself just now basically." Tio grinned up at her, wicked. "There's a boy in Manchester. You've got a face about him. Is he nice?"

Ella thought about a chip-shop step three hundred miles north, a different step, the same orange light, a boy who'd built her a room with the price torn off everything and a furnace he was learning to make into warmth and a dead brother buried four feet down in every song he made and a father in a stand tomorrow who didn't know the half of his own son.

"He's complicated," she said. Which was the truest answer available, and the only one she could give. "And I can't talk about it yet. It's — there's a lot. It's not just a boy. But —" she squeezed Tio "— yeah. He's the realest thing that's happened to me. And Mum's going to hate it for about a year and then probably decide she invented him."

"That means yes," Tio said, with enormous satisfaction, "and that he's trouble," and the two of them sat on the cold step under the orange London sky and laughed — two sisters, one who'd gone into the minefield and one who got to walk the cleared path — and it was, Ella thought, the lightest she had felt at this house in years.

She texted Cal from the step, while Tio went in for cake: *I told them. It went — not terribly. Mum said "show me" instead of "no." Dad wants them to spell the whole name right, both, won't let them shorten it. I cried at the sink and I'm not even ashamed.*

The reply came fast, which meant he'd been waiting, which meant he hadn't been sleeping, which meant the match was sitting on him the way the kitchen had sat on her: *Both names. Savage and Oluwa. I'll make sure of it on every asset, I'll fight the label on it personally. Proud of you. That's the bravest thing — saying it out loud at the table. Told you it's lighter.*

And then, a minute later, the thing that made her press the phone to her chest on the cold step: *I keep thinking about what you said. Both of us being brave in two cities at once. You did yours. Mine's tomorrow. I don't know if I can do mine. But you did yours, so maybe.*

She didn't know, reading it on the step, exactly how literally he meant it — that *mine's tomorrow* wasn't only the match — that somewhere three hundred miles north a boy was lying awake the night before the biggest fixture of his life having watched his secret girlfriend walk into a kitchen and tell the truth, and was wondering, for the first time in five years, whether he might be able to do the same at a rail, to a father, with everyone watching.

She didn't know it would get within one sentence of happening, and then not happen, and that she'd be there to see the not-happening from four seats over. She knew only that it was late, and her family loved her, and she'd set a weight down at a sink and it hadn't crushed anyone, and the climb her parents made had finally bought the thing it was for, and a complicated boy three hundred miles away was being brave in his own city tomorrow because she'd been brave in hers tonight.

Two cities. One weekend. The same orange sky, she'd have bet, over both.
