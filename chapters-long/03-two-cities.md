# Chapter Three — Two Cities

> *Long chapter · built to its natural length. Scenes marked with ◆. Two families, two cities, one secret. Ella goes home to South London for her mother's fiftieth and finally tells the truth about her career; Cal is pulled deeper into the rugby/family machine; the big match brings both worlds into one stadium, his father in the stand and Ella secretly watching, and the truth Cal's been holding gets within a single sentence of the light. Absorbs and expands old short-chapters 9–10.*

---

## ◆ i. The Train South

*Ella*

She went home to South London for her mother's fiftieth because there were things in life you did not get to skip, and a Savage-Oluwa milestone was the most non-negotiable of all of them, and she took the train down from Piccadilly on the Friday with a knot in her stomach that had nothing to do with the party and everything to do with the decision she had made, somewhere over the five stolen weeks, to finally tell her family the truth about what she was building. Not the graduate-scheme-adjacent version. Not the *I'm exploring some creative opportunities while I keep my options open* version she'd been feeding them in instalments to manage the disappointment down to a survivable size. The real one.

She'd practised it on Cal the night before, in the studio, pacing the worn boards while he sat at the desk and let her, because he'd learned that when Ella paced she was building something and the worst thing you could do was offer to help build it.

"I'm going to tell them I didn't apply for the Hargreave scheme and I'm not going to," she'd said. "That I've got a real project, a real client, a real release, my name in the credits of a thing the whole country's seen. That I'm going to walk into the degree show with the most ambitious final project on the course and use it to start a career, not a hobby. And that I love them and I see exactly what they gave up and I'm not spitting on the climb — I'm *spending* it, the way it was meant to be spent, on a life that's actually mine." She'd stopped pacing. "And it's going to go terribly."

"Maybe," Cal had said. He didn't do false comfort; it was one of the things she'd come to rely on, that he wouldn't tell her a thing was fine when it might not be. "But you'll have said the true thing out loud. That's the part that matters. A true thing you're carrying is a secret, and it weighs what a secret weighs. A true thing you've said out loud is just a fact in the world. It's lighter. Even when it goes badly. *Especially* when it goes badly, actually, because then at least you're not also carrying the lie on top of the bad thing." He'd looked at her with the thing in his face that she'd stopped being able to misread. "I'd know. I'm a national expert on the weight of the unsaid true thing."

"You should take your own advice. With your dad."

"One reckless honesty at a time, Savage-Oluwa." But it had landed; she'd seen it land; she'd filed it, the way she filed everything, for later.

She held *it's lighter, even when it goes badly* the whole way down, through the long grey middle of England, the towns going past in the dark like a melody she couldn't quite hear. She did not let herself think too hard about the other thing — the thing she also wasn't telling her family, the boy thing, the secret on top of the secret. One reckless honesty at a time. She would give them the career truth this weekend. The boy could wait. The boy *had* to wait, because the boy came with a locked room and a loaded gun and a father at a touchline, and you did not unpack a thing that complicated at a fiftieth.

Her phone buzzed as the train pulled into Euston. Cal: *Text me when you've said it. However it goes. I'll be up. (Match tomorrow, can't sleep anyway.)*

She'd forgotten, almost — the match. The big one, the regional final, the one Priya had mentioned because Tom had mentioned it, scouts in the stand, the kind of fixture that decided futures. Tomorrow, while she was being honest in a kitchen in Lewisham, Cal would be running out in front of the biggest crowd of his life with his father in the stand. Two cities. Two reckonings. The same weekend. She hadn't planned the symmetry and she distrusted it the way she distrusted all neat things, because neat things in her experience were usually a story tightening its grip.

*I'll text you,* she wrote back. *Good luck tomorrow. Play loose.* And then, because the stolen weeks had taught her she was allowed to: *Both of us being brave in two different cities at once. Don't lose it.*

The reply came as she stepped onto the platform into the particular roar of Euston, the south of England closing over her head like water she'd been born in and swum out of: *Both names spelled right. Go and tell them who you are.*

## ◆ ii. The House That Twenty-Five Years Built

*Ella*

The house in Lewisham was full, the way it was always full, the way it had been engineered over twenty-five years to be full — aunties and uncles who were not technically either, cousins to several degrees, the pastor and his wife, neighbours, the whole architecture of a community her parents had built out of nothing and jollof and relentless reliability, the social capital of a family that showed up, every time, for everyone, banked over a quarter of a century and paying out now in a front room packed wall to wall for Folake Savage-Oluwa's fiftieth.

Her mother was resplendent. There was no other word and Ella, who had a great many words, didn't reach for another: deep green and gold, gele architectural and exact, fifty years old and entirely unbowed, moving through the room like — and Ella felt the small shock of it land as she watched from the doorway — like Ella herself moved through an industry room. Reading it. Working it. Knowing where everyone was and what they needed and giving it to them a half-second before they asked. *Oh,* Ella thought, with a lurch that rearranged something. *That's where I got it. The watching. The managing. I thought I invented it in a cold hall at nine. I didn't. I inherited it. She's been doing it for fifty years and I learned it off her the way Cal learned the gap off Jordan.*

It went beautifully for three hours, and she let it. She held her mother and meant it. She danced with her father, who still danced like it was 1991, like the minicab years and the lost first job and the damp-country flat had never happened, joy as its own kind of defiance. She let Tioluwa — seventeen now, sharp, watching everything the way Ella had watched everything at seventeen, the next inheritor of the family gift — drag her into a corner and interrogate her about Manchester, about the project, about the culture-site piece that Tio had apparently shown her entire year group. She was, for three hours, the eldest daughter, home, a credit, the proof the whole room had been built to display.

And then the crowd thinned, the not-quite-aunties drifting off into the London night with foil-wrapped plates, and it was down to family in the kitchen — the washing-up, the leftovers, the particular late-party tiredness that makes people honest — and her mother, drying a serving dish with the methodical care she gave everything, said, without looking up, in the voice that had crossed two oceans, "So. The Hargreave scheme. The deadline passed. Tobi's mother asked me at church and I had to make a face like I knew what was happening with my own daughter, which I did not, which was not a nice face to have to make in front of Tobi's mother."

The kitchen didn't go silent — there were too many people, the tap running, Tio's music still going from her phone on the windowsill — but a particular silence opened in the middle of it, the family silence, the one Ella had grown up navigating like weather.

This was it. The platform. The space the train pulls into.

*It's lighter, even when it goes badly. Go and tell them who you are.*

"I didn't apply, Mum," Ella said. And turned off the tap, so the words would have somewhere to land. "And I'm not going to."

## ◆ iii. Show Me

*Ella*

"You forgot the deadline," her mother said. Carefully. Setting the dried dish down. Giving Ella the exit, the graceful lie, the off-ramp — *you forgot, it slipped, you're so busy, we'll sort it, there's always next year.* Her mother was a master of the merciful exit; she'd spent twenty-five years building exits for people so they could keep their dignity in her kitchen. It was, Ella realised, the same instinct as Cal's no-invoice kindness, the same instinct as her own — the family gift again, the watching, turned to grace.

"I didn't forget it." Ella's hands were wet. She didn't dry them. She looked at her mother in her green and gold, fifty and magnificent and afraid — always afraid, underneath, the fear that had crossed two oceans and never fully set its bags down. "I didn't apply because I'm not going to do it. I've got a real project, Mum. A real artist — you've heard of him, Tio's played him in this house a hundred times. A real label behind it, a real release, out now, doing numbers. I'm art-directing the whole visual world of it, the thing you saw in the piece, *creative direction by Eleonora Savage-Oluwa* — and I'm going to put it in my degree show in front of the assessors and the people who hire, and I'm going to start a career out of it. A real one. Not a hobby I do until the babies come. The thing I'm actually, genuinely good at."

"Eleonora —"

"Let me finish. Please. I've been practising and if you stop me I'll lose it." She kept her voice level, the strong-one voice, except she let something through it that she usually sealed off entirely, which was the cost. "I know what you and Dad gave up to get us here. I know it better than Tio does, better than the boys do, because I was old enough to *see* it. I saw you at this table — not this one, the old one, the flat — at midnight, when Dad lost the first job. I was nine. You weren't crying. You were holding yourself so still and breathing so carefully so that we wouldn't hear, and Dad came and put his hand on the table next to yours, not holding it, just there, and you sat like that over the letters, and I watched from the hall." Her mother had gone very still. Her father, by the back door with a bin bag, had stopped moving entirely. "And I decided that night — nine years old — that I would never be a thing you had to carry. That I'd be the easy one. That I'd handle myself so completely you'd never have to do that midnight maths with me in the sum. And I kept it, Mum. For twelve years. I have been the strong one, the sorted one, the credit, since I was nine, and I am so *tired* —" her voice cracked, clean, and she let it, in the crowded warm kitchen, in front of all of them "— I'm so tired, and the only place in my whole life where I'm not holding something up is when I'm building a world for a sound. It's the only place I get to put it down. And that's not nothing, and it's not a hobby, and it's not me spitting on the climb. You climbed so I could stand somewhere high enough to *choose.* So I'm choosing. I'm not asking permission — I'm twenty-one, I'm past permission. I'm asking you to be proud of me for the thing I actually am, instead of grieving the daughter who would have done law."

The tap dripped. Tio had turned her music off without anyone noticing the moment it stopped. The not-quite-aunties who'd drifted back in for tea had gone quiet by the door. Her father stood with the bin bag in his hand and his face doing something Ella couldn't read, which frightened her, because she could always read him.

For a long moment her mother said nothing, and Ella braced for the whole familiar architecture — the scripture, the comparison to Uncle Femi and the restaurant, the *creative people die poor*, the *we did not cross water for this.*

"Show me," her mother said instead.

"What?"

"The work. This *world* you have built. You think I have not —" her mother's mouth did something complicated, almost a tremor, almost the beginning of a thing "— you think I am fifty years old and dead? Tioluwa plays this boy in my house every single day. *Knox.*" She said the name carefully, like a foreign coin, but the tongs had loosened. "Show me what my daughter made for Knox. If you are going to throw away the Hargreave scheme — and you are, I can see it, you have your father's face when you have decided a thing — then the least you can do is let your mother *see* the thing you threw it away for. I am not agreeing to anything. I am not saying it is wise. I am saying —" and her voice, for one syllable, went somewhere it almost never went, somewhere unguarded "— *show me.*"

Ella's hands were shaking. She dried them, finally, and got out her phone, and she showed her mother the world: the orange light, the wrong film stock, the rain-ruined type, the single cover she'd built on a studio floor three feet from a boy she couldn't mention. She talked her mother through it the way she'd talked the label through it — *this is a secret that got out, this is homesick for now* — and her father set down the bin bag and came to look over her mother's shoulder, and Tio crowded in, and the not-quite-aunties drifted closer, and somewhere in the explaining the kitchen stopped being a tribunal and became a room full of people looking at a thing their girl had made with her own head and her own eye, a thing that existed in the world, that strangers had seen, that had her name on it.

"This is on the radio?" her father asked. He had a way of asking the practical question that was really the emotional one in disguise. "This song. With this — picture you made."

"It will be everywhere, Dad. It already is. The single's out. With my name in the credits. Your name. On a thing the whole country's going to see."

Her father looked at the phone for a long time. He was not a man who said things easily; he had spent twenty-five years saying things with his hands instead — with the jobs and the table and the dancing. "Our name," he said finally, quietly. "On a thing the whole country sees." And he nodded — once, small, downward, and Ella felt the floor tilt, because it was the *exact* nod Cal had described his father giving, the earned one, the one that runs a whole life — except this one she had earned not by doing the safe nameable thing but by refusing it, and the difference between those two nods was the entire distance between her family and his. "Good," her father said. "That's — good, omo mi. Make sure they spell it right. The whole name. The Savage and the Oluwa. Both. People will want to shorten it. Don't let them."

It was not approval, exactly. Her mother, returning to the dishes, said she still thought a graduate scheme behind her would not have *hurt*, that creative people died poor, that Ella should at least keep the option open, leave a door ajar — and Ella let her have all of it, because she'd won the only thing that mattered, which was *show me* instead of *no*, which was her father saying *both names, don't let them shorten it*, which was the climb, finally, paying out the thing it had been for.

## ◆ iv. The Step, Again

*Ella*

It was Tioluwa who found her afterward, when the kitchen had emptied and Ella had taken a plate she wasn't eating out to the back step in the cold, the South London sky orange overhead the way every sky she loved was orange — she'd never noticed before that she'd built a whole aesthetic out of the colour of the sky over the house she grew up under.

"You scared me in there," Tio said, sitting down beside her, pulling her sleeves over her hands. "You cracked. On purpose. At the sink, in front of everyone. To get Mum to say *show me.* I've never seen you do that. You don't crack. You're the one who doesn't crack." She looked at her sister with something between awe and accusation. "Was it real or was it a move?"

"Both," Ella said, surprised into the truth. "That's the thing nobody tells you about being the strong one, Tio. After enough years you can't always feel the seam between what you mean and what you're performing, because you've performed *fine* for so long that fine became a skill instead of a feeling. Tonight it was real *and* it was a move. I cried because I meant it and I let everyone see because I needed Mum to soften and I knew the crack would do it. Both. That's not lying. That's just being someone who learned to use her own true things as tools, because no one was ever going to use them *for* her, so she had to."

Tio was quiet a while, the muffled party-noise behind the door, the orange sky. "I'm not going to be the strong one," she said eventually, fierce, decided, seventeen. "You did it so I don't have to. You went into the minefield first. You took the law-versus-art fight and you basically *won* it tonight, and now when I do something mad in a few years Mum's going to have a — a script for it. Because of you. You're the one who walks into the minefield first so everyone behind you knows where the mines are."

And Ella looked at her little sister and felt something redistribute in her chest, an old weight shifting to a better-balanced place — because she'd spent her whole life experiencing the carrying as a tax, a thing done *to* her, the eldest's unfair sentence, and here was Tioluwa telling her it had *bought* something, that the strength she'd resented had cleared a path, that the wall she'd been for the family had been load-bearing in the good sense too, holding up a roof her siblings got to grow up under without ever having to crack on purpose at a sink.

"Don't walk into minefields," Ella said, and put her arm around her sister, and Tio let her. "But — yeah. I know where some of them are now. So ask me first. That's allowed. You're allowed to ask me first instead of finding them with your feet."

"Is he nice?" Tio said, into her shoulder.

Ella went still. "Is who nice?"

"Whoever you keep checking your phone for. You've looked at it fourteen times since dinner. You think I don't watch you? I learned watching off you, and you learned it off Mum, it's the family curse, you said so yourself just now basically." Tio grinned up at her, wicked. "There's a boy in Manchester. You've got a face about him. Is he nice?"

Ella thought about a chip-shop step three hundred miles north, a different step, the same orange light, a boy who'd built her a room with the price torn off everything and a furnace he was learning to make into warmth and a dead brother buried four feet down in every song he made and a father in a stand tomorrow who didn't know the half of his own son.

"He's complicated," she said. Which was the truest answer available, and the only one she could give. "And I can't talk about it yet. It's — there's a lot. It's not just a boy. But —" she squeezed Tio "— yeah. He's the realest thing that's happened to me. And Mum's going to hate it for about a year and then probably decide she invented him."

"That means yes," Tio said, with enormous satisfaction, "and that he's trouble," and the two of them sat on the cold step under the orange London sky and laughed — two sisters, one who'd gone into the minefield and one who got to walk the cleared path — and it was, Ella thought, the lightest she had felt at this house in years.

She texted Cal from the step, while Tio went in for cake: *I told them. It went — not terribly. Mum said "show me" instead of "no." Dad wants them to spell the whole name right, both, won't let them shorten it. I cried at the sink and I'm not even ashamed.*

The reply came fast, which meant he'd been waiting, which meant he hadn't been sleeping, which meant the match was sitting on him the way the kitchen had sat on her: *Both names. Savage and Oluwa. I'll make sure of it on every asset, I'll fight the label on it personally. Proud of you. That's the bravest thing — saying it out loud at the table. Told you it's lighter.*

And then, a minute later, the thing that made her press the phone to her chest on the cold step: *I keep thinking about what you said. Both of us being brave in two cities at once. You did yours. Mine's tomorrow. I don't know if I can do mine. But you did yours, so maybe.*

She didn't know, reading it on the step, exactly how literally he meant it — that *mine's tomorrow* wasn't only the match — that somewhere three hundred miles north a boy was lying awake the night before the biggest fixture of his life having watched his secret girlfriend walk into a kitchen and tell the truth, and was wondering, for the first time in five years, whether he might be able to do the same at a rail, to a father, with everyone watching.

She didn't know it would get within one sentence of happening, and then not happen, and that she'd be there to see the not-happening from four seats over. She knew only that it was late, and her family loved her, and she'd set a weight down at a sink and it hadn't crushed anyone, and the climb her parents made had finally bought the thing it was for, and a complicated boy three hundred miles away was being brave in his own city tomorrow because she'd been brave in hers tonight.

Two cities. One weekend. The same orange sky, she'd have bet, over both.

## ◆ iv-c. The 07:12 North

*Ella*

She wasn't going to go to the match. That was the plan she'd gone to sleep on, in her childhood bed under the orange Lewisham sky, having said the true thing at the sink and won her small victory: stay till Sunday, be the eldest daughter for one more day, let her mother fuss and her father play her the old highlife records he only played when he was happy. A good plan. A sensible plan. The strong-one plan.

She woke at six and booked the 07:12.

She did it before she could talk herself out of it, the way she'd booked nothing in her life — not researched, not optimised, not weighed against the cost — just lay in the dark with Cal's last text on the ceiling of her mind, *both of us being brave in two cities at once,* and understood that she could not be three hundred miles away while the person she — while *he* ran out in front of the biggest crowd of his life with his father in the stand and his whole locked self under the kit. She had to be there. Not for him to know — he didn't know, she wouldn't tell him, she'd just *be* there, four rows up, a witness, the one person in forty thousand who'd see all of him. It was the most useless, unstrategic, expensive thing she'd done in years: a day-return to Manchester for ninety minutes of a sport she didn't follow, to watch in secret a boy she couldn't claim.

It was, she thought, watching the grey middle of England unscroll backward through the window, also the most *her* thing she'd done in years — except the new her, the cracked-open one, the one who'd let a heater be on and a price be torn off and a friend's fear be real. The old Ella would have stayed in London and called it sensible. The new one was on the 07:12 with a coffee she didn't want, going north toward a stand, because she'd finally learned that showing up unstrategically for the people you love is not a weakness in the system, it *is* the system, it's the only part of any of it that's real.

Her phone buzzed through the journey, the two cities reaching after her. Tioluwa: *mum's already telling the aunties you're "in fashion media." you've been promoted overnight. she's so proud she's furious about it.* Priya, from Manchester: *TOM SAYS THE MATCH IS HUGE TODAY are you actually coming back for it?? i thought you were in london till sun, come SIT with me, i need you, long story, tom-related.* (So Priya needed her too; the timing wasn't only about Cal; the universe was efficient with its summons.) And Dr. Annan, of all people, a forwarded email with two words on top — *Read this* — the externals' preliminary note, mentioning *the Knox identity work* as "the standout real-world project in the cohort." On a train. From a tutor who never praised. Ella read it four times and pressed the phone to her chest in the empty carriage and let herself feel, for one stop, the enormous unfamiliar thing: that it might actually be working. That the reckless bet — real client, real stakes, real exposure — might actually beat Margot's flawless fiction. That her father's *both names, don't let them shorten it* might end up on a wall the externals walked past in June.

And under the joy, the cold tenant, right on time: *it's all downstream of one secret holding. The standout real-world project has a centre of gravity that's a ghost. If the ghost gets exposed, the standout project becomes the cautionary tale. Margot's only variable is Margot. Yours is a boy on a pitch three hundred miles ahead of this train, and a fixer with no eyes in her smile, and a story that wants out.* She watched the country go by. She did not have an answer to the cold tenant. She had only the 07:12, and a witness's seat, and the new conviction that you show up anyway, unstrategically, for the real thing, and let the variables do what variables do.

The train pulled into Piccadilly at five to ten. The match was at three. She had time to go home, change into something that wasn't yesterday's party, find the scarf Tom had left for her with Priya "just in case," and become, for one afternoon, an anonymous girl in a club scarf in a cold stand — the least visible she'd been in months, and, she'd realise later, watching Cal find the looseness in the second half, the most completely *present* she'd ever managed to be: there for everyone, claimed by no one, seeing all of it. The witness. The eye. Doing, in a stadium, the only thing she'd ever truly known how to do, which was see a person whole — except this time the person was hers, secretly, and the seeing was love, and no one in forty thousand knew but her.

## ◆ v. Both Worlds in One Stand

*Cal*

The fixture was the biggest of his life, and he had organised himself for it the way he organised himself for everything that mattered, which was to feel as little as possible until the whistle and then let the body do what five years had built it to do. His father had driven up from London — of course he had; Gareth Vance had not missed a match since Cal was six, the attendance itself a kind of fixed star, colder and heavier than love because it never once took a day off. He was in his seat, third row behind the posts, the seat he always took, and he did not cheer when Cal ran out, because cheering was for spectators, and Gareth Vance was not a spectator. He was *invested*. He sat with his arms folded and watched his son the way a man watches a thing he has built and is not yet sure will hold.

Cal had not told Ella about the match in any detail, beyond *match tomorrow.* She was in London. She was being brave in her own city; he hadn't wanted to crowd it with his. So he ran out onto the pitch in front of the biggest crowd of his life, did the thing he always did — looked up, on instinct, to find his father's cold invested stillness in the third row — and then let his eyes finish the sweep of the stand the way they always swept a room, automatic, threat-and-gaps, and they caught, four rows up and a little to the left of his father, on a face that should have been three hundred miles south.

Ella.

Wrapped in a scarf in his club's colours — Tom's doing, that scarf, had to be — sitting beside a beaming Priya, in a stand in Manchester on a Saturday afternoon when she was supposed to be recovering from her mother's fiftieth in Lewisham. She'd come back. She'd taken the early train, he'd work out later, the first one she could, three hundred miles for ninety minutes of a sport she didn't care about, to sit in a cold stand and watch him — and she was watching him now, across forty metres of churned grass, with an expression he couldn't read at that distance but that made his chest do something that had no place in the four minutes before a regional final.

Both worlds. In one stand. His father, third row, who needed Cal to be only ever the fly-half, the safe son, the nameable thing, the proof the wasteful one hadn't been the only outcome. And Ella, four rows up, who knew about the studio and the ghost and the furnace and Jordan, who'd lain with her head in his lap while the buried track played, who was the one living person on earth who knew there was a whole other Cal, a fuller one, standing on this pitch pretending to be half a man. Four rows apart. Neither knowing who the other was. The cotton-finishing-works city he'd shown her at midnight and the touchline city his father had raised him in, collapsed into a single set of terraced seats on a grey Saturday.

He played the worst first half of his life.

He couldn't find the stillness. The counting wouldn't come. Every time the ball came to him he was aware of being watched by the two people whose watching meant the most and meant the most *opposite* things — his father, who needed the performance to be total and seamless and only ever the one self; and Ella, who knew exactly how much that performance left out, in whose presence the performance had nowhere to hide, because you cannot show half a self to someone who has seen the whole thing. That was it. That was the diagnosis he'd arrive at, steaming, at half-time. It wasn't that she was watching. It was that in front of her he could no longer convincingly *be only the fly-half*, and he'd never in his life been all of himself at once on a pitch in front of his father, and the contradiction was tearing his game in half. He threw a pass into touch. He missed a tackle he'd have made asleep. He kicked a restart dead and heard, faintly, in the great wash of the crowd, the specific silence of his father not making a sound.

At half-time Marsh took him aside, low and furious. "Where the hell are you, Vance? You've left your head somewhere on the way here. There are men in that stand today who don't come back for a second look. Go and *get it.* Whatever it is. Find it and bring it back out here or this is the day it all stops, and you'll spend the rest of your life knowing it stopped on a Saturday you weren't paying attention."

And Cal stood in the cold with the steam coming off him and the half-time noise around him and understood, finally, completely, the thing he'd been refusing to understand for five years — the thing Ella had named for him in a studio weeks ago without either of them seeing how far it went. *You're not two people. You're one thing, split into two, so each half can hide from the people who'd take it.* The boxes didn't work anymore. He'd built his whole life on keeping the worlds apart, on being a different complete person in each, and it had only ever worked because no one stood in more than one room at once. But Ella stood in all of them. She was in the stand watching the fly-half *and* she was the only person alive who also knew the producer and the brother and the furnace, and you could not, it turned out, perform half a self for someone who'd seen the whole. The performance had nowhere to hide. That was why he was falling apart — not because she was watching, but because in front of her he could no longer *not* be all of it at once, and he'd never been all of it at once on a pitch, and the only way to play the second half was to stop trying.

So he stopped trying to be only the fly-half.

He went back out and he played the way he made music.

He stopped calculating the odds and started feeling the shape of the game the way he felt the shape of a track — where it wanted to rise, where it wanted to hang back half a beat, where the gap was, where the note you don't play lives. He played *loose*, the looseness Webb had clocked and feared and prized, except now Cal knew where it came from, knew it was Jordan, knew it was the box room and *listen under the kick* and a girl who read his hips, knew it was the exact same half-second of hearing-where-a-thing-is-going that made a beat land and a pass hang. The first time the ball came to him in the second half he didn't think about it at all. He looked at the man he wasn't passing to, sold him the lie with his hips the way Jordan had taught an eleven-year-old to sell a gap, and put the pass into space that wasn't there yet — soft, weighted, hanging in the cold air exactly as long as it needed to hang — and the winger ran onto it and went under the posts, and the stand came up roaring, forty thousand people on their feet, and Cal didn't look at his father.

He looked at Ella.

She was up with everyone else, scarf and all, but she wasn't doing the polite impressed thing the crowd was doing. She was *grinning* — the real one, the unmanaged one, the one he'd been collecting — and she had her hand pressed flat to her chest, and he knew that gesture, he'd earned that gesture a dozen times in a studio, it was the *that's it, that's the centre* gesture, the one she made when a thing he'd produced did the exact thing it was built to do. She was watching him produce a rugby match. She could see the music in it. She was the only person in forty thousand who knew she was watching all of him at once, the fly-half and the ghost in the same body in the same moment for the first time in his life — and she was not asking him to be less. She was pressing her hand to her chest because the whole of him had finally, for ninety seconds, shown up in one place, and she'd seen it, and it was the thing she'd been telling him for weeks was possible.

He played the second half of his life. They won by twenty. The scouts wrote things down; he'd find out later how much. And when the final whistle went and the crowd poured and his teammates piled onto him, Cal extracted himself and walked toward the stand, toward his father, because that was the ritual, the thing you did, you went to get the nod.

His father was waiting at the rail. Gareth Vance looked at his son for a long moment, and the cold invested stillness cracked — just slightly, just at the edges — into something that in another man might have been joy.

"You found it," his father said. "Second half. Whatever that was. Where did that come from?"

And Cal, high on the win, raw from the half-time understanding, with Ella four rows up watching the back of his head, *almost said it.*

It was right there. The whole truth, loaded and ready: *It came from the music, Dad. It came from playing like Jordan. That thing you've been trying to coach into me for sixteen years, the half-second of time on the ball that makes the pass different — it's the same thing that made Jordan hear what no one else could hear, the thing you called a waste, the thing you think killed him. It's been in me the whole time. I've been making music for five years. I'm V. The thing you grieve in him and the thing you're proud of in me are the same thing, and I've been hiding half of myself from you since I was sixteen because I thought it would kill you to see it.* It was all there, one sentence from the light, his father's face open for the first time in five years, the win still roaring around them, Ella four rows up who'd been brave in her city last night —

"Whatever you did," his father said, before Cal could choose, gripping his shoulder, hard, the grip that had run his whole life, "*do it every week.* That's the level the contract's at. That's the level that gets you capped. This is everything we worked for, son. Everything. Don't you dare lose it now." And the openness in his face resolved, as Cal watched, back into the old thing — the invested thing, the *one of you was going to be the safe son and it's you, it has to be you, I cannot survive it being neither* thing — and the window closed.

Cal gave him the nod. The small one. The inherited one. "Yeah, Dad," he said. "Every week."

And he turned away with the truth still locked in him, swallowed whole, sitting in his chest where it had sat for five years — and four rows up, Ella, who had watched the whole exchange from too far to hear the words and close enough to read the shape, who knew him well enough to read the shape of *anything* he did, had seen it. He met her eyes across the emptying stand. Her grin was gone. She'd watched the window open in his face and watched him fail to climb through it. She'd watched him have the true thing in his mouth — the lighter thing, the said-out-loud thing she'd managed at a sink the night before — and swallow it back down.

She didn't look away. Neither did he. Forty metres and a closed window between them, in a stadium with both his worlds in it, and the only thing that passed between them was the thing they both already knew: that she had done hers, and he had not done his, and that the distance between *show me* and *every week* — between a father who said *both names, don't let them shorten it* and a father who said *don't you dare lose it* — was the whole reason one of them got to be brave at a kitchen table and the other was still, after five years, a ghost at his own life, holding his breath at a rail, swallowing the bit at the end.

## ◆ v-b. The Other Couple in the Stand

*Ella*

She'd had a whole other afternoon, up in that stand, that Cal couldn't see from the pitch — because four rows up, beside her, Priya was having the worst day of her spring, and Ella, who'd come back from London on the first train specifically to be present for someone, spent half the match realising she'd come back for the wrong someone, or at least not the only one who needed her.

Tom's family had come to the final. ## ◆ v-c. What the Scouts Wrote Down

*Cal*

Webb rang on the Sunday morning, which meant the Saturday had changed Cal's life, because Webb only rang on Sundays when your life had changed.

"Right," Webb said, no hello, the relish of a man holding good cards. "I'm not going to gas you up, so I'll just say the facts and you can do whatever you do with your face on the other end of the phone. Two clubs. Senior setups, not academy — *senior.* One of them sent their attack coach, who does not travel for academy fixtures, who travelled for you. The second half, Cal. Whatever you found at half-time — they saw it. The attack coach used the word 'rare.' He said you've got something he can't coach, the time-on-the-ball thing, and he wants a conversation, and a conversation with this man is not a conversation, it's the beginning of a contract that changes your family's life." A pause, weighted. "So. We're going to take meetings. And here's where I do the part you hate. At this level, the due diligence is total. They will look at *everything*, Cal. Your socials, your associations, your medical, your habits, your nights. There are clubs that have walked away from better prospects than you over a thing that turned up in a search. So I'll ask you the Sunday question one more time, and this time it actually matters, because this time there's a contract behind it." His voice flattened into the register he kept for the things that were real. "Is there anything. Anything at all. That a club's due-diligence team, who are very good and very thorough and very much not your friends, would find. Because if there is, I need it now, today, so I can get ahead of it — and if there is and you don't tell me and they find it, it doesn't just cost you the contract. It costs you *me.* Because I can't represent a man who lets me walk into a room not knowing the thing that's going to be on the table."

And Cal stood in the bare room with the Sunday light coming grey through the curtain, the win still humming in his battered body, his father's *every week, son* still ringing, Ella's hand-pressed-to-her-chest still behind his eyes — and looked at the question that was no longer abstract, that had a senior contract and his family's changed life and his agent's loyalty stacked behind it now, and he understood that the locked room had just become the single most expensive object he owned. Not a private grief anymore. A *liability*, in the language of due diligence, a thing on a table, a search result waiting to happen. *V* had two hundred and fifty thousand streams and a Northern Quarter studio leased in a name that was his and a photographable window and a feature artist who called him *bruv* and a fixer building a reveal — and a due-diligence team that was very good and not his friend would find all of it in an afternoon.

"Nothing," Cal said. The flat pass. Weighted. Hung exactly as long as it needed to. "I'm boring, Webb. You've said so yourself."

The silence on the line was longer this time. Webb had clients for a living; Webb knew the sound of a man choosing a lie. "Mm," he said, finally, and there was something almost gentle under it, almost sad, the *mm* of a man deciding, again, not to push, and Cal understood that Webb had felt the lie and filed it and was giving Cal one more chance to bring it to him before the world brought it to them both. "All right. We take the meetings. Be boring. Stay boring through the contract. And Cal —" the pause, the door left open "— whatever the nothing is. The clock on it just changed. A secret you could've kept forever as a nobody is a secret with a *value* now, the day you're somebody, because now there are people who'd pay for it and people who'd lose money over it, and that's the day secrets stop being yours and start being *assets* — yours if you manage them, someone else's if you don't. You've got a window to decide how this one gets told. Windows close. That's all. I'll send the dates."

He rang off, and Cal sat on the edge of the bare bed with the phone in his hand and two warnings now tolling against each other — Webb's *windows close* and Ella's *the truth said out loud is lighter* — and for the first time the two warnings were saying the same thing from opposite directions, the agent and the girlfriend in unwitting chorus: *tell it yourself, on your terms, before it gets told for you.* He'd nearly done it at the rail. He hadn't. And now the price of not-doing-it had a number on it, a senior number, a family's-changed-life number, and the window Webb described was the same window Sasha was prying at from the music side, and somewhere in the middle of the two industries pulling at the locked door was a boy who'd carried a bomb so carefully for five years that he'd forgotten bombs have a shelf life, and that the most dangerous thing you can do with one is keep it long enough that someone else decides when it goes off.

He texted Ella — *meetings this week. senior clubs. it's happening. also: you were at the match. four rows up. don't pretend. we'll talk tonight* — and then he sat in the grey Sunday and did the arithmetic he could never make come out, except now there was a new term in it, a senior contract, his father's face when the nod finally meant *capped*, and against it, on the other side of the equation, a locked room with his brother in it and a girl who saw all of him and a window that everyone kept telling him was closing.

## ◆ vi. The Bomb You Carry So It Won't Go Off Near Anyone

*Ella*

That night, in the studio, she didn't congratulate him on the match.

She'd thought about it the whole way over — what to say, whether to say anything, how to hold the thing she'd watched from four rows up without dropping it on him before he was ready. She'd come straight from the stadium, peeled off from Priya and Tom with a lie about a deadline (another small load-bearing lie, added to the pack), and let herself into the unmarked room where Cal was already at the desk, not working, just sitting in the lamp-dark with a track playing low — the buried one, she realised, the centre, Jordan four feet down — and a beer he wasn't drinking, the same way he'd held a bottle he wasn't drinking at the party the night they met, the held prop, the tell.

She didn't say *you were incredible*, though he had been. She didn't say *the scouts looked happy*, though they had. She let him come down from it. She fed him — the no-invoice thing, returned now in the other direction, the Vietnamese already ordered, *I was getting mine*, and he caught the echo of his own kindness and almost smiled. She sat with him. And only much later, with the city dark and wet below and Jordan's four notes climbing and falling in the speakers, did she say, carefully, the way she said the heavy things:

"You almost told him. At the rail. I could see it from up there — I couldn't hear it, but I could see your face do the thing it does in here when you're deciding whether to play me the centre. You had it in your mouth. And then you swallowed it."

Cal was quiet for a long moment. The track played. "Yeah."

"What stopped you?"

"Same thing that always stops me." He looked at the dark window. "If I tell him, I lose him. He'll hear it as me choosing the thing that killed Jordan. He'll hear it as losing the last son too. He drove three hundred miles to watch me today, Ella. He cracked, at the rail, for half a second — that's more than I've got out of him in five years — and if I'd said it, the crack would've slammed shut and I'd have turned the best day of his year into the day he found out his living son's been secretly doing the wasteful thing in the dark the whole time. I can't. I can't do that to him. I can't lose him."

"Or," Ella said, very gently, and she put her hand flat on the desk next to his, not touching, just there, the midnight-table hand, on his side, "he'll hear that the thing he's been calling a waste for sixteen years is the *exact* thing that made the pass different today. The thing he was so proud of he drove three hundred miles for. You played like that *because* of the music, Cal — you told me so yourself, at half-time, in your head, I can see it on you. The looseness is Jordan. The time-on-the-ball is the box room. So maybe the truth isn't 'I'm choosing the thing that killed Jordan.' Maybe the truth is: 'Dad, the thing you loved in me on that pitch today and the thing you grieved in Jordan are the same thing. It's not a waste. It's the best thing in both of us. And you've spent five years trying to cut it out of the one son you've got left, because you think it's the disease, when actually it's the — it's the heart. It's the actual heart of both your boys, and you've been trying to perform surgery to remove it, on the survivor, out of love and out of terror, and it's killing him slower than the road killed his brother but it's still killing him.'"

The track played. Climbed. Fell. Cal didn't say anything.

"I'm not telling you to tell him," Ella said. "I told you weeks ago, I'd never — visibility isn't free, I know to the penny what it costs, you know to the penny what it costs, neither of us gets to be glib about it. I'm not handing you a brave speech and a deadline." She paused, and chose the truest thing she had, the thing she'd carried down the stand and across the city. "I'm just saying — you keep treating the truth like a bomb. And maybe it is one. But you've been carrying a live bomb for five years so it wouldn't go off near anyone you love. You've held it so carefully, for so long, walking through every room of your life with this thing in your arms that could detonate, never setting it down, never sleeping properly, because the second you put it down it might — and I've watched what carrying like that does to a person, Cal. I've *been* a person who carries like that. I did it for twelve years and I only set my smaller bomb down last night, at a sink, and you know what happened? It didn't go off. It just — became a fact. Lighter. Like you said." She looked at him. "I don't want to watch you carry your bomb for the next fifty years so it won't hurt your dad, while it quietly takes you apart instead. That's the only thing I'll say about it. That's all. I'll never say it again unless you ask."

She didn't say anything else about it. She kept her word — the way she always kept her word, which was the thing about her that had undone him from the start, the loyalty that cost her and that she paid without an invoice. They sat in the lamp-dark with the dead brother climbing and falling in the speakers and the two cities behind them — her kitchen where the truth got said, his rail where it got swallowed — and neither of them solved anything, because some things don't get solved in a night, they just get *seen*, and the seeing is the seed.

But Cal lay awake long after she'd gone, in the bare professional room, the buried track looping in his headphones, and for the first time in five years he let himself imagine actually saying it. The whole thing. Out loud. To his father. The truth as a fact in the world instead of a bomb in his chest. And it terrified him so completely, so totally, that he understood — with the cold clear part of him that had never once been wrong about a defensive line — exactly what the terror meant.

You only get that scared of the things you've already, somewhere underneath, decided you're going to do.

He didn't know when. He didn't know how. He didn't know that it would be taken out of his hands before he found the courage to do it on his own terms — that the bomb he'd carried so carefully for five years would be detonated by someone else, on someone else's schedule, in the worst possible order, at the worst possible time. He knew only that the carrying had a horizon now, where before it had had none; that a girl had stood in two cities in one weekend and shown him that the truth said out loud was lighter than the truth held; and that he was, God help him, beginning — just beginning, just at the edges, the way he'd begun to play loose — to want to set the thing down.

Outside, over Manchester, the sky was the same orange Ella had sat under in Lewisham the night before. She'd have bet on it, and she'd have been right. Two cities. One sky. One weekend in which one of them got brave and the other got within a single sentence of it — which is, in the end, how courage usually arrives: not all at once, but one city at a time, one sink at a time, one rail you didn't quite climb through but now know is there.

## ◆ vi-b. Six O'Clock, After

*Ella*

The six o'clock call changed after the sink, which Ella had not expected, because she'd braced for it to get worse — for the daily dread to curdle into a daily argument now that the truth was out. ## ◆ vi-c. The Father, Lingering

*Cal*

His father didn't drive straight back to London after the match. That was the thing that undid Cal, quietly, days later, when he let himself think about it: Gareth Vance, who ran his life on schedules and motorways and the economy of a man who'd never wasted a Sunday, *lingered.* Stayed over. Came to the Monday recovery session and stood at the rail in his good coat watching his son ice-bath and stretch, and afterward took Cal for a breakfast that wasn't about breakfast, the way Webb's coffees weren't about coffee, except his father had no relish in him, only the heavy unpractised effort of a man trying to say a thing his whole life had trained him not to say.

"That second half," his father said, over a fry-up he barely touched. "I keep thinking about it. Where it came from." He turned his fork over. "Your brother had a thing like that. Not in rugby — Jordan couldn't catch a cold, God love him." A rough sound that might have been a laugh, the first time in five years Cal had heard his father say Jordan's name without it being an accusation or a grave. "But he had — a thing. A way of knowing where something was going before it got there. In the music. I never understood it, the music, I thought it was —" the old word rose, *waste*, and Cal watched his father, with visible effort, set it down unsaid for once "— I thought it was a road to nowhere. But he had the *thing*, your brother. And on Saturday, second half, you had it too, and for a minute up in that stand I —" Gareth Vance stopped, and his jaw worked, and he looked out the café window at the wet Manchester street, and said, very low, to the glass and not to his son, "for a minute I thought I was watching Jordan. Same thing. Different game. The knowing-where-it's-going thing. And I didn't know whether to —" he didn't finish. He couldn't. He picked his fork back up. "Anyway. The clubs. Webb says it's serious. It's everything we worked for, son. Don't — just don't lose it."

And Cal sat across from his father with the truth roaring in him — *Dad, you DID watch Jordan, you watch him every time I play, the thing you saw is the thing Jordan taught me in the box room you never once climbed the stairs to, it's the SAME thing, the music and the pass are the same thing, and I make music, Dad, I'm V, the thing you grieve and the thing you're proud of are one thing and I am one person and I've been cutting myself in half for five years so you'd never have to see it* — the truth right there, his father having just, unprecedentedly, *opened the door from his own side*, said Jordan's name with love, named the thing, all but invited it —

And Cal said, "I won't lose it, Dad."

And the window stayed open, and he didn't climb through, again, because his father had cracked the door an inch and an inch was not the same as ready, and because the contract was right there and he could not bear to be, in the same week his father first said Jordan's name with love, the son who turned that into the day he learned the truth. *Soon,* Cal told himself, the coward's word, the word that had cost Jordan two unanswered days. *Soon, on my terms, when the contract's signed, when there's less to lose.* Not knowing the window was already closing faster than his courage was growing, that *soon* was the most expensive word in his vocabulary, that he was doing the exact thing he'd done at sixteen — *I'll reply to the voice note later, it can wait, he's permanent* — and that later, with the people you love, is the one thing that is never, ever guaranteed.

## ◆ vii. The Show Takes Shape

*Ella*

The week after the match, the degree show stopped being a date in June and started being a room with her name on a wall in it, because the course put up the floor plan, and the floor plan was politics made architectural.

Where your work hung was who you were. The wall by the entrance — first thing the externals and the recruiters saw, the wall that set the temperature for everything after — was the prize, and it had gone, unsurprisingly, to Margot Hale, whose immaculate fictional perfumery would greet every lanyard that walked through the door. Ella had been given the back third of the long room, past the textiles, in the spot Dr. Annan described, drily, as "the spot people reach when they've stopped being polite and started being interested," which was either a demotion or a compliment and was, Ella decided, characteristically both.

"It's the better wall, you know," Margot said, finding her at the floor-plan, because Margot found everyone, it was a gift. "The back. People are *tired* by the front wall, they've done their faces, they're performing taste for whoever they came in with. By the time they reach you they've given up performing and they're just — looking. If your stuff's any good, the back wall's where they actually *see* it." She said it generously and it was, Ella realised, genuinely generous, and also a flex — *I got the wall everyone wants and I'm telling you yours is secretly better, which costs me nothing because I'm not worried about you* — and Ella found, to her surprise, that she didn't mind, because Margot had accidentally described the entire thesis of Ella's life. The front wall, where everyone performed. The back wall, where you got seen. She'd spent twenty-one years on the front wall, being the polished impressive version. She'd built her whole project, her whole self this year, on the back wall, on the unperformed real thing.

"Then I'm in the right spot," Ella said. "I've never been any good at the front-wall version of anything."

Margot looked at her, and something flickered — not warmth exactly, but recognition, one talented frightened young woman clocking another. "No," she said. "You're really not. It's annoying. You make the rest of us look like we're trying too hard, which we are, which is the point of all this —" she gestured at the room, the course, the whole apparatus of taste "— and you just — see things. Where did you learn to see like that? It's not taught. I've had the best teaching money can buy and I can't do the thing you do."

And Ella, who could have said *I was raised by a woman who reads a room like a doctor reads a chart,* or *I learned it surviving, the watching is a wound,* or the truest thing, *I learned it the same place a boy I can't tell you about learned to hear the note he doesn't play* — said only, "I never had the best of anything. So I learned to look hard at what was actually there. That's all seeing is. It's just looking at the real thing instead of the impressive one." And Margot nodded slowly, filing it, and they stood at the floor plan a moment longer, rivals and not, the front wall and the back, and Ella understood that Margot Hale was not the enemy of her spring. Margot was just the other answer to the same question — be impressive, or be true — and the externals would choose, in June, which one they hired from, and Ella had bet her whole degree on the back wall, on the real client, on the unperformed thing, and there was no longer any way to hedge it.

## ◆ viii. Twelve Feet

*Ella*

Priya asked Tom to take her twelve feet, and Ella got the report at midnight, Priya bursting into the launderette-warm flat lit up like the front wall at the degree show.

"I did it. After the match. In the car park, freezing, his whole family there, and I just — I said, 'Tom, take me over and introduce me to your mum, properly, as your girlfriend, because if I'm only the fun one I need to know now.'" Priya dropped onto the floor, vibrating, the old delicious energy back but with something steadier under it now. "And Ella — his *face.* You told me to watch his face. It lit up. Like the boiler. He went, 'oh thank God, I've been trying to work out how to do this for weeks, I didn't want to dump my mad family on you, she's *a lot*' — and he grabbed my hand and dragged me over and his mum hugged me like she already knew everything about me, because she *did*, he'd told her *everything*, for months, she knew my coursework and my coffee order and that I make him watch terrible films — he'd been talking about me to his family the whole time, I just wasn't *in the room* for it because he's a thick conflict-avoidant rugby boy who thought introducing me was a bigger deal than it was, so he did nothing and let it read as me not mattering when actually —" she pressed her hands to her face "— actually I mattered the whole time. It was idiocy. The curable kind. You said. You called it."

"I called it," Ella agreed, and felt the warmth of it, and also the small specific ache underneath, because Priya's resolution threw Ella's own situation into hard relief: Priya had been terrified she was the unseen version, and the cure had been *making him say it* — one brave sentence in a cold car park, *take me twelve feet, claim me out loud* — and it had worked, because Tom had nothing to hide, because Tom's only flaw was cowardice about a thing that was already true. And Ella's situation looked identical from the outside and was the exact opposite at the root: she couldn't make Cal claim her in any car park, couldn't ask to be taken twelve feet and introduced, because the thing keeping them in the dark wasn't Cal's conflict-avoidance, it was a locked room and a dead brother and a due-diligence team and a fixer building a reveal. Priya's secret was a misunderstanding. Ella's was a structure. You could fix a misunderstanding with one brave sentence. A structure took something else, something neither she nor Cal had found yet.

"You're doing the quiet thing," Priya said, watching her, sharp under the joy. "The thing where you're happy for me and sad about something you won't say. You've done it all term. I've stopped asking because you always close the door, but I want you to know I *see* you doing it, Ella. Whatever it is. Whoever it is. I see that there's a door and you're standing in front of it, and I'm not going to push, but I'm here for when you can open it. That's all. I'm here twelve feet away whenever you're ready to point at me and say the true thing."

And Ella — who had spent the term rationing this exact friend to protect a secret — felt her throat close, because Priya had just done for her what she'd coached Priya to make Tom do: offered to be the witness, to wait twelve feet away without pushing, to be there for the true thing whenever it could be said. "I know," Ella managed. "I'm sorry I've been a closed door. It's not — it's not about you, I promise, it's the biggest, best, most complicated thing in my life and the second I can tell you, you're the first call, before anyone, I swear it." 

"I know," Priya said, and hugged her, and didn't push, and it was, Ella thought, the second great act of being-seen she'd witnessed that day — first Tom claiming Priya in a car park, now Priya choosing to wait at Ella's locked door without forcing it — and the whole chapter, both cities, kept rhyming the same way: everyone she loved was learning, in their own register, that love is either the thing that lets you be seen or the thing that waits, patiently, twelve feet away, until you can be.

## ◆ ix. The Window

*Cal & Ella*

They talked that Sunday night, in the studio, both their weekends stacked between them like evidence — her sink, his rail, Priya's car park, Webb's *windows close.*

"Everyone's saying the same thing to me," Cal said, at the desk, the buried track low, Jordan climbing and falling. "Webb says tell it before the due-diligence team finds it. Sasha's building a reveal whether I like it or not. Knox turned down ten million people to be a true thing out loud. You stood at a sink and told your whole family the truth and you said it was lighter. And I sat at a rail with my dad's face open for the first time in five years and I *swallowed it.*" He looked at his hands. "I'm the only one not saying the thing. I'm surrounded by people being brave in their own cities and I'm the ghost who won't sign his own homework."

Ella came and sat beside him, and didn't manage him, and put her hand flat on the desk, on his side. "The difference," she said gently, "is that everyone else's true thing is just *theirs.* Priya's car park, Knox's feature, my sink — we risked our own comfort. You're not risking your comfort. You're risking your dad. You think telling the truth means handing your father the thing he believes killed Jordan, on the best week of his year, right when a senior contract makes him proudest. That's not the same as a sink. That's not cowardice. That's a son trying not to be the second loss." She turned his face to her. "So I'm not going to tell you to do it. I keep saying that and I keep meaning it. But I'm going to tell you the thing Webb's too transactional to say and I'm too in-love-with-you to dress up —" the word landed, *in love*, neither of them flinched, it had been true for weeks and was only now in the air "— the window's real. The contract makes your secret valuable, and valuable secrets don't stay yours. So the only choice you actually have left isn't *tell or don't.* It's *tell it yourself, or have it told for you.* That's the whole choice now. And I'd rather watch you do the bravest, most terrifying thing on your own terms than watch it get done to you on someone else's, in the worst order, at the worst time. But it's your window. And your dad. And your name. I'll be there either way. Four rows up. Always."

Cal looked at the one person who'd ever stood in all his rooms at once, and the buried track climbed and fell, and outside the orange rain came down on the city he'd shown her at midnight, and he didn't open the door that night — he wasn't ready, the window was real but his courage wasn't, not yet — but he reached over and laced his fingers through hers on the desk, fully, no gap, the first time he'd held her hand without it being stolen or secret or in the dark of a function, just held it, in the lamplight, in the room where he kept the only true thing about himself, and said: "I love you too. For the record. Since the chip shop, probably. Maybe since the party. I've been the ghost so long I almost couldn't feel it, but it's the realest thing I've got, and I wanted to say it out loud at least once before the window does whatever it's going to do."

It was the most either of them had ever said out loud. It was also, neither of them knew, the last clean night before the catch stopped building and started falling — because the window Webb described and the reveal Sasha was seeding and the offer that was about to arrive for Ella were all, that very Sunday, beginning to move at once, and the next time these two sat in this room it would not be in lamplight with their fingers laced, it would be in the wreckage of a thing detonated on someone else's terms, in the worst order, at the worst possible time, exactly as they'd feared.

But that was the reckoning. On Sunday night there was only the studio, and the orange rain, and two people who'd each been brave in their own city that weekend finally saying the true thing out loud to each other — *I love you, for the record* — in the one room where neither of them had ever had to be anyone but the whole of themselves.

## ◆ x. The First Tremor

*Ella*

The catch announced itself, as catastrophes do, in an inbox, on an ordinary Tuesday, dressed as good news.

Two emails, the same morning, twelve minutes apart, and Ella would think of them later as the two ends of the trap closing — though that Tuesday they looked like the two best things that had ever landed in her life.

The first was from a name she'd had in a reference folder since she was sixteen: **Imogen Clarke-Bayo**, who ran the most respected creative studio in London, who did the campaigns and the brand films and the fashion-house work Ella had been screenshotting for years, and who had — Ella read it three times to be sure the words meant what they said — *seen the Knox identity work, found everything you've made, and would like to meet about a role.* A real one. Not an internship. A seat. *Take the train down, my treat, let's talk about your September.* Ella sat on the launderette floor with the dryers tumbling and felt the want rise so hard and clean it frightened her — the answer to six o'clock, the proof in writing, the thing she'd come to Manchester at eighteen to make possible, arriving in a single email from a woman whose taste she trusted more than her own.

The second email, twelve minutes later, was from Sasha. Cheerful. Cc'ing the label. *Great news — there's real momentum behind a "Who is V?" feature; three outlets are interested and we think a managed reveal in the next few weeks could double the album's reach and make stars of everyone attached, YOU especially, Ella — the eye who built the ghost's world. Let's talk timing. This is the moment.*

And Ella sat very still on the floor in the warm with both emails open, and the cold tenant who lived under all her hopes did not even need to speak this time, because she could see the whole shape of it at once, the way she saw the shape of a campaign before she built it. The two emails were not two pieces of good news. They were one trap with two jaws. Because Imogen's offer — the size of it, the *September*, the seat — would be made enormous by exactly the thing Sasha was describing: the supernova of a "Who is V?" reveal, the album going huge, Ella's name lifted on the back of the story breaking. Everything she wanted was suddenly, sickeningly, *downstream of Cal's exposure.* The bigger her break, the more it required the secret she'd sworn to protect to detonate — and Sasha, who built stories the way Ella built worlds, had arranged it so that Ella's deepest ambition and Cal's deepest fear were now pointed directly at each other, with Ella holding the only trigger that could fire both, and no clean way to want her own future that didn't cost him his.

She didn't tell Cal that night. (Of course she didn't. She gave him one more clean morning. The old reflex, the one she'd promised never to use on him, choosing the familiar shape — *I'll carry this alone so you don't have to feel it yet* — not knowing that the carrying-it-alone was the very fault line the whole thing would crack along.) She closed both emails. She put the phone face down, the way he always did. And she sat in the warm tumbling dark with the trap closed around her future and his, knowing only that the window everyone kept talking about had just slammed from *closing* to *closed*, that the catch had stopped building and started to fall, and that whatever happened next would not happen on anyone's terms but Sasha's — in the worst order, at the worst time, exactly as they had both, all spring, been afraid it would.

The two cities had held, all weekend. Two families, two truths, two brave sentences said in a kitchen and a car park and a lamplit studio. But a third city was coming now — the one made of inboxes and outlets and due-diligence teams and a fixer's smile — and it did not care about brave sentences, and it was already, that Tuesday, on its way.

---

*That was Chapter Three — Two Cities. Two families held up to the light: one that says* show me, *one that says* don't you dare lose it. *A daughter who set her weight down at a sink; a son who carried his to a rail and back; a best friend claimed in a car park; an artist who chose to be a true thing out loud; a rival who named the front wall from the back. The three words said in lamplight — and then, twelve minutes apart, the two emails that close the trap. The catch has stopped building. It is* falling. *Continues in Chapter Four — Reckoning.*
