# Chapter Two — Orbit

> *Long chapter · target ~45,000 words · built progressively. Scenes marked with ◆. Covers the slow burn: deepening collaboration, the secret of Jordan, the no-invoice kindness, the furnace under the lid, and the first kiss. Absorbs and expands old short-chapters 5–8.*

---

## ◆ i. The Argument That Was the Work

*Ella*

By the third week she understood that the arguing was the work, and that no one had ever matched her at it before.

Priya argued to win — to be declared right, to close the matter, arguments as small wars with a victor. Her mother argued to be obeyed, which wasn't arguing at all, it was instruction with the volume of argument. The boys she'd dated had argued to be soothed, every disagreement secretly a request to be told they were good and clever and not in trouble. And Ella, who argued the way she did everything, which was to find out what was *true*, had spent twenty-one years being slightly too much for every room she argued in — too fast, too willing to follow a thought past the point where it flattered anyone, too unbothered by losing a point if losing it got her closer to the real shape of the thing.

Cal argued the same way. That was the discovery. He didn't argue to win; he argued to *test* — he attacked an idea precisely in proportion to how much he respected it, the way you only really push the structures you suspect might hold, and Ella, who had been mistaking respect for combat her whole life because no one had ever shown her the difference, found in him the first person who pushed her ideas hard *because* he thought they were load-bearing.

"The single cover's wrong," he said, on a grey Tuesday, holding the rough she'd left on his desk as a joke — *Produced by V. (the boring answer)* — except now it was a real version, refined, beautiful, the orange and the bled film stock and the rain-ruined type.

"It's not wrong, it's the best thing I've made this year."

"It's the best thing you've made this year and it's wrong." He set it down between them. "It's too resolved. Look at it — it's *finished*. It's a beautiful closed object. And the track isn't closed, the track's a wound that won't scab, the drums never arrive, the whole thing's an open question. You've made a closed cover for an open song. The cover should feel like it got interrupted. Like someone walked in before it was done and the maker didn't get to finish making it nice. *That's* the three a.m. of it. Three a.m. isn't beautiful-and-resolved. Three a.m. is beautiful-and-unfinished, because at three a.m. you've run out of energy to make the feeling presentable."

And Ella opened her mouth to defend it — the reflex, the strong one, the work is good, *I* am good — and stopped, because he was right, and the rightness arrived in her not as a defeat but as a click, the specific click of a thing she'd half-known being said out loud by someone who'd seen it too. "The type," she said slowly. "I kerned it perfectly. I should leave one letter wrong. One. Like the rain got it and I didn't fix it."

"Or like you started fixing it and got called away and never came back."

"Like the maker had somewhere else to be." She was already reaching for the laptop, the want rising, the good want, the work-want. "Like the cover's homesick for a version of itself that had time to be perfect."

"Homesick for now," Cal said quietly, giving her her own phrase back, the one she'd used about his music in the pitch, and they looked at each other across the little distance of the studio, two people who'd just built something truer together in ninety seconds than either had ever built alone, and Ella felt the thing she was not going to name move through the room like a change in cabin pressure, and she went very fast back to the laptop to have somewhere to put her hands.

"You do this thing," she said, to the screen, kerning a letter wrong on purpose, her heart doing the irritating arrhythmic thing. "When something's right and you hear it. You go completely still. Everyone else nods or talks or says 'sick.' You stop. Like you're counting."

"Like I'm counting," he agreed.

"I noticed it at the party. Before I knew it was you, before any of this. You were standing in the loudest room in Fallowfield being the only still thing in it."

"You were the other still thing."

"I was leaving."

"You stayed forty minutes."

"I left at thirty-eight," Ella said. "To spite you. You'd predicted forty and I couldn't give you forty."

He laughed, the real one, the one she'd now heard maybe four times and had started, alarmingly, to collect — to keep a private tally of, the way you hoard a rationed thing — and the laugh filled the studio and the heater hummed and the city did its grey business below and Ella thought, kerning a letter wrong for a cover that was homesick for now: *I am in so much trouble. I have a deadline and a deal and a mother at six o'clock, and I am sitting in a room above a vintage shop collecting a boy's laughter like it's a currency, and the worst part, the genuinely frightening part, is that I have never in my life felt this much like a person and this little like a project manager, and I don't want it to stop, and not wanting it to stop is the most dangerous data I have ever collected on myself.*

She kerned the letter wrong. She saved it. It was the best thing she'd made that year, and now it was also true, and the difference between good and true was, she was learning, a boy three feet away who argued with her because he thought she could hold the weight.

## ◆ ii. Why Styling

*Cal*

He asked her the question on a night that overran, both of them slumped at opposite ends of the battered sofa under the high window, a single lamp on, the city dark and wet and smeared below, the work done and neither of them moving to leave.

"Why styling," Cal said. Not a deflection, not a transition — a real question, the kind he almost never asked, because asking gave something away, because a question is a confession of what you don't know and he'd built a life out of appearing to know everything. "You could run anything. I've watched you in two meetings with Sasha — you could be in any room you wanted, board rooms, the lot. You read people faster than anyone I've ever met. So why this. Why the least safe, least respectable, hardest-to-explain-at-a-dinner version of using that." A pause. "Does your mum know you're doing this instead of — whatever the safe thing was."

"Don't," Ella said. Sharp. Reflex.

He raised a hand, retreating instantly, no defensiveness, just *withdrawn, understood, sorry* — and the cleanness of the retreat was so unlike every man who'd ever pushed her that it undid the sharpness, and she sighed, and softened, and he watched her decide to spend something, the way he'd decided to spend things with her, the ledger between them that had been quietly running since the deal.

"Sorry," she said. "It's a — sore spot. My mum thinks this is a hobby I'm calling a career. She wants me on a graduate scheme. Law, finance, something with a building you can point to and a name the aunties recognise. And the worst thing — the thing that makes it impossible to just be angry at her — is that I understand exactly why." And she told him. The crossing of water. Twice as good and half as visible. The midnight kitchen table when she was nine, her mother not-crying on purpose, her father's hand flat on the table not-touching, the vow she'd flicked like a switch: *I will never be a thing they have to carry.* The twelve years of being the easy one so completely that the seam sealed over. "So when I choose the most visible, least safe thing there is, to her it doesn't look like courage. It looks like I'm taking the high safe ground they bought with their bodies and using it to do the exact precarious creative thing that ruined half the men in our family. It looks like spitting on the climb."

"And to you?"

No one had ever asked her that. Priya cheered her on; her mother grieved her in advance; the course treated it as ambition; no one had asked her what it *was.*

"To me it's the only thing I've ever done," Ella said slowly, the truth arriving even as she said it, surprising her, "where I'm not performing being fine. Everywhere else I'm the strong one. The eldest. The one who's got it handled, who carries, who never gets to fall apart because there's no one underneath her to catch the pieces — there's only siblings looking up. When I'm building a world for a sound, I'm not holding anything up. I'm just *seeing.* It's the only place I get to put the weight down." She looked at him, and made the leap, because he'd made leaps for her. "You'd know. It's what this room is for you. Isn't it. The one place nobody's watching you on a touchline. The one place the —" she almost said *held breath*, and didn't have the phrase yet, that was his, in his head, she only had the shape of it "— the one place you get to stop."

Cal went very still. The counting stillness. She watched him stand at the edge of something and decide whether to step off it.

"Yeah," he said, low. "That's exactly what it is."

And they sat there, two people who'd each spent their whole lives being the version of themselves that other people needed, in the one room where neither of them had to be, and the heater hummed, and the rain came down on the Northern Quarter, and the lamp made a small warm island of them in the dark — and Ella understood that they had just told each other the same secret in two different costumes, the strong one and the ghost, the switch and the held breath, the kitchen table and the box room (though she didn't know about the box room yet, didn't know there *was* a box room, knew only that the shape of his silence had a room in it with a door). And she thought: *we are the same animal. He's the first one of my own kind I've ever met. That's why I can't read him — I can't read him for the same reason I can't see my own face. He's too close. He's the same.*

She stood up. Too fast. "I should go. It's late."

"Yeah." He stood too, fast, and for a second they were close, the way they'd been close at the party, near enough that she could smell the cedar under the studio's coffee and electronics, and the air between them tightened like a held breath about to go.

She left first. She made sure of it. Looking away first was an apology and leaving first was a victory and she needed the victory more than she needed to know what would have happened if she'd stayed. On the stairs that smelled of old paper she stopped, one flight down, out of his sight, and pressed her hand flat against the cold wall and breathed — in for four, out for four, her mother's breath — and above her, through the floor, she heard him start to play something. Not the single. Something newer. Something with a melody in the top of it that she didn't recognise and somehow did, four notes, three climbing and one falling, except now there was a brightness over them she could have sworn hadn't been there before, a warmth laid across the grief, and she stood in the stairwell of the unmarked studio listening to the centre of Callum Vance come down through the floor, and she did not go back up.

But she didn't leave either. Not for a while. And that — the not-leaving, the sitting down on the step — was the most honest thing she'd done in years, and she did it where he couldn't see, which was the only way she knew how to be honest yet: in the dark, on the stairs, with her hand on the cold wall and a melody she half-knew coming down through the boards, letting herself, for the length of one track, simply want the thing she had a deadline and a deal and a mother at six o'clock telling her she couldn't have.

## ◆ iii. Listen to the Bit at the End

*Cal*

He told her about Jordan on a Tuesday, which he had not planned, which he had in fact spent five years planning never to do with anyone, and the way it happened was so small that he didn't see it coming until it had already happened — which was, he'd think later, the only way it ever could have happened, because anything he saw coming he could lock, and the whole point of Jordan was that Jordan was the one thing he could never quite get the door shut on.

They were working late, too late, the session overrunning because Knox had sent eleven voice notes of new ad-libs and Cal was chopping them at the desk while Ella sat cross-legged on the floor three feet away building the inner-sleeve artwork in real time, her laptop throwing blue light up onto her face the way the streetlamp threw orange across the launderette floor she'd never described to him but that he'd somehow built a picture of from the shape of her. And she was humming.

Under her breath. Not to anything. Not to the Knox track, which was in a different key — just a melody, idle, the way people hum when they've forgotten they're not alone, when the work has taken the front of the mind and left the back of it to wander.

Cal's hands stopped on the desk.

"What's that," he said.

"What's what?"

"What you're humming."

Ella looked up, genuinely surprised, not having known she was doing it. "I don't — nothing. It's just a thing. I don't even know where it's — my brother used to —" she frowned, chasing it. "It's nothing. A nursery thing, maybe, from when we were small. Why?"

But Cal had already gone somewhere else, somewhere five years deep, because the melody she'd been humming — idle, to no one, while building the inside of an album sleeve — was four notes that climbed and one that fell. Not the same. She couldn't have known the same; the buried version was pitched and chopped and four feet down where the body finds it before the ear does. But the *shape*. The exact shape. The reluctant rise and the small heartbreak of the drop. The bones of the thing he'd built his whole secret life around, hummed by accident by the one living person he'd let into the room, and the awful intimate coincidence of it — that she'd carried some cousin of his brother's melody in her own body since she was small, that the shape had been waiting in her too, that the centre of gravity had been *in her* the whole time — undid something in Cal that had been holding, white-knuckled, for five years and three months.

"Cal?"

"The voice," he heard himself say. His own voice sounded far away. "In the track. The deep one. The one you keep asking about. You asked who it was."

She closed the laptop. The blue light went out. She didn't say anything. She'd learned, he'd noticed, exactly when not to say anything, which was a skill almost no one had, which was the rarest skill there was — the discipline to let a silence stay open instead of rushing to fill it, to hold the door without pushing on it.

"It's my brother," Cal said. To the desk. To the dark window. To the city tumbling on below, indifferent, full of people each carrying their one unbearable thing. "Jordan. He's — he was. Six years older. He's the reason I make any of this. He used to make beats in a box room when I was a kid and I'd sit on the floor while he did it, where I wasn't allowed, and he was —" Cal's voice was very level, the dangerous level, the level that meant the opposite of what level usually meant "— he was so much better than me, Ella, you have to understand that, I'm good but Jordan *heard things.* He'd play me a kick and say 'listen under it,' and there'd be a whole note that wasn't being played, that your body played for itself in the gap, and he'd watch my face until I got it. 'That's the whole game, little man. Anyone can play you a note. The trick's making them hear the one you didn't play.'" He stopped. Started again, because if he stopped now he'd never. "And my dad's a rugby man. Played second row, broke himself, decided his sons were going to do it properly. And I was good at the rugby, so I got the —" *the nod*, he almost said, and didn't, because the nod was a whole other locked room "— I got picked. The useful son. And Jordan wasn't. Jordan was good at the thing my dad couldn't see the use of. And my dad called it a waste. To his face. For years. *There's no living in that. Look at your brother.* Look at me. The one who picked the nameable thing."

The studio was completely silent. Even the heater seemed to be holding its breath. Ella sat on the floor in the dark, not moving toward him, and he was grateful for it with a gratitude that frightened him, because if she'd crossed the room and held him in that moment he'd have come apart, and instead she did the one exact right thing, the thing almost no one alive knew how to do: she stayed where she was and let the weight of it sit in the room without trying to lift it off him too soon.

"And Jordan didn't fall apart loud," Cal said. "That's the thing people don't understand. He fell apart *quiet.* He got smaller. He stopped playing me stuff. He drove further and further for sessions, chasing the thing the box room couldn't give him and my dad never would — Leeds, Sheffield, once Bristol — tired all the time, they said tired, and one night he was coming back from Leeds, late, the road was wet, and —" Cal looked at his own hands on the desk like they belonged to someone else. "I was sixteen. I was asleep. He'd sent me a voice note at quarter past eleven. 'Listen to this, Cal, listen to the bit at the end, I think I cracked it, tell me I cracked it.' And I didn't —" his throat closed and he made it open "— I was at training, and then I was tired, and then it could wait, because everything could always wait, because I was sixteen and he was permanent. Two days. I didn't listen for two days. And by the time I opened it he'd been gone for —" He didn't finish. There was no end to that sentence that he had ever found.

Ella didn't say *I'm sorry.* She didn't say any of the things people said, the things that made grief about the comfort of the person witnessing it. She said, very quietly, after a silence she let run as long as it needed: "And the buried track. The voice four feet down. That's the voice note."

"It's the last thing he ever sent me. I never replied. So now —" Cal's voice cracked clean down the middle and he let it, in the dark, with her, for the first time in five years "— so now I put a piece of it in everything I make. Buried so deep no one can hear it, so deep it can't be taken, so deep my dad could never find it and be destroyed by it, because if my dad ever knew I was still doing the thing he thinks killed his other son — the day my dad hears my name on a track is the day I lose the only parent I've got left, and I'm not — I lost Jordan, I can't —" He stopped. Breathed. "I've put my brother in a quarter of a million strangers' headphones and not one of them knows they're carrying him. They play him on buses. They fall asleep to him. It's the only grave I could give him that my dad can't visit and ruin and that the world can't take, because the world doesn't even know it's there. That's the secret, Ella. That's the whole secret. I don't hide my name to protect a rugby career. I hide it because my name on that music is a loaded gun pointed at the last of my family, and I have been very, very careful, for five years, never to let anyone near the trigger."

For a long time neither of them spoke. The rain came down. The city carried its millions of unbearable things north and south through the dark.

"Cal," Ella said at last. "Thank you. For trusting me with that. I know what it costs to hand someone a loaded gun and call it trust."

"I didn't decide to. You hummed his melody. It just —"

"You kept going," she said. "After the humming. You didn't have to keep going. That was the decision. The humming was an accident. The rest was you, choosing, in real time, to let one living person listen under the kick." She uncrossed her legs and got up, slowly, and crossed the room — and she didn't hug him, she'd read that right too, she came and stood beside him at the desk and put her hand flat on the wood next to his, not touching, just there, a thing in the world that was on his side, and the gesture was so exactly her mother's hand on the midnight table that she didn't know she was doing it and Cal didn't know where it came from but it reached all the way down. "I'm not going to tell you to put your name on it," she said. "People are always telling me to be louder about who I am, and they mean well, and they don't understand that visibility isn't free, that some of us know to the penny what it costs. You know exactly what it costs. So I'd never. But I'm going to ask you one thing, and you can say no."

"Ask."

"Let me hear it. The actual voice note. Not the buried version — the real one. The bit at the end. The thing he wanted you to listen to."

Cal looked at her. Five years he'd carried the voice note. Five years he'd played the encrypted version, the safe version, the one where Jordan was hidden so deep he was un-loseable and also, finally, unheard. No one had ever asked to hear the actual thing. No one had known there was an actual thing to ask for. She'd reverse-engineered its existence from a melody she'd hummed by accident and a hand that went to a file and stopped, and now she was asking — not to fix it, not to make it about her, not for the story — just to do, five years late, the thing a sixteen-year-old hadn't: to listen.

His hands weren't steady. He got out his phone. He found the thread that said **Jords** with the photo of the stupid face in the car. He found the voice note, 0:47, the little mountain range he knew by heart and hadn't let make a sound in over a year. He looked at Ella once. She nodded — not pushing, just there, the hand on the wood.

He pressed play.

A laugh first. Jordan's laugh, bright and alive and completely unguarded, twenty-two forever. *"Cal, bro — listen, listen to this bit at the end, I think I cracked it, tell me I cracked it, man—"* And then the music. Rough, unfinished, recorded off a monitor in a box room or a hired room in Leeds, a little distorted — four notes that climbed and one that fell, the shape Ella had hummed an hour ago without knowing, raw and unmixed and so full of a person who was certain he had all the time in the world that it filled the studio like weather, like light, like a third presence standing in the room with them in the dark.

It ended. The silence after it was a different silence than any that had come before.

"He cracked it," Ella said softly. Not to Cal. To the room. To the bit at the end. To a boy in a frozen car. "Tell him he cracked it."

And Cal Vance, who had not let another human being watch him cry since he was sixteen years old, who had built an entire self out of stillness precisely so that no one would ever see the rooms inside him, put his head down on the desk in the unmarked studio above the vintage shop — and Ella Savage-Oluwa stood beside him with her hand flat on the wood, not touching, just *there*, on his side, the midnight-table hand, the not-crying breath she'd inherited now spent on letting someone else finally cry — and she did not lift the weight off him too soon, and she did not make it hers, and she did not say a single one of the wrong things.

She just stayed. On the step. In the dark. Listening under the kick.

She left an hour later, and in all that hour neither of them had touched the other, not once, not a hand, not a shoulder — and it was, by a distance that astonished them both, the most intimate night of either of their lives.

## ◆ iv. No Invoice

*Ella*

After the night of the voice note, something changed in the room, and the change was that he started looking after her.

Not loudly. Cal didn't do anything loudly. It arrived sideways, in increments so small that a less observant person would have missed them, and Ella was not a less observant person — she missed nothing, it was her whole function — which meant she caught every single one and had to decide, each time, what to do with the catching.

The first one was food. She'd mentioned, once, weeks ago, in passing, that the Vietnamese place two streets over did a tofu thing she'd think about at night. She'd said it the way you say things you don't expect anyone to file. And then one evening, around the hour her body started running on fumes and bad decisions, there it was on the little table she'd annexed — the tofu thing, from the place two streets over, still hot, no comment, Cal already back at the desk with his back half to her as if it had materialised out of the building. She'd looked at it for a long moment. She'd said, "You didn't have to." He'd said, "I was getting mine." Which was a lie, a small gallant one, because his was a different order entirely and she'd watched him not eat it for an hour, and the lie was the gift inside the gift: he'd built her an exit, a way to receive it without owing, *I was getting mine anyway, it's nothing, don't make it a thing.*

The second one was the heater. The studio was warm because Cal kept it warm now, on by the time she arrived, the cold hundred-year-old air already chased out of the corners, and he never mentioned it and she never thanked him for it because thanking him would have made it a transaction and the entire point — she understood this with a slow, frightening clarity — the entire point was that it *wasn't* a transaction. He was doing the thing she had never once in her life let anyone do. He was taking weight off her without being asked, without keeping score, without the small invoice that she had learned at nine was attached to every kindness, the IOU that turned a gift into a debt and a debt into a way of being owned.

The third one she only noticed because of the deadline.

She'd been stressed about a delivery — the label wanted the full single-rollout assets by the Friday, and a printer had let her down, and she'd been quietly catastrophising about it for two days in the way she catastrophised about everything, privately, managing it, never letting it show — and then on the Thursday the panic she'd budgeted for simply didn't arrive, because the thing was handled, the assets were with the label early, and when she traced back how she found that Cal had made a call. To someone he knew. A printer in Salford who owed him a favour from some music thing, who'd turned it around overnight. He hadn't told her. She found out from the label thanking *her* for the fast turnaround on a job she hadn't known was rescued. And when she'd looked at him across the studio, the question on her face, he'd just shrugged and said, "You'd have sorted it. I just sorted it faster. Saved you the two days of pretending you weren't stressed," and gone back to the desk — and that was the one that undid her, because it meant he'd *seen* the pretending. He'd seen her manage the panic privately, seen the strong-one performance she'd been running since she was nine, the one no one had ever once caught because the whole art of it was that no one caught it — and he hadn't called it out, hadn't made her admit it, hadn't asked for the vulnerability as the price of the help. He'd just quietly removed the thing she was being strong about, sideways, and let her keep her face.

That night she didn't go straight home. She sat in the studio after the work was done, on the battered sofa under the high window, and she said, to the room more than to him, "You're doing a thing."

"I'm mixing."

"You're doing the thing where you look after me and you build me an exit every time so I don't have to feel it. The food's always 'I was getting mine.' The printer was 'you'd have sorted it anyway.' You keep — you keep handing me things with the price torn off." She heard her own voice doing something unfamiliar, going thin at the edges, the strong-one register failing. "I need you to know that I notice. I notice everything, it's the whole problem with me, I've never once in my life *not* noticed. And I don't — I don't know what to do with it. Kindness that doesn't come with an invoice. I've genuinely never had any. Every nice thing anyone's ever done for me came with a balance owing, and I learned to keep the balance at zero by never accepting anything, by being the one who gives so I never owe, and you keep —" she stopped. "You keep zeroing the balance from your side. And I don't have a system for that. It makes me feel like I'm falling."

Cal turned from the desk. The full beam, but soft now, the detachment long gone. He looked at her for a moment in the lamplight with the city dark and wet below, and then he said, carefully, the way he said the things that mattered: "There's no invoice because there's no debt. That's not a system you have to learn. It's the absence of one." A pause. "You spent your whole life keeping the balance at zero by carrying everything yourself. I spent mine making sure nobody could ever leave by being gone before they could. We both built the same thing — a way to never need anyone, dressed up as strength. And I'm so tired, Ella. I'm so tired of mine. I've been tired since I was sixteen. So when I put the heater on before you get here, it's not for you to owe me. It's because for about ninety seconds, doing one small thing to take weight off one person, I'm not running the machine. You're not the only one getting something. That's the part you can't see from inside your system. Letting me take care of you sideways isn't you owing me. It's you letting me put my own weight down for ninety seconds. So you're not falling. We're both just — setting it down. At the same time. In the same room."

The heater hummed. The rain came down on the Northern Quarter. And Ella Savage-Oluwa, who had not let anyone take care of her since she was nine years old in a cold hall, felt the flinch arrive right on schedule — the hand-near-a-burn reflex, the *accepting this makes you a person with needs and you are not, you are the one who carries* — and for the first time in twelve years she watched the flinch arrive and chose, deliberately, with her whole will, not to obey it.

"Okay," she said. Quietly. It came out unsteady. "Okay. Then — thank you for the heater. And I'm not going to say it like it's a transaction. I'm going to say it like —" her voice cracked, clean, one fracture, and she let it, in the lamplight, in front of him, the strong one cracking on purpose for the first time in her adult life "— like a person who's letting someone be kind to her and is terrified, and is doing it anyway."

"That's the bravest thing I've watched you do," Cal said. "And I watched you take a record label apart with a moodboard."

She laughed, wetly, and it broke the unbearable thing into something they could both hold, and she wiped her face once, hard, and said, "Don't tell anyone I cried in your studio, V, I have a reputation," and he said "your reputation's safe, no one even knows I exist," and they sat in the warm dark and the awful tender thing settled between them into something survivable — and Ella understood, walking home later through the orange rain, that the deal was now comprehensively, irretrievably not a transaction, that it had never been a transaction, that she had been lying to herself since the first afternoon, and that the lie was finally, quietly, out of road.

She let herself feel it the whole walk home. The wanting. Undefended, un-folded, enormous. She didn't put it away. She let it walk beside her through the wet glittering city all the way to the launderette door, and only there, hand on the cold handle, did she say to it, gently, not a refusal now but a postponement: *Not yet. Soon, maybe. But not yet. There's still a deadline and a deal and a mother at six o'clock, and he's still got a locked room with his whole family in it, and the timing is catastrophic. But not never. For the first time — not never.*

It was the closest she had come, since she was nine, to letting herself have something. And the city, beautiful and slightly ruined, orange and wet and indifferent, witnessed it the way it witnessed everything: without comment, and without ever once looking away.

## ◆ v. The Furnace Under the Lid

*Cal*

The problem with finally wanting something, Cal discovered, was that it did not arrive alone. It dragged the rest of him in behind it — the locked rooms, the held breath, the five years of intensity he'd poured into a sport and a sound precisely so it would have somewhere to go that wasn't a person. He had thought he was a cool man. He had built a cool man, carefully, over years, ten thousand reps of stillness. It turned out the cool man was a lid, and Ella Savage-Oluwa had eased it off without trying, and underneath it Cal Vance was not cool at all. Underneath, Cal Vance was a furnace, and now the furnace had a direction, and the direction was a person, and that frightened him more than anything Sasha or his father or the leak-that-hadn't-happened-yet could do.

He found out how much at a thing of Knox's — a low-key album playback in a bar off Stevenson Square, industry only, the kind of room Sasha curated and worked like a croupier. Ella was there in her professional capacity, and she was *good* at it, better than good; she moved through the industry crowd like she'd been doing it a decade, reading every room within the room, and Cal stood in his corner being V, the silent magic no one could place, and watched a man he half-recognised — Declan, a manager, older, the practised charm of someone who'd learned women were a category of opportunity — put his hand flat on the small of Ella's back to steer her toward someone.

Cal's vision did a thing he did not like. It went very clear and very narrow — the counting stillness, except not calm at all, the opposite of calm, the stillness of a held thing about to stop being held. He was three steps across the room before he'd decided to move, and he caught himself, hard, mid-stride, and stopped, and stood there with his heart doing something violent and stupid, appalled at himself, because he recognised the feeling. He'd grown up inside it. It was his father's thing — intensity that confused itself with ownership, love that arrived as control, the grip on the shoulder that ran a whole life. *I poured it into the wrong place,* he thought, frozen between his corner and her, *and now it's pointed at a person and it wants to manage her, protect her, decide things for her — it wants to be my dad, and she is the last woman alive who would survive being managed.*

Ella handled it herself, of course. She stepped neatly out from under Declan's hand without seeming to, redirected the whole interaction, kept her smile, gave the man precisely nothing — she had never needed rescuing, would have been *insulted* by rescuing, the rescuing would have been an act of war against the entire self she'd built. Cal watched her not need him and felt two things at once, and the order they arrived in mattered: first the ugly thing, the disappointment that she hadn't needed him; and then, right behind it, the better thing, the relief, the *good, she's exactly who I thought she was, she doesn't need a bodyguard, thank God, because I would make a terrible one and a worse cage.*

She found him in his corner later. She read his face the way she read everything.

"You went somewhere," she said. Low. Even. No anger in it, which was worse than anger, because it meant she'd thought about this before it happened, had it ready. "When Declan put his hand on my back. I watched you take three steps and stop."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, and I want to be really clear about one thing, because it's the thing that ends us if we get it wrong." She kept her voice down, steady, and there was no heat in it, only a hard-won clarity. "I will not be a thing you protect. I've spent my whole life being the strong one, and I am not trading one kind of carrying for another. If you go full caveman every time a man stands near me — if you decide what I can handle and manage me for my own good — this is over, and I'll mean it, because I've watched that movie my whole life with the genders the other way round and I know how it ends. I need a partner. Not a bodyguard. And definitely not an owner."

It landed exactly where it should have, in the part of him that was his father's son. He made himself breathe. The narrow vision widened back out.

"You're right," he said. "I'm sorry. And I need to tell you the true version, because you'll find it anyway." He looked at her. "When I want something, I want it like a problem I have to solve. All the way in. There's no setting between off and everything — it's how I am with the rugby, with the music, with — I go to the bottom of things. And I've never once pointed it at a person, because I watched what it did to my dad, the way his went bad, turned into a grip, a touchline, a project he made out of his own kids. So I don't actually know how to aim this at you without it becoming the thing you just described. I'll get it wrong. Not because I don't respect you — because I've never done it before and the only model I had for it is the man I'm most afraid of becoming." A pause, and then the real offer, the only one he could honestly make. "So here's the deal I can actually keep. Not that I'll never get it wrong. I will. The deal is: you tell me when I do, and I listen, every time, and I put it down. Corrigible. That's the promise. Not perfect. Correctable."

Ella looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face softened, because that — she'd tell him later — was the exact right thing, the realer thing: not a vow to be flawless, which is a lie everyone breaks, but a vow to be *correctable*, which is the only promise between two people that's worth anything, because it's the only one a human can actually honour.

"Okay," she said. "That's a deal I'll take." And then, because she could never let a moment stay heavy, because the strong one always reached for the joke: "Caveman."

"I didn't even do anything. I took three steps."

"You took three steps and made a *face.* You've got a face, Cal. It said, 'I have considered four ways to remove this man's hand and I'm selecting the most merciful.'"

"Three ways," Cal said. "I'm not as far gone as you think."

"You're exactly as far gone as I think," Ella said, "which is the problem, and also, God help me, the appeal," and she went back into the room to finish working it, and he let her, and that was the lesson, the whole lesson of those weeks compressed into one bar room: the furnace did not get to be a leash. He could feel the heat and not feed it into another person. It was the hardest rep he had ever attempted, harder than the flat pass, and he practised it the same way — ten thousand times, getting it wrong, being told, putting it down — and slowly, over the spring, under her steady unsentimental correction, the furnace started to learn the one thing his father's never had: how to be warmth instead of fire. How to heat a room instead of burning down the person standing in it.

## ◆ v-b. The Competition

*Ella*

The thing about the project being real was that it had to coexist, in Ella's actual life, with the thing that was supposed to be unreal — the degree, the course, the cohort, the looming degree show in June that the real project was technically *for* — and the two did not always sit comfortably in the same week, because the course had its own gravity and its own politics and, in the specific shape of Margot Hale, its own weather.

Margot Hale was the best in the year, or had been until Ella, and she had the kind of confidence that comes pre-installed: father something in advertising, a childhood of private views and the right names dropped without weight, an instinct for taste that was genuine — Ella would never pretend it wasn't, that was the whole problem, Margot was *good* — and entirely untested by anything resembling consequence. For her final project Margot was building a fictional luxury brand, a heritage perfumery invented whole, and it was immaculate: the imagined provenance, the bottle, the campaign, every serif chosen with a jeweller's precision. It was the safe choice, the controllable one, the closed system Ella had rejected in September as cowardice — and in the bright cruel light of the studio crit, with the assessors-who-mattered coming in June, it did not look like cowardice. It looked like polish. It looked like someone who would never, ever fail in public, because she had arranged her entire project so that public failure was structurally impossible.

"A *real* client," Margot said, at the crit, when Ella presented the Knox work — and she said *real* the way Ella's mother said *styling*, with tongs. "How brave. Aren't you terrified, though? You can't *control* a real artist. He could put out a terrible second single and tank your whole identity through no fault of your own. With a fictional brand at least the only variable is you." A smile, warm, lethal, the smile of someone who had identified the exact load-bearing fear and pressed it gently, just to watch. "I admire it. I could never. I like to know I'm being marked on my work and not on someone else's luck."

And the worst of it — the thing that kept Ella up, the thing she'd never say to Priya — was that Margot was *right* about the risk. Ella had bet her degree on a living artist and a real release, on a world she didn't fully control, and if Knox stumbled, if the project soured, if the second single underdelivered, Ella's final project failed in public in a way Margot's perfumery never could. She'd called fiction cowardice and reality courage, and Margot had just calmly pointed out that the line between courage and recklessness is drawn entirely after the fact, by whether it worked.

"I'd rather be marked on something that's actually at stake," Ella said, holding her voice level, her mother's breath, "than something that can't lose because it can't do anything. Your perfumery's beautiful, Margot. It's also never going to be smelled by a single human being. Mine's going to be on a billboard. I'll take the risk."

It was a good line and Margot's eyes flickered, because it landed, but walking home Ella knew the line was armour and the fear was real, and the fear was this: that she'd staked everything — the degree, the future, the argument with her mother she'd half-won at a sink — on a project whose centre of gravity was a secret she was sleeping beside, a ghost behind the boards whose whole life was one leaked session photo from collapse. Margot's only variable was Margot. Ella's variables included a fly-half, a dead brother, a fixer with no eyes in her smile, and a story that wanted out. If any of those moved wrong, the most ambitious final project on the course became its most public catastrophe.

Dr. Annan, the course leader, caught her on the way out — dry, exacting, impossible to read, the kind of tutor whose praise you learned to detect only in the absence of criticism. "Savage-Oluwa. The Knox work." A pause that Ella couldn't fill because she didn't know which way it ran. "The externals asked about you. By name. Twice." Annan let that sit, and almost — almost — smiled. "Margot's perfumery is the best fictional project I've seen in five years. Yours is the only *real* one in the cohort. The externals can hire from polish or they can hire from nerve. In my experience, polish gets the first job and nerve gets the career. Don't tank it. Keep your artist on the rails." And she was gone, leaving Ella to walk home through the orange rain having been, in the same five minutes, threatened by her rival and quietly knighted by her tutor, carrying both, the way she carried everything — except now there was a warm room three miles north where she could set even this down, sideways, with someone who understood exactly what it was to bet your whole self on a thing you couldn't fully control.

## ◆ v-c. The Feature

*Cal*

The label wanted Knox to do a feature, and the feature was wrong, and Cal knew it was wrong the way he knew a pass was going to be intercepted before it left his hands.

It came down through Sasha, the way everything came down through Sasha, smooth and reasonable and framed as opportunity: there was a pop act, a big one, a streaming monster with a sound like sugar dissolving, and they wanted Knox on a crossover single — a hook, a verse, a moment — and it would, Sasha explained, dropping onto the studio sofa, "put Marcus in front of ten million people who've never heard of him. One song. It doesn't change the album. It just *opens the door.*"

"It changes the album," Cal said, from the desk. "It changes what the album *means*. You can't put Knox next to that sound and then ask people to take the three-a.m. stuff seriously. You'd be teaching the new audience that he's the sugar guy, and then the real record lands and it's nobody's idea of who he is. You don't open a door by walking through the wrong one."

"V's a purist," Sasha said, to the room, fondly, the way you'd describe a charming flaw. "Purists make incredible records and small careers. I've watched a hundred of them. The graveyard's full of artists who were too good to be heard." She turned to Knox, who was sprawled on the sofa pretending to be asleep, which was how Knox handled decisions he didn't want to make. "Marcus. Ten million people. That's not a sellout, that's a *staircase.* You do the feature, the feature pops, suddenly the album's got an audience instead of a cult. You can be pure later. Pure's a thing you can afford after you're big. It's a thing that costs you everything before."

And Cal watched Knox — generous, chaotic, brilliant Knox, who'd known Cal's real name since before either of them was anyone, who'd built the whole project on the V sound on a handshake and a shared faith — sit up slowly, and Cal saw the temptation move across his face, real and human and not at all stupid, because Sasha wasn't wrong, that was the awful thing, Sasha was never wrong about the mechanics, only about the cost. Ten million people. A staircase. The thing every artist in the city was clawing for. And weighed against it: a sound. A *feeling.* A three-a.m. ache with a dead boy buried four feet down in it that nobody knew was there.

"What do you think," Knox said. To Cal. Not to Sasha. The way he always did, the handshake holding.

Cal could have told him what to do. He didn't. Because that was the thing he'd learned from a girl who walked away from a handle — you don't get to open another person's door for them. "I think," Cal said carefully, "that the sugar guys are interchangeable and there's only one of you. I think you could have ten million people who think you're someone else, or a hundred thousand who know exactly who you are, and the first number's bigger and the second one's *yours.* And I think —" he met Knox's eyes "— that you already know which one you want, or you wouldn't be pretending to be asleep about it. But it's your name on it, man. Not mine. Mine's never on anything. So it's your call, and I'll make whichever one you choose sound like the best decision you ever made, because that's the job. But you asked what I think, so: don't take the staircase that leaves you at the top as someone you're not. There's no climbing back down from that one."

Sasha looked at Cal for a long moment, and the smile stayed warm and the eyes did the inventory, and Cal understood that he'd just made an enemy of the most dangerous person in the room — not because he'd won, but because he'd reminded Knox that there was a version of his career that didn't run through Sasha, and Sasha's whole power was being the only door. "Sleep on it, Marcus," she said, rising, gathering the coat. "Doors don't stay open." And at the studio door, the last to leave, she paused, and looked back at Cal with something almost like respect and entirely like warning. "You really would rather be a secret than be wrong, wouldn't you. Both of you." Her eyes did one slow sweep of the room — the second mug, the heater, the corner table that was Ella's. "It's romantic. It's also how people stay small. Think about that too." And she was gone, and the thread she'd pulled — *is staying true the same as staying small?* — hung in the studio long after her heels had faded down the stairs that smelled of old paper, because it was the one question neither Cal nor Ella had a clean answer to, the question the whole spring was secretly about.

## ◆ vi. The Wrong Film Stock

*Ella*

The shoot for the first single happened in a railway arch in Mayfield that someone knew someone to get the keys to, and it ran from nine at night until the sky over the city went the colour of a bruise healing, and it was, Ella would think later, the night her world stopped being a deck on a laptop and started being a thing that existed in the air.

She'd built it and now she got to watch it come true, which was a feeling she would chase for the rest of her life and never fully catch again, because the first time is the only first time. The wrong film stock — she'd insisted, against the DoP's instinct, expired stock that bled the colour. The orange streetlight, achieved with gels and a borrowed sodium lamp and a fog machine that the arch's damp turned into something better than fog, a genuine haze that made everyone look like a memory of themselves. Knox at the centre of it, electric, doing take after take, the camera loving him because she'd told him to stand the way the sound stood — reluctant, half-turned, like he had somewhere else to be — and the whole identity she'd promised the label assembling itself in real time, three feet in front of her, beautiful and slightly ruined and exactly, precisely true.

Cal wasn't supposed to be there. Producers weren't, usually, and a producer who was secretly a fly-half whose face couldn't be in a single frame definitely wasn't — but he came anyway, late, hood up, hanging back in the dark beyond the lights, and Ella clocked him the second he arrived because she'd developed, to her private alarm, an instinct for it. The room would reorganise and she'd know, before she turned around, that he'd walked into it. She didn't go to him. She couldn't; she was working, she was the creative director, forty people were looking to her. But she felt him there in the dark watching her world come true, and she understood that he'd come to watch *her* have the thing — that he'd risked the location and the eyes to stand in the dark and witness the one night she got to be undeniably, publicly good at the thing she was — and the witnessing was its own kind of gift with the price torn off.

Sasha materialised at her shoulder during a reset, two coffees, handing her one. Sasha did this — appeared, gave you something, watched what you did with it. "Your mysterious producer's here," she murmured, pleasant, her eyes doing the slow inventory of the dark beyond the lights. "V. He never comes to shoots. Two years and I've never once got him in the same room as a camera." A sip. "Funny thing about a producer that good with no face. Whole scene wants to know who he is. That's not shyness — shyness doesn't make a record like the single. That's a *strategy*, and a strategy that good has a story under it, and stories are my whole job." She let it sit, warm, lethal. "If you ever found out who he is — and you're in that studio more than anyone — and you wanted to tell me, off the record, it would be very, very good for you, Ella. The eye who unmasked the ghost. That's not a styling credit. That's a launch. That's the difference between getting hired and being *wanted.*"

And there it was, laid on the table as smoothly as Ella had laid Cal's own moves out at the party — the first real probe, the shape of the cliff visible now through the haze. Ella kept her face exactly where it was, the professionalism welded on, her mother's breath in her chest, and felt the cold settle in her stomach that she would later recognise as the first clear sight of what was coming.

"I genuinely don't know who he is," Ella said. "He's a closed door. I make the outside of it look nice. That's the whole arrangement." A lie now, fully a lie, load-bearing, and she watched Sasha receive it.

"Sure," Sasha said, and smiled, and the smile said *I don't believe you and I respect the loyalty and I will be here when it gets expensive,* and she drifted off to do inventory on someone else, and Ella stood holding a coffee she hadn't asked for, in the haze and the orange light, in the middle of the most beautiful thing she'd ever made, and understood that the danger in this project had a name and the name was not Cal's father. The name was the story. And the story wanted out, the way every secret she'd ever met wanted out, the way she'd said it herself, in the pitch, weeks ago, not knowing she was describing her own spring: *like a secret that got out.*

She found him in the dark beyond the lights when the wrap was called and the crew started breaking down the world she'd built, coiling the cables, killing the sodium lamp so that the arch went back to being just an arch.

"Your fixer just offered to make me famous," she said, low, "if I tell her who you are."

He didn't react the way she expected. He didn't tense or pale. He turned his head and looked at her, the counting stillness, and said — evenly, the dangerous level — "Are you going to?"

"No."

"Why not? She's right. It'd be a launch. The eye who unmasked the ghost. You'd never have to find a brand again — they'd come to you, the rest of your life." His voice was perfectly level and she realised, with a lurch, that he was *testing* it, the door he'd given her the key to, finding out at last whether she'd open it the second it became useful, whether the *useful* she'd claimed on the first afternoon was the truth or the posture. "You told me you're only keeping it because it's useful. Well — it just stopped being useful and started being expensive. Sasha's going to ask again, and louder, and eventually someone offers you the thing you've wanted your whole life in exchange for eight words. So. Are you going to."

Ella looked at him in the dying orange light, at the boy who'd told her about Jordan with his head on the desk, who'd built his brother a grave out of buried sound, who'd handed her the one key that could destroy him and then spent weeks watching to see if she'd use it — and she decided to stop lying. To him, and to herself, in the same breath.

"Cal," she said. "I lied to you. The first afternoon. When I said I was only keeping the secret because it was useful."

Something moved across his face.

"I kept it because you told me the true thing instead of the brand answer," she said. "At the party you knew I'd see through you and you came over anyway, and in the studio you handed me a loaded gun and called it trust, and that's the realest thing anyone's done for me in about twelve years, and I've been pretending it didn't land because landing is dangerous, because I've got a deadline and a deal and a mother who calls at six o'clock, and I cannot afford —" her voice did the thing and she let it "— I cannot afford to want a thing I can't have. I've spent my whole life not wanting things so I could carry the things I had to. And you are the single most inconvenient thing that has ever happened to me, Callum Vance, and I'm not going to tell Sasha who you are, not because it isn't useful — because it *is* useful, that's the whole point, the useful thing and the right thing are finally pointing opposite directions and I'm choosing the other one — I'm choosing *you*, your secret over my launch, and that is the most terrifying sentence I have ever said out loud, and I'd like it on the record that I'm furious about it."

The arch had gone quiet around them, the crew packing the last of the world into flight cases, the haze thinning. Cal looked at her for a long moment in the dark.

"Come outside," he said.

## ◆ vii. The Chip-Shop Step

*Ella*

The street outside the arch was empty and wet and the rain had stopped and the air smelled of iron and the cold spine of the city, and they walked without deciding to, away from the lights, past the dark hulk of the old station, and neither of them said anything for a while, and the not-saying was the loudest thing either of them had done all night.

"I've never told anyone about Jordan," Cal said eventually. "Not Tom. Not Webb. Not in five years. And I told you because you hummed four notes by accident. I keep trying to work out why it was you, and I think it's because you're the only person I've ever met who's also two people — the one everyone sees and the one carrying it. You knew where my door was because you've got the same door. I didn't let you in. You just already had the key, because it's the same key. It's the same lock."

"I know," Ella said. "I clocked it at the party. Before I knew your name. You were the only other still thing in the loudest room and I thought, *that one's like me, that one watches because being watched is worse.*"

He stopped walking. They were under a streetlight — the orange, the exact orange she'd built two whole worlds out of now, the campaign and the night — and it occurred to her that she'd specified this colour for the shoot because somewhere underneath she'd already known it was the colour of this, of him, of the thing she wasn't letting herself want. "I'm not good at this," Cal said. "Whatever this is. I read rooms, I read odds, I don't know how to do a thing I can't calculate. And I can't calculate you. I've tried. I've run it every way. You keep coming out as the same answer and the answer doesn't make sense for a man in my position."

"Which is?"

"Worth it." He said it like a man stepping off a high thing into the dark. "You keep coming out as worth it. The deal, the project, your deadline, your mum, Sasha, my dad, the leak that's going to come someday because secrets always get out — I've run all of it, all the catastrophic odds, and the answer's still worth it. I've never had a number do that before. Worth-it is not a number I trust. And it won't go away."

There was a chip shop three doors down — the only lit thing on the street, open absurdly late, the kind of place that exists entirely to catch people at exactly the moment Ella and Cal were having, two people who'd run out of road on a thing too big to say standing up. Later neither of them could fully explain why they ended up in it, except that the moment had become too large and they were both people who'd learned to survive large moments by getting smaller and more practical — so they bought chips. Chips at three in the morning, in polystyrene, drowned in vinegar, and they sat on the cold step outside while the city's last empty buses went past gold and lit and carrying no one.

"This is so romantic," Ella said. "Vinegar. A bin. The actual bins of Mayfield."

"You built a whole campaign out of beautiful-and-slightly-ruined," Cal said. "I'm just doing product testing. Quality control on your own brief."

She laughed, properly, the unmanaged one, the one that got loose maybe twice a year and had got loose, she'd noticed, far more than twice since February — and he watched her do it with the counting stillness, and when she turned to say something else he was very close, closer than the step required, and the laugh died in her throat, not unpleasantly, the way a held breath dies when you finally decide to let it go.

"I'm going to kiss you," Cal said, "unless you tell me not to. And I want it on the record — since you're so fond of the record — that I've run the odds on this, properly, all of them, and they're terrible. Your deadline. The project. Sasha doing inventory. My dad. The leak. The fact that the second this is real it's a thing that can be taken from both of us. The odds are catastrophic."

"They're apocalyptic," Ella agreed, not moving away.

"Apocalyptic. So I need you to know I'm not doing this because it's a good bet." His eyes did the thing, the full beam, the measuring that had stopped being detached somewhere back in February. "I'm doing it because it's the only thing I've wanted in five years that's just for me and not for anyone watching — no touchline, no streams, no nod to earn. Just this. Just you. You can decide what that tells you."

And Ella Savage-Oluwa — who had folded the wanting-part away at nine in a cold hall, who had spent twelve years making sure she was never a thing anyone had to carry, who had said *not yet, soon maybe, not never* to herself at a launderette door and meant the *not never* more than she'd meant anything — closed the distance herself. Because looking away first was an apology, and waiting to be kissed was a kind of looking away, a kind of letting someone else cross the room, and she was done apologising for wanting things, and she was done being the one who waited to be reached.

"It tells me," she said, against his mouth, "that you finally stopped reading the room."

She kissed him on a chip-shop step in Mayfield at three in the morning — vinegar and cold and the iron smell of the city, under the exact orange light she'd built a whole world out of — and the kiss was not careful, and it was not calculated, and somewhere behind them the last gold bus went past empty, and for once in both their lives neither of them was performing being fine, because they were not fine, they were something far more dangerous and far better than fine. For the length of that kiss neither of them cared who was watching, because for the first time in either of their lives the answer was *no one* — no touchline, no camera, no aunties, no scouts, no Sasha, no father, no quarter of a million strangers — and the only person who could see them was the only person either of them had ever actually wanted to be seen by.

When they finally broke apart she kept her forehead against his, and the chips had gone cold, and she said, "The timing is catastrophic."

"Apocalyptic," he corrected.

"We're going to regret this so much."

"Probably." His arm came round her on the cold step and she let it, she let it without flinching, the burn-near-a-hand reflex arriving and being, for once, simply overruled. "But the catch can have everything else. The deadline, the deal, the leak, all of it — the catch can have the lot. It's not having tonight." He pressed his mouth to her hair. "It's not having three a.m. on a chip-shop step. That one's ours. I'm taking that one off the table before the catch even gets here."

And Ella sat on the cold step in the orange light with cold chips and a warm boy and the whole apocalyptic future stacked up somewhere past the end of the street, and let herself have the one thing, the tonight, the three a.m., un-folded, undefended, hers — and it was, by a distance she couldn't measure, the bravest and most reckless and most alive she had ever, in twenty-one carefully managed years, allowed herself to be.

The city, beautiful and slightly ruined, witnessed it without comment. The last bus was gone. There would not be another till morning. Neither of them moved to find one.

## ◆ vii-b. The Sweepstake

*Ella*

The secret cost her Priya, slowly, in a way she didn't see happening until it had nearly happened, which was the cruellest kind of cost, the kind you only invoice in arrears.

It started as comedy. Tom's V-sweepstake had grown — forty quid, then sixty, then a shared spreadsheet with columns and a closing date — and Priya, who found the whole thing delicious, had appointed herself lead investigator, on the entirely correct theory that her flatmate spent more hours with the mystery producer than anyone alive. "You're my inside woman," she announced, one night, cross-legged on the front-room floor in the launderette-warm dark, brandishing cereal. "Tom's promised me half his winnings and I've promised him we'll split it three ways with you, which is generous, because you're doing all the actual spying. So. V. Give me *something.* Tall? Short? Old? Does he have a tragic past? I've decided he has a tragic past, it's the only thing that explains the sound."

And Ella — who knew exactly how tall he was, knew the tragic past to its four-note centre, knew the shape of his hands and the cedar smell of him and the way he held a bottle he wasn't drinking — looked at her best friend's open, delighted, scheming, trusting face, and lied.

"He's a closed door, Pri. I've told you. I work in the corner, he works at the desk, he barely speaks. I genuinely couldn't pick him out of a lineup." A lie now stacked on weeks of lies, load-bearing, structural, and she felt the whole pack of them shift on her back. "Sorry. I know it's boring. There's no tragic past. There's just a man with a laptop and a hood."

"You're the worst spy in history," Priya said, fond, returning to her cereal, and let it go — and that was the thing, that was the splinter: Priya let it go *every time*, trustingly, because it had never once occurred to her that Ella would lie to her, because in three years Ella never had, and so Priya took each closed-door deflection at face value and moved on, never knowing she was being handled by an expert, never knowing the friend she told everything to had built a locked room and put Priya on the outside of it.

But friendships are not maintained by the big confidences. They're maintained by the small ongoing ones, the daily traffic of *guess what* and *you'll never believe* and *can I tell you something*, and Ella had started, without deciding to, rationing that traffic — because so much of her life now ran through the secret that she couldn't tell, and a person who's editing half their life in real time gets quiet in ways that read, from the outside, as distance. She stopped initiating. She deflected the *how's the studio* questions a beat too smoothly. She was tired in the evenings from a happiness she couldn't name, and the tiredness looked, to Priya, like withholding, which it was, though not the kind Priya feared.

It came to a head over Tom.

Because Priya had her own thing going wrong, that spring, and needed her best friend, and her best friend was three-quarters elsewhere. "He's never going to introduce me to his family," Priya said one night, not delicious now, small, the cereal abandoned. "We've been together five months and I've met every single one of the lads but I've never met his mum, and when I say it he goes 'ah she's mad, you don't want to meet her,' and laughs, and changes it, and the thing is, Ella — I think I'm the fun one. I think I'm the girlfriend he has at uni and there's a different, *serious* version of his life that I'm not in. The rugby's serious. His family's serious. His future's serious. And I'm the fun. I'm the laugh. I'm not — I don't think he sees me as a *person* he's going to keep. I think he sees me as a brilliant time he's going to have until the serious life starts." Her voice wobbled, and Priya never wobbled, Priya was the confident one the way Ella was the strong one, and the wobble was Priya doing the thing Ella had done at the sink in Lewisham — cracking the surface to show what was under it. "Does that sound mad? Tell me that sounds mad."

And Ella — who knew everything about being kept as a version, who'd spent her whole life as the strong-one version and was only now learning to be a whole person in a warm room three miles north — should have been all the way there for this. It was her exact territory. Being seen versus being a useful or pleasing version of yourself; the fear that the people who loved you loved the performance. She *was* the expert. But she was tired, and three-quarters elsewhere, and guarding her own secret, and so she gave Priya the slightly-too-quick reassurance, the *of course he sees you, you're being mad, he's just rubbish at this, all the lads are* — the friendship equivalent of the closed-door deflection — and she watched, too late, Priya's face do the small thing it did when she'd been handled instead of heard, the same thing Ella's own face must have done a hundred times, and Priya said "yeah, you're probably right," and picked the cereal back up, and the conversation closed, unfinished, the way Ella's used to close at her own family table.

Ella lay awake that night doing the arithmetic. *The secret is costing me Priya. Not in a blow-up — in a slow leak. I'm so busy guarding the thing I can't say that I've stopped saying the things I can. I'm rationing my best friend to protect a boy she doesn't know exists. And the worst part is I just did to her the exact thing I'm afraid Cal's dad does to him, the exact thing I'm afraid the world does to me — I gave her the reassuring version instead of the true seeing. She needed me to say "no, that's not mad, that's the realest fear there is, I know it from the inside, let's actually look at it" — and I gave her "you're being silly" because I didn't have the bandwidth, because my bandwidth's all spent on the locked room.* She turned over. The dryers tumbled. *Sasha asked if staying true is the same as staying small. Here's a worse question: is keeping Cal's secret making me a worse friend? Is the price of one person being fully known by me that everyone else gets a little less of the truth?*

She didn't have an answer. She had a deadline and a deal and a mother at six o'clock and now a best friend going quietly unseen three feet away while Ella guarded a ghost — and the B-side of the great secret love of her life was turning out to be a slow accumulating debt to everyone she wasn't allowed to tell, and the debt, unlike Cal's kindnesses, came with an invoice, and the invoice was just going to sit there, growing, until the day the secret got out and everyone found out at once exactly how long and how completely the strong, honest, reliable Ella Savage-Oluwa had been editing her own life.

She resolved to do better by Priya. She meant it. She would, eventually — but not before the cost compounded, because that was the nature of the thing she and Cal had built: a room so warm and so true that being in it made the whole rest of the world a fraction colder and falser, and neither of them had yet learned that a love which costs everyone else the truth is a love with a leak in it, and that leaks, like secrets, always, always find their way out.

## ◆ viii. The Quietest He Had Ever Been

*Cal*

They didn't tell anyone. That was the first decision, made silently and mutually on the walk home from the chip shop as the sky went from bruise to grey, and it was the right one and it was also the thing that would eventually cost them, though neither could have known that yet. If the scene found out V was seeing the project's art director, it became gossip — became a story, became exactly the loose thread Sasha was already pulling at. And if the rugby world found out Cal was *anything* in the music scene, the whole edifice came down: the contract, the father, the locked room with the loaded gun in it. So they were careful. They were so careful. And the carefulness, perversely, made it more — every stolen hour in the unmarked studio more charged, every glance across an industry room more loaded, every late walk through the wet city more *theirs* precisely because no one else could have it, or even know it existed to be had.

Here was the thing Cal had not expected, the thing that undid the cool man completely: a relationship conducted in secret belongs entirely to the two people in it. A public thing belongs partly to everyone who sees it — to the friends with opinions, the family who weigh in, the city that files you under *couple* and hands you a script. Theirs belonged to no one. It existed only in one unmarked room above a vintage shop and in the gaps between two impossible schedules, undiluted, uncommented-on, unwitnessed — and Cal, who had spent his entire life being watched, who had never once been in a room without performing for it, discovered that the secret was the first space he'd ever occupied where the static simply switched off. He had thought the studio did that. He'd been wrong, the way he'd been wrong about so much; it had never been the room. It had been being unwatched. And now there was a person in the unwatched space with him, the most watching person he'd ever met, and the static still stayed off — better than off, it inverted, it became its opposite, a quiet so deep he could hear himself think for the first time since he was sixteen.

He learned her. This was the part that finished him.

He learned that she ran on three hours' sleep when she was building something and then crashed for twelve, that her body kept the books even when her will refused to. He learned that she called her mother every evening at six and put on a voice for it, a particular daughter-voice, and was wrung out for an hour afterward, sitting very still, and that the best thing he could do in that hour was nothing, just be in the room, warm, undemanding, the heater on. He learned that she'd been the strong one so long she didn't know how to be looked after and flinched from it like a hand near a flame — so he learned to do it sideways, the no-invoice way, the food that appeared, the deadline quietly rescued, the weight lifted before she'd announced she was carrying it. Never named. Never a debt. Just the weight, briefly, off — the way she'd taken the weight of Jordan off him that first night without trying to lift it too soon.

And she learned him, which was harder, because there was more locked away and the locks were older. She learned that the stillness was a held breath and not a calm. She learned that when his vision went narrow she was to name it, plainly, and that he would put it down — that the furnace was real and that he was, against everything his father had modelled, genuinely trying to make it warmth. She learned the four notes were everywhere once you knew to listen, and that he'd started, since the night of the voice note, to lay a *fifth* thing over them in the newest tracks, a brightness, a warmth across the grief, and that she was the brightness, that he'd put her in the top of his brother's melody without quite meaning to, the living over the lost, and that this was the most enormous thing he had ever done and he could not say it out loud so he said it in the only language he and Jordan had ever fully shared, four feet down, in the gap, in the note he didn't play.

"You're doing the thing," she said one night, head in his lap on the battered sofa while he worked one-handed on a mix, the other hand in her hair, the city dark and wet and theirs below. "The sideways looking-after thing. The tofu was here when I got here. I didn't order it."

"Don't know what you mean."

"I mean I notice. I notice everything, it's my whole job, you can't sneak a kindness past me." Her eyes were closed. The track played low. Jordan's four notes were in it somewhere, climbing and falling, and now, over them, faint, the brightness. "I just — let you. Which I don't do. I don't let people. It's the most frightening thing I've ever done, letting you, and I do it every day now, and it gets very slightly less frightening each time, and I wanted that on the record."

Cal looked down at the one person who'd ever walked into the room where he kept the only true thing about himself and decided to stay — to sit on the step, to listen under the kick, to let the loaded gun stay holstered when a launch was on the table — and he felt the furnace turn all the way to warmth, no fire left in it, and he understood that he had been wrong his entire life about what wanting was. He'd thought wanting was a problem to solve, a thing to lock down, his father's grip. It wasn't. Wanting, done right, was just this: keeping the room warm, leaving the price off, putting your own weight down at the same time as you took someone else's, in the same quiet, where no one could see, asking for nothing back except that they let you.

"It's on the record," he said softly, and kept his hand in her hair, and let the city tumble on below, and for a stretch of weeks — five of them, he'd count later, five luminous stolen weeks — two people who had spent their whole lives being seen by everyone and known by no one got to be, in one unmarked room, fully and dangerously and entirely known.

He should have known the bill always comes for that. In his experience it always did. But he was happy — properly, for the first time he could remember, the kind of happy that made even a man who calculated everything forget, for a few weeks, to run the odds — and happiness is the one condition under which the careful get careless, the watched stop watching the exits, the ghost forgets he is supposed to already be gone.

The catch was already in the building. It was wearing box-fresh trainers and an understated coat and a smile with no eyes in it. But for five weeks he didn't hear it on the stairs, because for five weeks, for the first time in his life, Callum Vance was not listening for the thing that takes people away. He was just — there. In the warm. With her. Putting the weight down.

It was the happiest he would ever be before everything broke, and some animal part of him knew it, and held on, and did not look at the door.

## ◆ viii-b. A City You Can Only Have at Night

*Ella*

They learned to have the city the way you have a thing you're not supposed to have — late, sideways, in the hours when the people who'd recognise either of them were asleep.

It became a kind of game, though neither of them called it that, because calling it a game would have admitted it was a relationship and they were both still, in daylight, pretending it was a deal with an unusual amount of kissing in it. The rules were unspoken and absolute: nowhere either world went. Not the bars off Stevenson Square where the scene drank; not the places near the academy where the lads ended up; not anywhere a phone might point and a story might start. Which left them, gloriously, with the other Manchester — the one underneath the photographed one, the one Ella had fallen for at eighteen and Cal had hidden in for five years, and which turned out, to both their surprise, to be largely the same Manchester, as if they'd been circling each other through the same back streets for years without colliding.

They walked the canals at midnight, the towpaths black and shining, the water holding the orange light in long broken ribbons, and Cal told her which of the converted mills had been what — *that one was a cotton finishing works, my brother did a session in the basement of that one before it was flats, there's a bloke does mad ambient stuff out of the arch under there still* — and she realised he carried a whole private map of the city, a producer's map, venues and studios and the histories of buildings, laid invisibly over the one everyone else walked through. She gave him hers in exchange: the fabric wholesalers behind the station that sold offcuts by weight, the print place in Salford that owed half the city favours, the caff on the edge of Ancoats that did the only flat white worth the walk, the spots where the light went the exact orange she built worlds out of. Two maps of the same city, each invisible to everyone but its maker, laid over each other at last.

They went to a late thing once — the only public risk they took, and they took it because Knox was playing a tiny unannounced set in a railway arch in the small hours, the kind of thing the scene called a *function*, and Cal wanted her to see what the music actually was, in a room, before the campaign, before the gloss, the raw live fact of it. They stood at the back in the dark, not touching, two strangers who'd met at a party, and Knox ran the new tracks over a system too big for the room, and when the single came on — her single, the one she'd built the world for — the arch full of people who didn't know either of them put their hands up, and Ella felt Cal go still beside her in the dark, the counting stillness, and she knew that four feet down in the track his brother was climbing and falling through a hundred bodies who had no idea they were carrying him, and she found Cal's hand in the dark, just for a second, just the once, where no one could see, and held it, and let go, and neither of them said anything, and it was the loudest thing that happened all night.

And they spent hours, mostly, in the studio doing nothing — which was the part that frightened her most, because Ella had never in her life been good at nothing, had never once experienced an empty hour as anything but a thing to be filled, optimised, made useful. With Cal she learned to waste time. She learned to lie on the battered sofa while he built a beat and not reach for her phone, not plan, not manage, just exist in a warm room while someone she — while someone made something beautiful three feet away. She'd catch herself, sometimes, doing nothing, and feel the old panic start, *you're wasting time, time is the one thing you can't earn back, get up, be useful, be the strong one* — and then she'd look at the back of his head bent over the desk and she'd let the panic go, deliberately, the way she'd learned to let the flinch go, and she'd stay, doing nothing, being no one's strong one, and it was the most radical thing she had ever done.

"What are you smiling at," he said one such night, not turning round.

"I'm not doing anything. I'm lying here doing absolutely nothing and the world hasn't ended."

"Revolutionary."

"It actually is. For me. You have no idea." She put her arm over her eyes. "My whole life, an empty hour was a debt. You had to pay it back in productivity or it counted against you. And I'm just — spending one. On nothing. On a sofa. While you make something I'm not even going to bill anyone for." She heard him stop working, the small sound of the room when he went still. "Don't make it a thing. I'll get up in a minute and be useful again."

"Don't," Cal said. "Get up, I mean. There's nothing to be useful for. That's the whole — that's the only thing this room's for. It's the one place neither of us has to be useful. Stay useless. With me." And she'd stayed, useless, in the warm, and the dryers of her own flat three miles south kept tumbling other people's laundry and the canals held the orange light and the city ran its sleepless underneath, and for five weeks two of the most relentlessly useful people in Manchester learned, in secret, in an unmarked room, the entirely forbidden art of being, together, gorgeously, restfully, good-for-nothing.

It could not last. They both knew it couldn't last. That was almost the point — that they held it the way you hold a held breath, knowing the body will eventually insist, treasuring it precisely because it was running out. But for five weeks they had the city you can only have at night, and each other, and the radical uselessness of a warm room, and Ella Savage-Oluwa, who had been the strong one since she was nine, got to find out what she was like when she wasn't carrying anything at all.

She was, it turned out, funny. She was, it turned out, soft. She was, it turned out, a person, underneath the project manager — and the person had been waiting down there, folded small, since a cold hall and a midnight kitchen table, for someone to build a room safe enough to come out in. He'd built it sideways, with a heater and a second mug and the price torn off everything, and she'd walked into it, and for five weeks she lived there, and it was the first home she'd had since she was nine that didn't require her to hold up the roof.

## ◆ ix. The First Crack

*Ella*

The crack came in, as cracks do, through the place that was bearing the most weight — which in their case was the gap between how good the work was getting and how quiet it had to stay.

The single came out in the third week of the stolen weeks, and it did the thing. Not a slow build — a *catch*, the way a fire catches, the way Cal described certain drum patterns catching, where one minute it's nothing and the next it's everywhere. The world Ella had built went out into the world and the world reposted it: the orange light, the wrong film stock, the rain-ruined type, the whole three-a.m. ache of it, reproduced across a hundred thousand screens, and a culture site ran a piece titled *The Visual Language of Knox's New Era* with her name in the third paragraph — *creative direction by Eleonora Savage-Oluwa, both names spelled right, she'd checked* — and her course leader emailed to say the external assessors had been asking about her, and her mother sent the culture-site link to the family group chat with the caption **MY DAUGHTER** in capitals and seventeen of the not-quite-aunties reacted with the hands-up emoji, and Tioluwa texted privately, *you're so sick, I'm telling everyone at school you're my sister, can you get me into something*, and Ella cried in the launderette-warm flat over a screenshot, which she'd have been mortified about if Priya hadn't cried too.

It was everything she'd wanted. And it was the exact thing that started the fray, because the louder the project got, the more the question got asked — and the question was always the same, in every interview request, every blog comment, every industry whisper: *who is V?*

"They want a reveal," Sasha said, dropping onto the studio sofa one afternoon — appearing at one of the odd late times now, the times Ella and Cal had thought were theirs — getting out her phone, all warmth. She'd taken to arriving like this, unannounced, the fixer's prerogative, and Ella had started to notice that Sasha's eyes did an inventory of the room each time that lingered, fractionally, on the things that didn't belong to a purely professional space: the second mug by the little sink, the heater that someone kept on, the toothbrush Ella had stopped pretending wasn't hers. "The single's doing numbers. Which means the label wants the album rollout bigger. Which means they want a *story*, and the story they keep coming back to is the mystery producer. 'Who is V.' A feature. A reveal. It'd be the biggest press the project gets — bigger than the music, honestly, which is bleak, but it's the world we're in." She let it sit. "V's resisting, obviously. But here's the thing, and I say this as a friend, Ella, because I think we are becoming friends: when an artist this size has a producer this good and this hidden, the press finds out *eventually*, whether we manage it or not. Someone always talks. A leaked session photo. A tagged location. A teammate who recognises a face." Her eyes flicked — just once, just a half-second — to the toothbrush. "Better it comes from us. On our terms. With a narrative we control. That's all I keep telling him. And you'd be central to that story — the eye who built the ghost's world. You two are the whole narrative, honestly. It'd make your career overnight."

The cold settled all the way through Ella now, the clearest sight yet of the cliff. Because she understood, with the part of her that read the gap between what people said and what they meant, exactly what Sasha was doing. She wasn't asking. She was *informing them of the weather.* She was telling them the reveal was coming — managed or leaked, their terms or someone else's — and offering Ella a role in it that wasn't really a choice: be the architect of the story and make your name, or be collateral when the story breaks anyway. And the thing she'd just done with her eyes, the half-second flick to the toothbrush, said the other part, the part Sasha was far too smooth to say aloud: *I know there's something between you. I know it's a better story than even the reveal. And I will use whichever one I need, when I need it.*

"V will reveal when V's ready," Ella said, keeping her voice level, her mother's breath in her chest. "It's his face. His name. Not the label's. Not mine."

"Of course," Sasha said warmly. "Of course it's his choice." A beat, perfectly weighted. "I'd just hate for it to *stop* being his choice. Stories have a way of choosing for you, if you wait too long." She stood, gathered the understated coat. "Think about it. Both of you." At the door — exactly where Ella had once stood, the last to leave — she paused. "Funny how that line keeps coming back, isn't it. *The eye who unmasked the ghost.* Almost like the story already knows how it wants to end. Stories are like that. They're already finished before we write them. Our job's just to stop fighting them." And she was gone, down the stairs that smelled of old paper, her heels precise on the worn treads.

Ella sat in the studio that had become theirs with the cold all the way through her, looking at the toothbrush, the second mug, the whole fragile luminous thing they'd built in the cracks of two impossible schedules, and understood that the catch had arrived — and that it was worse than either of them had braced for. Because it wasn't going to force Cal to choose between his two worlds. It was going to choose *for* him. On someone else's terms. At the worst possible time. The reveal was no longer a thing he could prevent by staying still; staying still just meant someone else got to pull the trigger on the loaded gun he'd spent five years keeping holstered.

## ◆ x. The Morning the Catch Didn't Get

*Ella*

She didn't tell Cal that afternoon.

She told herself it was timing — that he was deep in a mix, that Knox was coming later, that there was no good moment to say *Sasha as good as told me the reveal's coming whether you choose it or not and she knows about us and she'll weaponise whichever fact she needs.* But that was the posture over the real reason, and Ella had got better, over the stolen weeks, at catching her own postures. The real reason was older and worse: she wanted to give him one more clean morning before the world came in. She wanted to carry it by herself for one more day. The strong-one reflex, dressed up as a kindness — *I'll hold this so you don't have to feel it* — the exact thing she'd promised Cal she'd never do, the thing she'd sworn at him about at Knox's playback, *I will not be a thing you protect, I need a partner not a bodyguard,* now turned around and pointed the other way, because it turned out the reflex didn't care which direction it ran. Protecting someone from a weight you could carry alone felt like love and was, sometimes, just the old wound choosing the familiar shape.

So she gave them one more morning.

It was a Sunday, and it rained all day, the proper Manchester climate-rain, and neither of them had anywhere to be — his training cancelled for a waterlogged pitch, her assets delivered, the world briefly not asking either of them to be anyone — and they stayed in the studio with the heater on and the high window streaming and made a whole day of it, which neither of them had ever done with another person, given a clear day and just *spent* it. He cooked, badly, on the single hob meant for warming takeaway, and she sat on the counter and narrated his failures like a nature documentary. He retaliated by making her listen to the worst beat he'd ever made, age fifteen, a genuinely catastrophic thing he'd dug off an old drive, and she laughed so hard she nearly went off the counter and he caught her, automatic, his hands knowing where she'd be before she fell — the same gift that put a pass into space that wasn't there yet — and they stayed like that, her on the counter, him between her knees, both grinning like idiots, and he said, "I've never had a day off in my life. Not really. Even the days off were training for the days on. This is the first one that isn't *for* anything," and she said, "Me too," and meant it more than she'd meant most things, because every day of her life had been *for* something — the family, the proof, the future she was forcing into being — and this one was for nothing, this one was just theirs.

She fell asleep on the sofa in the afternoon — she who never let herself be unconscious near anyone, because unconscious was undefended and undefended was for people who had someone underneath them to catch the pieces — and woke an hour later under his jacket, which he'd laid over her, and he was at the desk working with the volume low so as not to wake her, and she lay there pretending to still be asleep just to watch him work in the half-dark when he thought no one could see, the still one, the magic, the ghost, and she understood with a clarity that frightened her that *this* — not the offer, not the degree show, not her name in the third paragraph — *this* was the thing she'd been trying to build her whole life without knowing it. Not a career. A room where she could be unconscious and undefended and wake up covered.

"You're awake," he said, not turning round. "Your breathing changed."

"You weren't supposed to notice."

"I notice everything. It's the family curse." He turned, and looked at her under his jacket in the grey streaming light, and something moved across his face that she'd never seen on it — fear's gentler cousin, *wanting something this much is dangerous and I've made a study of not wanting things this much* — and he said, "I keep waiting to get bored. Of people. I always get bored, it's why the bit works, I've never met anyone still interesting on the second day. It's been thirty-five days."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's a fact," they said together, and laughed, and she got up still wrapped in his jacket and crossed the room to him — because in the studio she was always the one who crossed the room now, he'd crossed it first at the party and ever after she closed the distance, having learned from him that crossing a room you might lose is the bravest thing a person does.

She held the catch by herself for one more day. She gave them the clean Sunday. And it was, she'd think later, the kindest and most cowardly thing she ever did — she let him have one more day of being only happy, carried the weight alone the way she'd carried weight her whole life, and told herself it was a gift, and it was, and it was also the old reflex that would detonate in both their faces the moment the catch finally landed, precisely because they were two people who had each, separately, learned that some weights you are supposed to set down in the middle, between you, where both of you can see them — and who had not yet learned to do it, not really, not when it counted, not yet.

But that was coming. On the clean grey Sunday it hadn't come. There was only rain, and a bad beat, and a jacket laid over a sleeping girl, and a boy who noticed her breathing change, and the last full day before the orbit they'd been spinning in for five luminous weeks finally, inevitably, began to decay — pulled down, as orbits always are, by the gravity of the thing at the centre that neither of them could see, and neither of them could stop, and both of them had spent their whole lives pretending wasn't there.
