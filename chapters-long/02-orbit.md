# Chapter Two — Orbit

> *Long chapter · target ~45,000 words · built progressively. Scenes marked with ◆. Covers the slow burn: deepening collaboration, the secret of Jordan, the no-invoice kindness, the furnace under the lid, and the first kiss. Absorbs and expands old short-chapters 5–8.*

---

## ◆ i. The Argument That Was the Work

*Ella*

By the third week she understood that the arguing was the work, and that no one had ever matched her at it before.

Priya argued to win — to be declared right, to close the matter, arguments as small wars with a victor. Her mother argued to be obeyed, which wasn't arguing at all, it was instruction with the volume of argument. The boys she'd dated had argued to be soothed, every disagreement secretly a request to be told they were good and clever and not in trouble. And Ella, who argued the way she did everything, which was to find out what was *true*, had spent twenty-one years being slightly too much for every room she argued in — too fast, too willing to follow a thought past the point where it flattered anyone, too unbothered by losing a point if losing it got her closer to the real shape of the thing.

Cal argued the same way. That was the discovery. He didn't argue to win; he argued to *test* — he attacked an idea precisely in proportion to how much he respected it, the way you only really push the structures you suspect might hold, and Ella, who had been mistaking respect for combat her whole life because no one had ever shown her the difference, found in him the first person who pushed her ideas hard *because* he thought they were load-bearing.

"The single cover's wrong," he said, on a grey Tuesday, holding the rough she'd left on his desk as a joke — *Produced by V. (the boring answer)* — except now it was a real version, refined, beautiful, the orange and the bled film stock and the rain-ruined type.

"It's not wrong, it's the best thing I've made this year."

"It's the best thing you've made this year and it's wrong." He set it down between them. "It's too resolved. Look at it — it's *finished*. It's a beautiful closed object. And the track isn't closed, the track's a wound that won't scab, the drums never arrive, the whole thing's an open question. You've made a closed cover for an open song. The cover should feel like it got interrupted. Like someone walked in before it was done and the maker didn't get to finish making it nice. *That's* the three a.m. of it. Three a.m. isn't beautiful-and-resolved. Three a.m. is beautiful-and-unfinished, because at three a.m. you've run out of energy to make the feeling presentable."

And Ella opened her mouth to defend it — the reflex, the strong one, the work is good, *I* am good — and stopped, because he was right, and the rightness arrived in her not as a defeat but as a click, the specific click of a thing she'd half-known being said out loud by someone who'd seen it too. "The type," she said slowly. "I kerned it perfectly. I should leave one letter wrong. One. Like the rain got it and I didn't fix it."

"Or like you started fixing it and got called away and never came back."

"Like the maker had somewhere else to be." She was already reaching for the laptop, the want rising, the good want, the work-want. "Like the cover's homesick for a version of itself that had time to be perfect."

"Homesick for now," Cal said quietly, giving her her own phrase back, the one she'd used about his music in the pitch, and they looked at each other across the little distance of the studio, two people who'd just built something truer together in ninety seconds than either had ever built alone, and Ella felt the thing she was not going to name move through the room like a change in cabin pressure, and she went very fast back to the laptop to have somewhere to put her hands.

"You do this thing," she said, to the screen, kerning a letter wrong on purpose, her heart doing the irritating arrhythmic thing. "When something's right and you hear it. You go completely still. Everyone else nods or talks or says 'sick.' You stop. Like you're counting."

"Like I'm counting," he agreed.

"I noticed it at the party. Before I knew it was you, before any of this. You were standing in the loudest room in Fallowfield being the only still thing in it."

"You were the other still thing."

"I was leaving."

"You stayed forty minutes."

"I left at thirty-eight," Ella said. "To spite you. You'd predicted forty and I couldn't give you forty."

He laughed, the real one, the one she'd now heard maybe four times and had started, alarmingly, to collect — to keep a private tally of, the way you hoard a rationed thing — and the laugh filled the studio and the heater hummed and the city did its grey business below and Ella thought, kerning a letter wrong for a cover that was homesick for now: *I am in so much trouble. I have a deadline and a deal and a mother at six o'clock, and I am sitting in a room above a vintage shop collecting a boy's laughter like it's a currency, and the worst part, the genuinely frightening part, is that I have never in my life felt this much like a person and this little like a project manager, and I don't want it to stop, and not wanting it to stop is the most dangerous data I have ever collected on myself.*

She kerned the letter wrong. She saved it. It was the best thing she'd made that year, and now it was also true, and the difference between good and true was, she was learning, a boy three feet away who argued with her because he thought she could hold the weight.

## ◆ ii. Why Styling

*Cal*

He asked her the question on a night that overran, both of them slumped at opposite ends of the battered sofa under the high window, a single lamp on, the city dark and wet and smeared below, the work done and neither of them moving to leave.

"Why styling," Cal said. Not a deflection, not a transition — a real question, the kind he almost never asked, because asking gave something away, because a question is a confession of what you don't know and he'd built a life out of appearing to know everything. "You could run anything. I've watched you in two meetings with Sasha — you could be in any room you wanted, board rooms, the lot. You read people faster than anyone I've ever met. So why this. Why the least safe, least respectable, hardest-to-explain-at-a-dinner version of using that." A pause. "Does your mum know you're doing this instead of — whatever the safe thing was."

"Don't," Ella said. Sharp. Reflex.

He raised a hand, retreating instantly, no defensiveness, just *withdrawn, understood, sorry* — and the cleanness of the retreat was so unlike every man who'd ever pushed her that it undid the sharpness, and she sighed, and softened, and he watched her decide to spend something, the way he'd decided to spend things with her, the ledger between them that had been quietly running since the deal.

"Sorry," she said. "It's a — sore spot. My mum thinks this is a hobby I'm calling a career. She wants me on a graduate scheme. Law, finance, something with a building you can point to and a name the aunties recognise. And the worst thing — the thing that makes it impossible to just be angry at her — is that I understand exactly why." And she told him. The crossing of water. Twice as good and half as visible. The midnight kitchen table when she was nine, her mother not-crying on purpose, her father's hand flat on the table not-touching, the vow she'd flicked like a switch: *I will never be a thing they have to carry.* The twelve years of being the easy one so completely that the seam sealed over. "So when I choose the most visible, least safe thing there is, to her it doesn't look like courage. It looks like I'm taking the high safe ground they bought with their bodies and using it to do the exact precarious creative thing that ruined half the men in our family. It looks like spitting on the climb."

"And to you?"

No one had ever asked her that. Priya cheered her on; her mother grieved her in advance; the course treated it as ambition; no one had asked her what it *was.*

"To me it's the only thing I've ever done," Ella said slowly, the truth arriving even as she said it, surprising her, "where I'm not performing being fine. Everywhere else I'm the strong one. The eldest. The one who's got it handled, who carries, who never gets to fall apart because there's no one underneath her to catch the pieces — there's only siblings looking up. When I'm building a world for a sound, I'm not holding anything up. I'm just *seeing.* It's the only place I get to put the weight down." She looked at him, and made the leap, because he'd made leaps for her. "You'd know. It's what this room is for you. Isn't it. The one place nobody's watching you on a touchline. The one place the —" she almost said *held breath*, and didn't have the phrase yet, that was his, in his head, she only had the shape of it "— the one place you get to stop."

Cal went very still. The counting stillness. She watched him stand at the edge of something and decide whether to step off it.

"Yeah," he said, low. "That's exactly what it is."

And they sat there, two people who'd each spent their whole lives being the version of themselves that other people needed, in the one room where neither of them had to be, and the heater hummed, and the rain came down on the Northern Quarter, and the lamp made a small warm island of them in the dark — and Ella understood that they had just told each other the same secret in two different costumes, the strong one and the ghost, the switch and the held breath, the kitchen table and the box room (though she didn't know about the box room yet, didn't know there *was* a box room, knew only that the shape of his silence had a room in it with a door). And she thought: *we are the same animal. He's the first one of my own kind I've ever met. That's why I can't read him — I can't read him for the same reason I can't see my own face. He's too close. He's the same.*

She stood up. Too fast. "I should go. It's late."

"Yeah." He stood too, fast, and for a second they were close, the way they'd been close at the party, near enough that she could smell the cedar under the studio's coffee and electronics, and the air between them tightened like a held breath about to go.

She left first. She made sure of it. Looking away first was an apology and leaving first was a victory and she needed the victory more than she needed to know what would have happened if she'd stayed. On the stairs that smelled of old paper she stopped, one flight down, out of his sight, and pressed her hand flat against the cold wall and breathed — in for four, out for four, her mother's breath — and above her, through the floor, she heard him start to play something. Not the single. Something newer. Something with a melody in the top of it that she didn't recognise and somehow did, four notes, three climbing and one falling, except now there was a brightness over them she could have sworn hadn't been there before, a warmth laid across the grief, and she stood in the stairwell of the unmarked studio listening to the centre of Callum Vance come down through the floor, and she did not go back up.

But she didn't leave either. Not for a while. And that — the not-leaving, the sitting down on the step — was the most honest thing she'd done in years, and she did it where he couldn't see, which was the only way she knew how to be honest yet: in the dark, on the stairs, with her hand on the cold wall and a melody she half-knew coming down through the boards, letting herself, for the length of one track, simply want the thing she had a deadline and a deal and a mother at six o'clock telling her she couldn't have.

## ◆ iii. Listen to the Bit at the End

*Cal*

He told her about Jordan on a Tuesday, which he had not planned, which he had in fact spent five years planning never to do with anyone, and the way it happened was so small that he didn't see it coming until it had already happened — which was, he'd think later, the only way it ever could have happened, because anything he saw coming he could lock, and the whole point of Jordan was that Jordan was the one thing he could never quite get the door shut on.

They were working late, too late, the session overrunning because Knox had sent eleven voice notes of new ad-libs and Cal was chopping them at the desk while Ella sat cross-legged on the floor three feet away building the inner-sleeve artwork in real time, her laptop throwing blue light up onto her face the way the streetlamp threw orange across the launderette floor she'd never described to him but that he'd somehow built a picture of from the shape of her. And she was humming.

Under her breath. Not to anything. Not to the Knox track, which was in a different key — just a melody, idle, the way people hum when they've forgotten they're not alone, when the work has taken the front of the mind and left the back of it to wander.

Cal's hands stopped on the desk.

"What's that," he said.

"What's what?"

"What you're humming."

Ella looked up, genuinely surprised, not having known she was doing it. "I don't — nothing. It's just a thing. I don't even know where it's — my brother used to —" she frowned, chasing it. "It's nothing. A nursery thing, maybe, from when we were small. Why?"

But Cal had already gone somewhere else, somewhere five years deep, because the melody she'd been humming — idle, to no one, while building the inside of an album sleeve — was four notes that climbed and one that fell. Not the same. She couldn't have known the same; the buried version was pitched and chopped and four feet down where the body finds it before the ear does. But the *shape*. The exact shape. The reluctant rise and the small heartbreak of the drop. The bones of the thing he'd built his whole secret life around, hummed by accident by the one living person he'd let into the room, and the awful intimate coincidence of it — that she'd carried some cousin of his brother's melody in her own body since she was small, that the shape had been waiting in her too, that the centre of gravity had been *in her* the whole time — undid something in Cal that had been holding, white-knuckled, for five years and three months.

"Cal?"

"The voice," he heard himself say. His own voice sounded far away. "In the track. The deep one. The one you keep asking about. You asked who it was."

She closed the laptop. The blue light went out. She didn't say anything. She'd learned, he'd noticed, exactly when not to say anything, which was a skill almost no one had, which was the rarest skill there was — the discipline to let a silence stay open instead of rushing to fill it, to hold the door without pushing on it.

"It's my brother," Cal said. To the desk. To the dark window. To the city tumbling on below, indifferent, full of people each carrying their one unbearable thing. "Jordan. He's — he was. Six years older. He's the reason I make any of this. He used to make beats in a box room when I was a kid and I'd sit on the floor while he did it, where I wasn't allowed, and he was —" Cal's voice was very level, the dangerous level, the level that meant the opposite of what level usually meant "— he was so much better than me, Ella, you have to understand that, I'm good but Jordan *heard things.* He'd play me a kick and say 'listen under it,' and there'd be a whole note that wasn't being played, that your body played for itself in the gap, and he'd watch my face until I got it. 'That's the whole game, little man. Anyone can play you a note. The trick's making them hear the one you didn't play.'" He stopped. Started again, because if he stopped now he'd never. "And my dad's a rugby man. Played second row, broke himself, decided his sons were going to do it properly. And I was good at the rugby, so I got the —" *the nod*, he almost said, and didn't, because the nod was a whole other locked room "— I got picked. The useful son. And Jordan wasn't. Jordan was good at the thing my dad couldn't see the use of. And my dad called it a waste. To his face. For years. *There's no living in that. Look at your brother.* Look at me. The one who picked the nameable thing."

The studio was completely silent. Even the heater seemed to be holding its breath. Ella sat on the floor in the dark, not moving toward him, and he was grateful for it with a gratitude that frightened him, because if she'd crossed the room and held him in that moment he'd have come apart, and instead she did the one exact right thing, the thing almost no one alive knew how to do: she stayed where she was and let the weight of it sit in the room without trying to lift it off him too soon.

"And Jordan didn't fall apart loud," Cal said. "That's the thing people don't understand. He fell apart *quiet.* He got smaller. He stopped playing me stuff. He drove further and further for sessions, chasing the thing the box room couldn't give him and my dad never would — Leeds, Sheffield, once Bristol — tired all the time, they said tired, and one night he was coming back from Leeds, late, the road was wet, and —" Cal looked at his own hands on the desk like they belonged to someone else. "I was sixteen. I was asleep. He'd sent me a voice note at quarter past eleven. 'Listen to this, Cal, listen to the bit at the end, I think I cracked it, tell me I cracked it.' And I didn't —" his throat closed and he made it open "— I was at training, and then I was tired, and then it could wait, because everything could always wait, because I was sixteen and he was permanent. Two days. I didn't listen for two days. And by the time I opened it he'd been gone for —" He didn't finish. There was no end to that sentence that he had ever found.

Ella didn't say *I'm sorry.* She didn't say any of the things people said, the things that made grief about the comfort of the person witnessing it. She said, very quietly, after a silence she let run as long as it needed: "And the buried track. The voice four feet down. That's the voice note."

"It's the last thing he ever sent me. I never replied. So now —" Cal's voice cracked clean down the middle and he let it, in the dark, with her, for the first time in five years "— so now I put a piece of it in everything I make. Buried so deep no one can hear it, so deep it can't be taken, so deep my dad could never find it and be destroyed by it, because if my dad ever knew I was still doing the thing he thinks killed his other son — the day my dad hears my name on a track is the day I lose the only parent I've got left, and I'm not — I lost Jordan, I can't —" He stopped. Breathed. "I've put my brother in a quarter of a million strangers' headphones and not one of them knows they're carrying him. They play him on buses. They fall asleep to him. It's the only grave I could give him that my dad can't visit and ruin and that the world can't take, because the world doesn't even know it's there. That's the secret, Ella. That's the whole secret. I don't hide my name to protect a rugby career. I hide it because my name on that music is a loaded gun pointed at the last of my family, and I have been very, very careful, for five years, never to let anyone near the trigger."

For a long time neither of them spoke. The rain came down. The city carried its millions of unbearable things north and south through the dark.

"Cal," Ella said at last. "Thank you. For trusting me with that. I know what it costs to hand someone a loaded gun and call it trust."

"I didn't decide to. You hummed his melody. It just —"

"You kept going," she said. "After the humming. You didn't have to keep going. That was the decision. The humming was an accident. The rest was you, choosing, in real time, to let one living person listen under the kick." She uncrossed her legs and got up, slowly, and crossed the room — and she didn't hug him, she'd read that right too, she came and stood beside him at the desk and put her hand flat on the wood next to his, not touching, just there, a thing in the world that was on his side, and the gesture was so exactly her mother's hand on the midnight table that she didn't know she was doing it and Cal didn't know where it came from but it reached all the way down. "I'm not going to tell you to put your name on it," she said. "People are always telling me to be louder about who I am, and they mean well, and they don't understand that visibility isn't free, that some of us know to the penny what it costs. You know exactly what it costs. So I'd never. But I'm going to ask you one thing, and you can say no."

"Ask."

"Let me hear it. The actual voice note. Not the buried version — the real one. The bit at the end. The thing he wanted you to listen to."

Cal looked at her. Five years he'd carried the voice note. Five years he'd played the encrypted version, the safe version, the one where Jordan was hidden so deep he was un-loseable and also, finally, unheard. No one had ever asked to hear the actual thing. No one had known there was an actual thing to ask for. She'd reverse-engineered its existence from a melody she'd hummed by accident and a hand that went to a file and stopped, and now she was asking — not to fix it, not to make it about her, not for the story — just to do, five years late, the thing a sixteen-year-old hadn't: to listen.

His hands weren't steady. He got out his phone. He found the thread that said **Jords** with the photo of the stupid face in the car. He found the voice note, 0:47, the little mountain range he knew by heart and hadn't let make a sound in over a year. He looked at Ella once. She nodded — not pushing, just there, the hand on the wood.

He pressed play.

A laugh first. Jordan's laugh, bright and alive and completely unguarded, twenty-two forever. *"Cal, bro — listen, listen to this bit at the end, I think I cracked it, tell me I cracked it, man—"* And then the music. Rough, unfinished, recorded off a monitor in a box room or a hired room in Leeds, a little distorted — four notes that climbed and one that fell, the shape Ella had hummed an hour ago without knowing, raw and unmixed and so full of a person who was certain he had all the time in the world that it filled the studio like weather, like light, like a third presence standing in the room with them in the dark.

It ended. The silence after it was a different silence than any that had come before.

"He cracked it," Ella said softly. Not to Cal. To the room. To the bit at the end. To a boy in a frozen car. "Tell him he cracked it."

And Cal Vance, who had not let another human being watch him cry since he was sixteen years old, who had built an entire self out of stillness precisely so that no one would ever see the rooms inside him, put his head down on the desk in the unmarked studio above the vintage shop — and Ella Savage-Oluwa stood beside him with her hand flat on the wood, not touching, just *there*, on his side, the midnight-table hand, the not-crying breath she'd inherited now spent on letting someone else finally cry — and she did not lift the weight off him too soon, and she did not make it hers, and she did not say a single one of the wrong things.

She just stayed. On the step. In the dark. Listening under the kick.

She left an hour later, and in all that hour neither of them had touched the other, not once, not a hand, not a shoulder — and it was, by a distance that astonished them both, the most intimate night of either of their lives.

## ◆ iv. No Invoice

*Ella*

After the night of the voice note, something changed in the room, and the change was that he started looking after her.

Not loudly. Cal didn't do anything loudly. It arrived sideways, in increments so small that a less observant person would have missed them, and Ella was not a less observant person — she missed nothing, it was her whole function — which meant she caught every single one and had to decide, each time, what to do with the catching.

The first one was food. She'd mentioned, once, weeks ago, in passing, that the Vietnamese place two streets over did a tofu thing she'd think about at night. She'd said it the way you say things you don't expect anyone to file. And then one evening, around the hour her body started running on fumes and bad decisions, there it was on the little table she'd annexed — the tofu thing, from the place two streets over, still hot, no comment, Cal already back at the desk with his back half to her as if it had materialised out of the building. She'd looked at it for a long moment. She'd said, "You didn't have to." He'd said, "I was getting mine." Which was a lie, a small gallant one, because his was a different order entirely and she'd watched him not eat it for an hour, and the lie was the gift inside the gift: he'd built her an exit, a way to receive it without owing, *I was getting mine anyway, it's nothing, don't make it a thing.*

The second one was the heater. The studio was warm because Cal kept it warm now, on by the time she arrived, the cold hundred-year-old air already chased out of the corners, and he never mentioned it and she never thanked him for it because thanking him would have made it a transaction and the entire point — she understood this with a slow, frightening clarity — the entire point was that it *wasn't* a transaction. He was doing the thing she had never once in her life let anyone do. He was taking weight off her without being asked, without keeping score, without the small invoice that she had learned at nine was attached to every kindness, the IOU that turned a gift into a debt and a debt into a way of being owned.

The third one she only noticed because of the deadline.

She'd been stressed about a delivery — the label wanted the full single-rollout assets by the Friday, and a printer had let her down, and she'd been quietly catastrophising about it for two days in the way she catastrophised about everything, privately, managing it, never letting it show — and then on the Thursday the panic she'd budgeted for simply didn't arrive, because the thing was handled, the assets were with the label early, and when she traced back how she found that Cal had made a call. To someone he knew. A printer in Salford who owed him a favour from some music thing, who'd turned it around overnight. He hadn't told her. She found out from the label thanking *her* for the fast turnaround on a job she hadn't known was rescued. And when she'd looked at him across the studio, the question on her face, he'd just shrugged and said, "You'd have sorted it. I just sorted it faster. Saved you the two days of pretending you weren't stressed," and gone back to the desk — and that was the one that undid her, because it meant he'd *seen* the pretending. He'd seen her manage the panic privately, seen the strong-one performance she'd been running since she was nine, the one no one had ever once caught because the whole art of it was that no one caught it — and he hadn't called it out, hadn't made her admit it, hadn't asked for the vulnerability as the price of the help. He'd just quietly removed the thing she was being strong about, sideways, and let her keep her face.

That night she didn't go straight home. She sat in the studio after the work was done, on the battered sofa under the high window, and she said, to the room more than to him, "You're doing a thing."

"I'm mixing."

"You're doing the thing where you look after me and you build me an exit every time so I don't have to feel it. The food's always 'I was getting mine.' The printer was 'you'd have sorted it anyway.' You keep — you keep handing me things with the price torn off." She heard her own voice doing something unfamiliar, going thin at the edges, the strong-one register failing. "I need you to know that I notice. I notice everything, it's the whole problem with me, I've never once in my life *not* noticed. And I don't — I don't know what to do with it. Kindness that doesn't come with an invoice. I've genuinely never had any. Every nice thing anyone's ever done for me came with a balance owing, and I learned to keep the balance at zero by never accepting anything, by being the one who gives so I never owe, and you keep —" she stopped. "You keep zeroing the balance from your side. And I don't have a system for that. It makes me feel like I'm falling."

Cal turned from the desk. The full beam, but soft now, the detachment long gone. He looked at her for a moment in the lamplight with the city dark and wet below, and then he said, carefully, the way he said the things that mattered: "There's no invoice because there's no debt. That's not a system you have to learn. It's the absence of one." A pause. "You spent your whole life keeping the balance at zero by carrying everything yourself. I spent mine making sure nobody could ever leave by being gone before they could. We both built the same thing — a way to never need anyone, dressed up as strength. And I'm so tired, Ella. I'm so tired of mine. I've been tired since I was sixteen. So when I put the heater on before you get here, it's not for you to owe me. It's because for about ninety seconds, doing one small thing to take weight off one person, I'm not running the machine. You're not the only one getting something. That's the part you can't see from inside your system. Letting me take care of you sideways isn't you owing me. It's you letting me put my own weight down for ninety seconds. So you're not falling. We're both just — setting it down. At the same time. In the same room."

The heater hummed. The rain came down on the Northern Quarter. And Ella Savage-Oluwa, who had not let anyone take care of her since she was nine years old in a cold hall, felt the flinch arrive right on schedule — the hand-near-a-burn reflex, the *accepting this makes you a person with needs and you are not, you are the one who carries* — and for the first time in twelve years she watched the flinch arrive and chose, deliberately, with her whole will, not to obey it.

"Okay," she said. Quietly. It came out unsteady. "Okay. Then — thank you for the heater. And I'm not going to say it like it's a transaction. I'm going to say it like —" her voice cracked, clean, one fracture, and she let it, in the lamplight, in front of him, the strong one cracking on purpose for the first time in her adult life "— like a person who's letting someone be kind to her and is terrified, and is doing it anyway."

"That's the bravest thing I've watched you do," Cal said. "And I watched you take a record label apart with a moodboard."

She laughed, wetly, and it broke the unbearable thing into something they could both hold, and she wiped her face once, hard, and said, "Don't tell anyone I cried in your studio, V, I have a reputation," and he said "your reputation's safe, no one even knows I exist," and they sat in the warm dark and the awful tender thing settled between them into something survivable — and Ella understood, walking home later through the orange rain, that the deal was now comprehensively, irretrievably not a transaction, that it had never been a transaction, that she had been lying to herself since the first afternoon, and that the lie was finally, quietly, out of road.

She let herself feel it the whole walk home. The wanting. Undefended, un-folded, enormous. She didn't put it away. She let it walk beside her through the wet glittering city all the way to the launderette door, and only there, hand on the cold handle, did she say to it, gently, not a refusal now but a postponement: *Not yet. Soon, maybe. But not yet. There's still a deadline and a deal and a mother at six o'clock, and he's still got a locked room with his whole family in it, and the timing is catastrophic. But not never. For the first time — not never.*

It was the closest she had come, since she was nine, to letting herself have something. And the city, beautiful and slightly ruined, orange and wet and indifferent, witnessed it the way it witnessed everything: without comment, and without ever once looking away.
