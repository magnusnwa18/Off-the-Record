# Chapter Four — Reckoning

> *Long chapter · ≥15k. Scenes marked ◆. The catch falls: the offer made concrete, the impossible arithmetic, the leak, the worst-order exposure, and the black-hour break. Absorbs/expands old short-chapters 11–15. Continuity: Ella & Cal secretly together ~6 weeks; Ella has Imogen's offer + Sasha's reveal email; Cal has the senior contract pending; both holding half the catastrophe alone.*

---

## ◆ i. Soho

*Ella*

She took the train down on a day-return she could barely afford, and Imogen Clarke-Bayo bought her a flat white in a too-expensive place off Berwick Street and laid the rest of her life on the table between them.

"You've got the rarest thing," Imogen said — plainly, the way powerful people who don't need to perform power say things. "Most people in this industry can only *see.* They make things look right. You make things *sound* right, visually, which makes no sense and is the entire job. The Knox work isn't styled, it's *scored.* I want you. Junior creative director, not an intern, a real seat, start in September after your show. You'd work on the campaigns you've been screenshotting since you were sixteen. I'll pay you properly, which no one does for someone your age, and I'm only doing it because I've watched twenty years of agencies let people like you walk out the door because they couldn't see what you'd be in five years. I can see it. So." She slid a folded sheet across the table. "That's the offer. Take a week."

Ella did not unfold it, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not let them shake in front of Imogen Clarke-Bayo. It was everything. It was the whole argument with her mother, won, in writing — *the thing you're good at IS a thing you can build a life on, here is a respected woman paying you properly to prove it.* It was the answer to six o'clock. It was the thing she'd come to Manchester at eighteen to make possible, arriving in a folded sheet she didn't dare open.

"Why now," she heard herself say. "The album's not even fully out. You could've waited for the campaign."

Imogen's smile had an edge of the industry in it. "Honest answer? Because the Knox project is about to get very loud, and I want you signed *before* it does. Your name's attached to a story that's about to break — the producer, the mystery one, V. Everyone in London's heard the reveal's coming; Sasha Reyes has been seeding it for a fortnight, she's good, she's got three outlets circling. And when that breaks, the whole campaign goes supernova, and you — the eye who built the ghost's world — become the person everyone wants, and I won't be able to afford you. So I'm getting you a week early. Before the explosion. While you're still a secret too." A pause, lighter, not light at all. "Between us — do you know who he is? V? Because if you did, and wanted to tell me, off the record, it wouldn't change the offer. The offer stands either way. But I won't pretend it wouldn't be useful to know what's coming."

And there it was — the same line, in a kinder mouth, in a more expensive coffee shop. *Off the record. It'd be useful. The girl who unmasked the ghost.* Sasha had seeded it so well that it had grown into the soil of Ella's actual dream job, and the two things were tangled at the root.

"I don't know who he is," Ella said, and it was the first time she'd told the lie to someone she actually liked, and it cost her. "I just made the outside of the door look nice."

Imogen looked at her a long moment — the look Sasha gave her, except where Sasha's was inventory, Imogen's was almost respect. "You know," she said, "I believe you don't want to tell me, which isn't the same as not knowing, but it's a better answer than most people would give me, because most people would lie *enthusiastically.* Loyalty's rare in this business. It usually gets people nowhere." She stood, gathered her coat. "Take the week. And Ella — whatever you're protecting? I hope it's worth the supernova passing you by, because it might. I'll be straight: the *size* of your start is downstream of that story breaking. If the reveal doesn't happen, the project stays a cult thing — a lovely CV line, not the thing that makes me unable to afford you. The offer stands either way. But you should know exactly what the loyalty's costing you before you spend it." A card on the table beside the folded sheet. "Most people never find out who they are because they never have to choose between what they want and who they want to be. You're about to. Lucky you. It's horrible. Call me."

## ◆ ii. The Arithmetic That Won't Come Out

*Ella*

She didn't unfold the offer until the train north, through the dark, and the number on it was more than her parents had made between them most years she was growing up, and she sat with it in the rattling carriage and let herself, for one stop, just *want* it. Cleanly. The seat, the money, the campaigns, the answer to six o'clock, the proof. She'd earned it. God, she'd earned it.

And all she had to do to make it as big as it could be was let the worst day of Cal's life happen.

That was the arithmetic, laid bare on a train outside Stockport, and it would not come out. Everything she wanted was downstream of the reveal. The album going supernova, this offer at its full size, her career launching the way Imogen described — all of it ran through the explosion of Cal's secret. She could keep his secret and watch the project stay small and the offer quietly shrink to a CV line. Or the reveal could happen — on Sasha's terms, the headline version, the betrayal version, the one that would reach Cal's father as the worst possible blow — and Ella's career would launch on the back of the single most destructive day of the person she loved.

She couldn't win clean. Sasha had built it that way on purpose — the way Ella built worlds, with total command of how every element served the story — and the story Sasha was telling set Ella's ambition and Cal's secret against each other so that *someone* would crack and the reveal would come, and it didn't matter to Sasha which of them broke, because either break made her story.

She thought about her mother at the kitchen table at midnight, not crying so the children wouldn't see. She thought about Cal with his head on the desk, *you can't bury a man who's already buried himself.* She thought about the toothbrush and the second mug and the morning the catch wasn't allowed to have, and Knox turning down ten million to be a true thing out loud, and Imogen's *most people never find out who they are.*

And somewhere outside Stockport, in the dark, with the offer in her hands and the lights of the towns going by like a sound she couldn't quite hear, Ella stopped trying to win clean — because there was no clean win, Sasha had seen to that — and started, instead, to think about how you change the game when someone else wrote the rules. Not how to choose between what she wanted and who she wanted to be. How to *refuse the choice itself.* How to take the story away from the person telling it. She didn't have the plan yet, on the train. She had only the shape of it, and a refusal, and the first true certainty she'd had in weeks: she would not build her career on his ruin, and she would not let Sasha be the one who decided when his life detonated. There had to be a third door. She built worlds for a living. She would build one more.

She texted Sasha three words — *Let's get coffee* — the first move of a game she meant to write the rules of herself this time. Then she folded the offer back up, and watched the dark country go by, and for the first time in the whole story she was not the one being read. She was the one doing the reading.

She did not tell Cal about the offer that night either. (One more morning. The old reflex. The fault line.) She would tell him — she meant to, the next day, the whole shape of it, *here's the trap, here's how I think we beat it together* — she had it planned for the Wednesday.

The leak broke on the Wednesday morning, six hours before she got the chance.

## ◆ iii. Exposure

*Cal*

It broke at 6 a.m., which was wrong, which was the first sign it had slipped everyone's control — Cal had braced for a Thursday, had braced for *managed*, for Sasha's smooth machinery rolling the reveal out on schedule. Instead his phone went at 7:40 in the changing room before training, and it was Knox, and Knox never called, Knox sent eleven voice notes, so the call itself meant something had gone wrong.

"Cal. Bro. It's out. Someone leaked it — there's a piece, it's got a session photo, through the studio window, it names you. *Callum Vance.* Pro rugby prospect. It's everywhere, man, it's —"

The room went very narrow and very clear.

A smaller outlet than Sasha would have chosen — a music blog with a chip on its shoulder, the kind that broke things to spite the people who managed things — headline *EXCLUSIVE: The Secret Behind Knox's New Sound Is a Pro Rugby Prospect.* A photograph, blurry, taken through the high window from the building opposite: Cal at the desk. His name. The academy. A paragraph of breathless speculation about "the double life of the fly-half who could be capped within two years" and another about "questions this raises about his commitment to the sport." Everything he'd buried for five years, dug up and laid out in the worst possible order, up since 6 a.m., screenshotted into every group chat in two industries by 7:40.

Including, he knew with a certainty that arrived before any other thought, his father's.

He didn't finish the call. He stood in the changing room with the lads filtering in around him, some already looking at him sideways, already having seen, and felt the two worlds he'd kept in two boxes for his whole life slam together so hard that the man between them simply stopped being able to move.

Tom found him. Tom, who'd covered for his disappearances for two years and never pushed, who ran the sweepstake on the very secret now detonating, took one look at Cal's face and steered him out by one enormous arm into the corridor. "I didn't — Cal, the *music* — I won the sweepstake and I don't even —" Tom's face was doing something terrible, the betrayal-and-love of a best friend learning the size of what he hadn't been trusted with. "Why didn't you *tell* me, man? Two years. I'd have —" But there wasn't time, because the phone rang in Cal's hand, and the screen said **Dad**, and Cal looked at it ringing and could not make himself answer, because answering it was the moment he'd organised his entire life to avoid: his father finding out that the son he'd saved from being wasted had been doing the wasteful thing in the dark for five years — finding out from a *headline*, with a blurry photo, the betrayal version, the exact worst version Ella had warned him about, the one where he didn't get to say it himself.

It rang out. Started again. **Dad.**

"Answer it," Tom said quietly. "Whatever it is. It's worse if you don't."

Cal answered it.

"Dad."

Silence. A long silence, his father's silence — except there was breathing in it, and the breathing wasn't cold, the breathing was a thing Cal had heard only once before in his life, on a wet night when he was sixteen and a phone had rung a different way.

"Is it true," his father said. Not cold. *Wrecked.* "The music. The — is it you. In the photo. Is it true."

"Dad, I can explain, I was going to —"

"*Is it true, Callum.*"

"Yes," Cal said. "It's me. I've been making it for years. I'm sorry. I never wanted you to find out like —"

And his father made a sound Cal would hear for the rest of his life, a sound with no words in it, the sound of a man who had buried one son for loving this exact thing and had just learned his other son had loved it in secret the whole time, right under him — and the sound held grief and betrayal and fear and, underneath all of those, something Cal was too far gone to hear — and then his father said, in a voice gone flat and final, "Don't come to the house," and ended the call.

*Don't come to the house.* The thing he'd spent five years and one buried brother's voice avoiding had happened in a single Wednesday morning, on someone else's terms, in the worst order, and it had cost him exactly what he'd always known it would cost him.

## ◆ iv. The Ghost Does What Ghosts Do

*Cal*

He didn't go back to training. He didn't go to the studio — the studio was burned now, photographed, a location, the one room where he'd been whole turned into evidence. He walked. Hours, through the grey city, hood up, phone off, past the warehouse where they'd shot the single and the chip shop in Mayfield and the canals he'd shown her at midnight, and somewhere in the walking the cold narrow clarity hardened into the thing he'd built the ghost out of when he was sixteen: *if you're already gone, you can't be left.* The ghost was safe. The ghost had done the leaving first.

So when he finally turned his phone on, at dark, it lit up with everything — missed calls, texts from the lads, from Knox, from Sasha (*we can fix this, call me, we control the next part of the narrative*), from Webb (*call me immediately, we manage this NOW, why didn't you TELL me*), and from Ella. Eleven messages from Ella. *are you okay. please answer. Cal it wasn't me, please believe me it wasn't me. where are you. please. I think I know how to fix it. please call me.*

He looked at her name eleven times and did the ghost thing.

Because she'd told him about the offer two days ago — no. She hadn't. She'd been holding it; he'd find that out later; that night he only knew she had an offer, that everything she wanted was downstream of his reveal, that she'd said as much, *I can't win clean.* And now the reveal had happened, from a small blog that breaks things to spite the people managing them, the kind of outlet you'd leak to if you wanted the reveal but didn't want it traced to a fixer — and her offer was about to go supernova. The timing sat in his wrecked mind and rearranged itself into the worst possible story, because that was what wrecked minds did, they were just bad producers, chopping the truth into the cruelest order: *she said she couldn't win clean unless the reveal happened. The reveal happened. And now her offer goes supernova.*

He knew, even thinking it, that it was the wrecked-mind version. He knew her. He knew she'd kept his secret when Sasha offered to make her famous, knew she'd lied to Imogen's face about who he was, knew she'd texted *it wasn't me* before he'd accused her of anything — which is what innocent people do, and also what guilty people are smart enough to do, said the cold ghost, and Cal was too far gone, too sixteen, too *don't come to the house* to tell the truth from the thing that would let him do the leaving first.

He texted her back. One line. The cruelest order he could chop it into.

*Everything you wanted is downstream of this. Congratulations. You win clean after all.*

Then he turned the phone off again, before she could reply, before she could be innocent at him, before she could see him — because being seen was the whole danger and he was done with the danger — and he walked back out into the city that wasn't theirs anymore, the ghost again, safe again, already gone, and told himself it was better this way, the way he'd told himself everything for five years, and almost believed it, which had always been the problem.

The catch had everything now. It had taken the morning too.

## ◆ v. The Knife in the Soft Place

*Ella*

She read his text in the launderette-warm flat with the dryers tumbling, and she read it four times, and each time it took something else out of her.

*Everything you wanted is downstream of this. Congratulations. You win clean after all.*

He thought she'd done it. After everything — after Jordan, after the buried track, after *I love you, for the record* in the lamplight three nights ago — he thought she'd leaked him. He'd done the cold thing, run the odds on her loyalty against her ambition and decided the ambition won, because that was how he was built, he read odds, and the odds said the girl whose whole career was downstream of the reveal had made the reveal happen.

The cruelty of it was surgical. *Congratulations.* He knew exactly where she was softest — the fear that underneath the loyalty she was, in the end, just ambitious; the thing her mother had named, *a hobby you're calling a career*; the private worry she'd never said aloud, that maybe she really would step on anyone to prove the thing she was could be a life. He'd found that exact place and put the knife in it, on purpose, because hurting her was the fastest way to make her leave first — the ghost thing, *if you're already gone you can't be left*, burning it down so the loss would be his choice instead of his fear.

She understood it completely. And understanding it didn't help at all, because the thing about being understood-out-of-cruelty is that it still cuts; the knife doesn't care that you can see the hand.

Priya came home to find her on the floor in the warm, not crying, which was worse, the strong-one not-crying she'd learned from her mother at a midnight table. "I saw the piece," Priya said carefully, lowering herself down. "The whole — is that — Ella, the producer, V, that's *Cal*? Tom's *Cal*? You've been —" the pieces landing on her face, three years of friendship recalculating. "All term. The closed door. The quiet. It was *him.* You've been with him all term and you couldn't tell me because —"

"Because it wasn't mine to tell," Ella said, flat. "It was his. His whole family's. His dead brother's. I couldn't, Pri. I wanted to. You're the first call, I promised you that, and I couldn't make it, and I'm sorry."

"His *dead* —" Priya stopped, recalibrating again, the size of it. "Okay. Okay. And the leak — does he think —" She'd read the screen over Ella's shoulder. Her face went from hurt to fury in one motion. "He thinks *you* did it? After you kept it all term? I'll kill him, I'll actually —"

"Don't." Ella's voice was a flat calm that frightened them both. "He's not — he's doing the thing he does. He lost his dad this morning, Pri. His dad said *don't come to the house.* And his whole self is built on the idea that if he leaves first he can't be left, so he's burning me down before I can — it's not about me. I know it's not about me. He's pointing it at the only person who'll stay and take it, because he can't be angry at the blog or at Sasha or at his dad or at his brother for dying, so it's me. And I told him I wouldn't be the place he puts the thing he's too scared to feel, and I did it for my whole family for twenty years, and I'm not —" her voice finally cracked, one clean fracture "— I'm not going to be it for him. Even now. Even like this."

"So what do you do," Priya said softly.

And there it was — the choice Imogen had named in a Soho coffee shop. *Most people never find out who they are because they never have to choose between what they want and who they want to be.*

Because here was the brutal arithmetic, laid bare now that the worst had happened: Cal had cut her loose. He thought she'd betrayed him. The smart thing — the strong-one thing, the protect-yourself thing, the thing her mother would counsel and her ambition would thank her for — was to take the offer. Sign it. Let the supernova carry her to London and the seat at Imogen's studio and the answer to six o'clock. Cal didn't want her anymore; he'd made that surgically clear. Her loyalty had already nearly cost her the offer's full size; why should it cost her again now, when the person she'd been loyal to had just knifed her on his way out the door?

She could hear the argument perfectly. It was a good argument. It was, in its way, the same one her whole family had made her whole life: *be safe, protect yourself, the world will not protect you, build something no one can take.*

And she found, on the floor in the warm with the worst day of her life tumbling around her, that she didn't want it. Not the safe version. Not the win that cost Cal his terms. Not the seat she'd get by riding the supernova of his betrayal. Because the thing she'd realised on the train outside Stockport was still true even now that he'd hurt her: she would not build her career on his ruin, and she would not let Sasha be the one who decided when his life detonated — and now, worse, the detonation had already happened, on the cruelest terms, and if she took the offer she'd be cashing in on it, the eye who got famous off the ghost's exposure, exactly the thing she'd sworn she wouldn't be.

"I'm going to do the thing I was going to do anyway," Ella said slowly. "Before the leak. I had a plan, Pri — I texted Sasha, I was going to take the story away from the people telling it. I'm still going to. Not for him; he's made it clear I'm not doing anything for him. For *me.* Because I refuse to let the version where I'm the villain be the version that's true, and the only way to make a different version true is to go and build it, the way I build everything." She got up off the floor, and the strong-one settled onto her — except different this time, not the exhausted mask but a thing she was choosing, the way Cal had chosen to play the second half loose. "And maybe it fixes things with Cal and maybe it never does. Maybe he never believes it wasn't me. Maybe he stays a ghost forever and I watch from London. But I'm not going to let the last true thing I did be carrying his weight in secret all term and getting knifed for it. The last thing I do in this is going to be *loud.*"

"That's the most you thing you've ever said," Priya said. "What are you actually going to do?"

Ella picked up her phone. Sasha had replied to *let's get coffee* an hour before the leak — *Always! When?* — not knowing the blog was about to take the reveal out of her hands too, not knowing her carefully seeded story had been stolen by a spiteful outlet and her own control was as gone as everyone else's.

"Sasha built a story where me and Cal had to be enemies so one of us would crack and the reveal would come. She's a producer, basically — she chops the truth into the order that serves her. And the thing about a producer," Ella said, and her eyes did the reading thing, except now three moves ahead, "is that anyone can do it. You just need command of the elements. And I've got an element nobody else has. I know who V actually is. I know about Jordan. I know what the buried track is and whose voice is in it. I know the *real* story — the one that's a thousand times bigger and truer than 'rugby boy has secret hobby.' The blog only got the shallow version, the betrayal version. So there's still a deeper version to tell." She looked at Priya. "The question is whether I've got any right to tell it. Because it's not my secret. It's his. And telling someone's deepest truth to save them is just betraying them in a nicer font — *unless* —" she stopped, the plan finishing in her head, and it was good, and it was terrifying, and it required the one thing she couldn't manufacture: "unless *he* tells it. Unless I build him a stage and a moment and a world so safe and so true that the ghost decides, on his own, to step out of the corner and cross the room. Because that's the only version where it's not me unmasking him. It's him taking off the mask. I can't make him do it. I can only build the room. The way I built the room at the party I didn't know I was building. The way I always build the room."

"And if he won't step out?"

"Then I built a room for nobody," Ella said. "And I'll have found out who I am anyway. Which Imogen says is the whole point, and which I'm starting to think is the most expensive thing there is."

She sat back down. She opened the laptop. The blue light came up onto her face the way it had the night Cal told her about Jordan, and outside the rain came down on the grey glittering city, and Eleonora Savage-Oluwa — the strong one, who had never once been strong for herself — began to build the most important world of her career for an audience of one terrified ghost who'd just told her she'd won, and might never know that the only thing she was actually trying to win was him.

## ◆ vi. The Friend Who Won the Sweepstake

*Cal*

Tom turned up at Cal's door that night — the bare flat, the room Cal only slept in — because Tom was the kind of friend who, when he couldn't reach you by phone, came and stood outside your door until you opened it, and he stood there now, enormous, filling the corridor, holding two bags of chips like a peace offering or an apology or both.

"I won the sweepstake," Tom said. "Forty quid. I'm not gonna take it. Feels — wrong. Feels like winning money off finding out my best mate's been —" he stopped, and his big honest face did the thing, the hurt-and-love. "Two years, Cal. I covered for you for two years. Every time you vanished, every weird night, the lads asking where you were and me going 'dunno, he's private,' and the whole time it was — *this.* You were making the best music in the city and you couldn't tell *me.* I'd have kept it. You know I'd have kept it. I kept everything else without even knowing what I was keeping." He held out a bag of chips. "So that's the thing that actually hurts. Not that you had a secret. That you didn't think I was safe enough to hold it."

And Cal — who had spent the day doing the ghost thing to Ella, burning down the one person who'd held the secret so he wouldn't have to wait to be left — looked at the second person he'd shut out, the friend who'd held a door for two years without ever trying the handle, and felt the full shape of what the ghost cost. Not just him. Everyone. He'd decided, alone, at sixteen, that no one was safe enough to hold the real thing, and he'd been wrong about every single one of them — Tom, who'd have kept it; Knox, who did keep it; Ella, who kept it all term and got knifed for it; his mother, who kept Jordan's whole room; his father, who'd said Jordan's name with love over a fry-up. He'd been wrong about all of them, and the wrongness had a name, and the name was the ghost.

"I'm sorry," Cal said, and it came out cracked, the daylight voice gone. "You'd have kept it. I know you would. I didn't keep it from you because you weren't safe. I kept it from everyone because *I* wasn't — I built this thing where nobody got to hold any of it, and I told myself it was to protect them and it was really so that I'd never have to —" he stopped. "Take the forty quid, Tom. You won it fair. And — thanks. For two years of not trying the handle. I didn't deserve a mate that good and I treated it like it was nothing."

Tom looked at him a long moment, then stepped in and hugged him, a vast crushing thing, and said into the top of his head, "You're an idiot, and idiocy and not-loving-people are different diagnoses, and yours is the curable one, but you have to actually *let* people, Cal. That's the cure. You have to let us." And Cal — who'd heard a version of that sentence from Ella, *let me take care of you sideways, we're both setting it down* — understood that everyone who loved him had been telling him the same thing in different scarves all spring, and that he'd waited until the worst possible day to start hearing it.

(Tom kept the forty quid. He spent it, weeks later, on the round after the chapel. But that's ahead.)

## ◆ vii. Knox, In the Wreckage

*Cal*

Knox came to the burned studio that night — because where else, it was still the only room — and found Cal sitting in the dark with the lamp off, not working, the monitors dead, the high window now a thing that had a camera pointed at it from across the street.

"They're saying I should distance myself," Knox said, dropping onto the sofa. "The label. Sasha. 'Get ahead of it, Marcus, the story's about the rugby boy now, it's eclipsing the music, protect the brand.'" He laughed, short. "Protect the brand. From the man who *made* the brand. From the four-notes-under-the-drums thing that makes my chest do the thing." He looked at Cal in the dark. "You know what I told them? I told them V's been on every record I've ever cared about, and the day I distance myself from the person who made the sound to protect the *story about the sound* is the day I'm exactly the sellout I turned the sugar feature down to not be. So no. I'm not distancing. You're my producer. You're my boy. The blog can run whatever it wants — I'll put your name in the credits *myself*, big, *Produced by Cal Vance*, and dare them to make that a scandal instead of a flex."

"You can't," Cal said. "My dad —"

"Your dad already knows, bruv. The worst version already happened. That's the thing about the worst version —" Knox leaned forward "— once it's happened, the thing you were protecting is already gone, and the only choice left is whether you let the worst version be the *final* version, or whether you go and tell a better one. The blog told 'rugby boy has shameful secret.' That's the worst version and it's out. But it's not the *true* version, and it's definitely not the *last* one — unless you let it be." He stood. "I've known you since we were sixteen, Cal. I know there's a why. I've always known there's a why. And whatever it is — the thing you've never told me — I think today's the day it stops being a secret you protect and starts being a story you get to tell. Your call. But I'm not distancing. I'm putting your name on it. Soon as you say the word. And if you never say the word, I'll keep being a ghost's artist forever, and I'll mean it, because that's what loyalty is." He left the chips Tom-style on the desk. "Eat something. And call the stylist. Whatever happened there — I saw your faces all spring, man, I'm not blind — whatever you did today, fix it. She kept your secret when Sasha offered to make her famous off it. I was in the room. She said no to her own launch to protect you. Whatever the blog made you think — that's not a person who'd leak you. Do the maths properly."

And Knox left, and Cal sat in the dark with two friends' loyalty stacked on the desk like chips going cold, and Knox's last line working at the wrecked arithmetic — *she said no to her own launch to protect you, do the maths properly* — and for the first time since the cruelest text, the cold ghost certainty cracked, just a hairline, just enough to let one true thing through: *I knifed her. She didn't leak me. I knew she didn't even as I sent it. I did the ghost thing to the one person who never once gave me a reason to.*

But knowing it and undoing it were different, and Cal was still too far down, too *don't come to the house*, to do anything but sit in the dark with the knowledge, which is its own circle of the black hour: to see exactly what you've broken and not yet have the strength to reach for it.

## ◆ viii. The Centre of Gravity Is a Scandal

*Ella*

The degree show was in three weeks, and Ella's entire final project — the most ambitious in the cohort, the real-client bet, the back-wall thing the externals had asked about by name — had, overnight, acquired a centre of gravity that was now a tabloid-adjacent scandal.

Dr. Annan called her in. Dry, exacting, impossible to read. "The Knox identity work. Your final project." A pause. "You're aware the artist's producer has become — a story. The externals read the trades. There's a version of the next three weeks where your project is 'the standout real-world work in the cohort,' and a version where it's 'the student whose project got tangled in *that* mess.' Same work. Different frame. And the frame, as you of all people know, Savage-Oluwa, is not decoration — the frame is the whole thing." Annan looked at her over the glasses. "Margot, meanwhile, has a flawless fictional perfumery that cannot be tangled in anything, because it is about nothing and nobody, which is its weakness and, this month, its strength. So. I'll ask you what I'd ask any creative director facing a story they didn't choose: what's your move?"

And Ella — who'd spent her whole life being handed frames other people built — heard, in Dr. Annan's dry question, the same thing Imogen had named and Sasha had weaponised and Knox had survived: *whoever frames the story owns what it means.* The blog had framed it as scandal. Sasha would frame it as supernova. Margot's silence framed Ella's risk as recklessness. And every one of those frames was someone else holding the story.

"My move," Ella said slowly, "is that I'm not going to let the blog be the frame. The project was always about a secret that got out — I built the whole identity on that idea, *a secret that got out, beautiful and a kind of grief.* I just didn't know I was describing the literal future. So the frame isn't 'student tangled in a mess.' The frame is: this is the *only* project in the show that's about a real living truth happening in real time, with real stakes, and I'm going to direct how it's seen the way I'd direct anything — completely. I'm not going to hide the project from the story. I'm going to make the project *the* place the story gets told right."

Dr. Annan looked at her for a long moment, and almost — almost — smiled. "Margot will get the first job," she said. "Polish always does. But you, Savage-Oluwa, are going to have the career, because polish runs from a story it didn't choose and nerve *re-frames* it. Don't tank it. And —" as Ella reached the door "— whatever you're building. The thing behind the project, the real one. The externals can smell a person who's only protecting herself, and they can smell a person protecting something bigger. Be the second one. It's the only thing in this industry that's worth anything in twenty years." Which was almost word for word what Imogen had said, two tutors of taste arriving at the same verdict from three hundred miles apart, and Ella walked out into the rain having been told, twice now, by two people who hired for a living, that the thing she was about to do — build a room for a ghost who'd knifed her, refuse the safe win, protect something bigger than herself — was not the naïve thing her fear called it. It was the only thing that lasted.

Margot found her in the corridor, and for once didn't needle. "I saw the trades," she said. "Your producer. Grim, the way they did it — through a window, his name, no consent. That's not a reveal, that's a mugging." A pause, and then, unexpectedly, the realest thing Margot had ever said to her: "My perfumery can't be mugged because there's nobody in it. You bet on a real person and now a real person's getting hurt and it splashes on you, and everyone on the course is quietly glad it's you and not them. I'm not. I think what you did — betting on something real — was braver than anything in my immaculate empty bottle, and I think you're going to find a way to turn this that none of us could, because you see the thing the rest of us can't." She shrugged, the armour coming back up. "Or you'll crash and I'll get the front wall *and* the career. But I don't think so. Annoyingly." And she walked off, and Ella stood in the corridor having been, for the second time, granted grudging knighthood by a rival — and added it to the small pile of evidence she was gathering, scene by scene, that the loud true thing she was about to build might actually be the right one.

## ◆ ix. Coffee With the Producer of People

*Ella*

She met Sasha in a bright place in the Northern Quarter, two days after the leak, and she went in having done the thing she did before every room that mattered: she'd built the world first. She knew exactly what she'd say and the order she'd say it in and which silence she'd let sit. She was not, this time, going to be the one being read.

"Terrible business, the leak," Sasha said, all warmth, no eyes. "We'd have done it so much better. A managed reveal, a narrative, dignity. This little blog just — mugged him. Crude." She sipped. "But it's out now, so the question is what we do with it. And the good news, Ella, genuinely — for you — is that 'out' is still 'out.' The album's spiking. Your name's in every piece. The supernova's happening anyway, just uglier than I'd have made it. You could still ride it. The eye who built the ghost's world. The offer in London gets bigger by the day this story runs. You did nothing wrong and you get everything. That's a clean outcome for you, whatever it is for him."

And Ella looked at Sasha — the producer of people, who chopped the truth into the order that served her, who'd built a story where Ella and Cal had to be enemies so one would crack — and she understood that Sasha genuinely thought this was a kindness, genuinely could not conceive of a person who'd refuse a clean win that landed in their lap, because Sasha had spent so long managing stories that she'd forgotten people weren't elements.

"Here's the thing, Sasha," Ella said, calm, the world she'd built unrolling. "You're a producer. Not of music — of *stories.* You take the elements and you cut them into the order that serves the rollout. It's a real skill. I do the same thing with worlds. So I'm going to talk to you producer to producer." She set her cup down. "Your story — 'who is V, the secret rugby boy' — is the *shallow* version. It's the worst cut of the footage. And it's out, and it's ugly, and it's eclipsing the music, which means it's failing even by your metrics, because nobody's streaming a song to find out about a contract dispute. The story's burning the thing it was supposed to sell." She let that land. "I have the deep version. The true one. I know why there's no name on the work. I know what's buried in the tracks and whose it is. And it's a story a thousand times bigger and truer and more streamable than yours — it's about grief, and loyalty, and a person who turned a private loss into a gift for half a million strangers and never asked for credit. That's not a scandal. That's the best music story of the year. And I'm going to tell it — not as a leak, not as an unmasking, but the right way, on the right terms, at the right moment — and it's going to make your shallow version look like exactly what it is, which is a mugging through a window."

Sasha's smile didn't move, but her eyes did, finally — the inventory stopping, the recalculation. "You're going to reveal him. Properly. For your own launch. After all the loyalty." Something almost like respect. "I underestimated you. You're more ruthless than I —"

"No," Ella said. "That's the part you can't see, because you're a producer and to a producer everything's an element. I'm not revealing him. *He* is — if he chooses to, which he might not, and if he doesn't, the deep version never gets told and you're stuck with your ugly shallow one forever. I'm just building the room. And I'm telling you to your face so that when it happens you don't get to frame it as yours. You don't own this story anymore, Sasha. You lost it to a spiteful blog, and now I'm taking what's left of it somewhere you can't follow, because the one element you never had and never will is the *truth*, and it turns out the truth is the only thing that can't be produced. It can only be told. By the person it belongs to." She stood. "Thanks for the coffee. The offer in London — tell whoever's asking — is irrelevant to all of this. I haven't decided about it and it has nothing to do with what I'm about to build. I wanted that on the record."

And she walked out into the rain, having, for the first time in the whole story, been the one who framed the room instead of the one framed by it — and behind her Sasha sat with a cooling coffee and the unfamiliar sensation of having been out-produced by a twenty-one-year-old who'd just calmly informed her that the truth was an element she'd never be able to buy.

## ◆ x. The Bottom

*Cal*

The club went quiet.

Not a dramatic withdrawal — worse, a professional one. Webb called, grim, no relish left: "The due-diligence team flagged it. The contract's not dead, but it's *paused*, pending — they used the phrase 'reputational clarity.' They want to see how the story settles. Which means they want to see if you're a liability or a man with an interesting backstory, and right now the blog's framing says liability, and the frame is the thing, Cal, you know the frame is the thing." A pause. "I told you. I begged you, on four separate coffees, to tell me so I could get ahead of it. I'd have built the rollout, made it a strength — *the fly-half with the secret artistry, depth, dimension, the thinking man's ten.* Instead a blog built 'distracted prospect with a double life,' and now I'm fighting their frame instead of writing my own, from behind, with one hand." His voice softened, the sad *mm* made into words. "This is the cost of the secret you wouldn't let me hold. Not the music. The *secrecy.* The music was never the problem. A club would kill for a fly-half with that kind of inner life. The problem was that you made it a thing that could be *discovered* instead of a thing you'd *told*, and discovered things look like liabilities and told things look like depth, and that's the whole difference, and it's the difference you had control over right up until 6 a.m. on Wednesday."

And his father didn't call. That was the bottom, under the bottom. *Don't come to the house* had been the wound, but the silence after it was worse — days of it, his father not calling, not texting, the fixed star of the touchline gone dark for the first time since Cal was six. He'd had his father's cold attention his whole life and resented its weight and now it was gone and the absence was so much heavier than the weight had ever been. He drafted texts and deleted them. *Dad. Dad, please. Dad, I can explain, it's not what you think, it's the opposite of what you think, the music is Jordan, the music is the thing you saw in the second half, it's not the thing that killed him it's the thing that — * Delete. Because how do you text that. How do you put five years and a dead brother and a buried voice note into a thing that fits on a screen, to a man who'd said *don't come to the house* in the wrecked voice he'd last used the night Jordan died.

So Cal sat in the burned studio with the camera pointed at the window and the contract paused and his father silent and Ella knifed and gone, and did the full ghost retreat — the thing he'd built at sixteen, *if you're already gone you can't be left*, except now he'd done the leaving so thoroughly that there was no one left to leave him, he'd cleared the room himself, and the safety of it was the loneliest thing he had ever felt, a man standing in the exact centre of the disaster he'd spent five years engineering precisely so it could never surprise him. He'd been right. That was the horror. The catch had come, exactly as he'd always known it would, and he'd been so completely prepared to lose everyone that he'd lost them pre-emptively, on schedule, by his own hand, and the preparation hadn't protected him from the pain at all — it had just made sure he was alone for it.

He played the voice note that night. The real one. *Listen to the bit at the end, I think I cracked it, tell me I cracked it.* Jordan's laugh, alive, certain, twenty-two forever. And Cal sat in the dark and said, out loud, to the empty studio, to the bit at the end, "I cracked it and then I broke it. I broke all of it, Jords. I did the thing. I left everyone first so they couldn't leave me, and it worked, and I'm completely alone, and it turns out that's not safe, it's just — alone." And the track climbed, three notes, and fell, one, the way it always had, the small heartbreak of the drop, and for the first time in five years Cal heard the drop not as grief but as instruction — *the fall is the point, the fall is where the feeling lives, you taught me that, the note that falls is the one that means it* — and somewhere in the dark, at the very bottom, the ghost began, without his permission, to want to climb.

## ◆ xi. The Original Ghost

*Cal*

He went south. Not to the house — *don't come to the house* still rang in him like a struck bell — but to the other house, the small flat in Catford, because the ghost had run out of rooms in Manchester and there was one place left in his life he hadn't been thrown out of, and it was Jordan's.

His mother kept it. That was the thing nobody in the Vance family talked about: that Cal's mother, who'd left when Cal was fourteen, unable to live inside the way Gareth turned grief into a project, had nonetheless been the one to keep Jordan's room intact — the decks, the milk crate of records, the foam tiles half-falling off the walls, the spider plant somehow still alive. Two graves for the same brother, and neither parent had ever told the other they kept one.

"You look like him," his mother said, opening the door, and then she saw his face and didn't say anything else, just stepped back and let him in, the way Ella let him in, the way the people who actually loved him always knew not to crowd the wound.

He sat on the floor of Jordan's room where he'd sat as a kid while his brother made the air into shapes, and his mother sat in the doorway, and the spider plant did its slow green thing, and Cal told her. All of it. The studio, the name, the streams, the buried voice note in every track. The match, the rail, the truth swallowed. The leak, the photo, *don't come to the house.* Ella — the girl who saw all of him — and the cruelest text, the knife in the soft place, the ghost thing done to the one person who'd never given him a reason.

His mother listened the whole way through, the way you listen to someone confessing to a crime they committed against themselves. When he finished, she said, "You've made yourself into a ghost. You think I don't recognise it? I'm the original ghost of this family. I left your father because he turned Jordan's death into a *project* — he couldn't feel it, so he managed it, poured it all into making you the safe son so the loss would mean something. And I couldn't watch him do it, so I left, and leaving was its own ghost move — the same one you just did to that girl. *Leave first so you can't be left.* It runs in us, baby. It's the family disease."

"Does it work?" Cal asked. Small. The smallest he'd ever been.

"No," his mother said. "That's what twenty years of being the family ghost taught me. You can't get out ahead of loss by leaving first. All you do is lose people while they're still alive. I left your father and he was *still there to lose* — I just lost him on purpose, on my own schedule, so it would hurt when I chose instead of when it chose. That's not safety. That's grief you administer to yourself in advance, every day, forever, so it can't ambush you. It's the worst deal there is and I signed it for twenty years and I'm only telling you now because I can see you signing the exact same one, and I would rather die than watch you spend your twenties the way I spent my forties — safe, and alone, and certain you arranged it that way for good reasons."

She came and sat beside him on Jordan's floor — the first time she'd touched him in he couldn't remember how long. "The girl," she said. "Did she leak you?"

"No. I knew it even when I sent the text. She kept it all term. She said no to her own launch. She texted *it wasn't me* before I'd even — she knew me well enough to predict the cruelty before I did it. And I did it anyway. I did the family thing. I pointed it at the one person who'd stay."

"Yes," his mother said gently. "We do. The question isn't whether you did it. You did. The question is whether you're going to be the kind of ghost who knows he's a ghost and does nothing — like me, for twenty years — or the kind who finds out he's a ghost and decides to come back to life, which is harder and more humiliating and the only thing worth doing." She squeezed his hand. "You crossed a room once. At a party, you said. You knew she'd see through you and you crossed it anyway. That's the least ghost thing a person can do. You've got it in you. You just have to do it again — except this time everyone's watching, and you've already wronged her, and there's no guarantee, and *that's* the brave version. The party was easy."

"And Dad?"

His mother was quiet a moment. "He said *don't come to the house.* He didn't say *never speak to me.* And he said it from inside the worst sound he's made since the night Jordan — Cal, I was married to him eighteen years, I know the difference: that wasn't shame. You heard it as shame because you've braced for shame for five years. But that was *fear.* He's not afraid the music will waste you. He's afraid it'll *take* you, the way it took Jordan, because in his head the music is the road his son died on. *Don't come to the house* isn't 'I'm ashamed of you.' It's 'I can't look at the thing that killed your brother living in the face of the son I've got left, not yet, give me a minute, I'm drowning.'" She held his face the way Ella did. "If you want him back, you can't wait for him to cross the room — he doesn't know how, your father, he's the worst coward about feeling there ever was. You'll have to do what Jordan never got the chance to do: show him the music isn't the road that kills. Show him it's the thing that made the pass different in the second half. You already know it is. You told me. He drove three hundred miles to watch you do the Jordan thing on a pitch and didn't know that's what he was watching. So show him. That's the climb. It's the only one worth making."

And Cal sat on the floor of one of his brother's two graves, the spider plant green in the Catford window, and felt the ghost thing start to lift — not gone, never gone, it ran in the family — but lifting enough that he could see, underneath it, the whole of what he'd thrown away in a single Wednesday: the girl who built rooms, the father who couldn't feel, the brother who'd cracked it and never got told, the friends who'd held doors. And slowly, at the very bottom, he began to do the only productive thing available at the bottom, which was plan the climb.

"I have to fix it with Ella," he said. "I have to call her, I have to —"

"No," his mother said. "You have to do better than call her. You knifed her in the softest place she has. A call is just more words from the boy who used words to hurt her. You built a wall and gave her the only key and then accused her of using it against you — you don't fix that with a phone call, baby. You broke it where it cost her, and you have to mend it somewhere it costs *you*, somewhere she can see it cost you. She builds rooms, you said — worlds where people can be seen safely. So build her one back. Build her the thing she's spent her whole life building for everyone else and never once getting for herself. Stop being the ghost in the corner. Cross the room where everyone can watch you do it. That's the only apology the same size as the wound."

## ◆ xii. The Six O'Clock She Finally Took

*Ella*

While Cal was on a floor in Catford, Ella did her own version of the climb, which was to finally, at six o'clock, tell her mother the truth — the whole truth, the boy on top of the career — because she'd learned, at a sink three weeks before, that the true thing said out loud is lighter, and she was carrying too much to keep carrying it alone.

"There's a boy, Mum," she said, into the 6:02 call. "Was. Is. I don't know. The producer — the one in the news, the rugby one, V — that's him. We were together all term. In secret. And it's a long story and most of it isn't mine to tell, but he's — his brother died, and he's spent five years hiding, and he just got exposed in the worst way and he lashed out and accused me of doing it, which I'd never, and we're — broken, I think. And I have an offer in London, a real one, the thing I always wanted, and the only way it gets as big as it could is if I cash in on his worst day, and I'm not going to, and I don't fully know why I'm telling you all this except that I'm so tired of holding things by myself and you held things by yourself for twenty-five years and I thought maybe —" her voice cracked "— maybe you'd know how you do it. How you carry something this heavy without it crushing you."

A long silence on the line. Lewisham reaching north.

"You don't carry it without it crushing you," her mother said finally. Quiet. The realest she'd ever been. "That is the lie I told you children, with my body, at that kitchen table — that the strong one carries it and is fine. I was not fine, Eleonora. I was crushed, every night, and I performed not-crushed so you would not be frightened, and you watched, and you learned the wrong lesson — you learned that being crushed in secret is strength. It is not. It is just being alone in the worst place." A breath. "The thing I should have shown you, instead of the not-crying, is your father's hand on the table. Do you remember? Next to mine. Not holding — just *there.* That was the real thing. Not me being strong. Him being *there,* so I was not alone in it. That is the only way you carry something this heavy, omo mi. Not by being stronger. By not being alone. So." Her mother's voice did the thing it had done after the party, went somewhere it rarely went. "This boy. The offer. The right thing. Do not carry it alone to look strong for me — I am done being someone you perform strength for. Tell me which one keeps you up. Then we will look at it together, the way your father and I looked at the letters. That is what a mother is for. Not to approve. To put her hand on the table."

And Ella sat in the launderette-warm dark and cried, properly, the real kind, the not-strong kind, because her mother had just handed her, at fifty, the thing she'd needed since she was nine: permission to set the weight down in the middle, where someone could see it. And she told her mother the whole shape of the plan — the room she was going to build for the ghost, the film, the refusing of the supernova — and her mother listened, and did not tell her it was wise, and did not tell her it was foolish, and at the end said only, "Both names. On whatever you build. Spelled right. And — Eleonora. The boy. If he is worth this, he will come and stand where you can see it cost him. And if he does not come, you will have built a true thing anyway, and you will not be alone in it, because I am here now, with my hand on the table. Either way you are not alone. That is the only thing I needed twenty-five years to learn and I am giving it to you at twenty-one so you do not have to wait. Now go and build your room."

## ◆ xiii. What the Wreckage Showed

*Ella*

The break didn't happen in a vacuum — it happened in the middle of everyone's ordinary lives, and the wreckage lit up the people around her like a flare lights a field, showing Ella exactly who they were.

Priya moved in close, the way she hadn't been let to all term. She cancelled plans. She slept on the floor of Ella's room the first two nights when Ella couldn't be alone with the dryers and the not-crying. She didn't say *I told you there was a door*; she said *I'm here, twelve feet, I always was, point at me whenever you need to* — and Ella, who'd rationed this friend all term to guard the secret, finally stopped rationing, told her everything, the whole story, Jordan and the buried notes and the chip shop and the cruelest text, and Priya held it the way Ella had held Cal's, without trying to lift it too soon, and the friendship came back not just to where it had been but *deeper*, because a secret that nearly broke it had been replaced by the truth that mends. (That was the thing the wreckage showed about Priya: that the friend you protect by lying to is always a smaller thing than the friend you trust with the real load. Ella had had the order backwards all term. The wreckage corrected it.)

Tom, improbably, helped most. He came round with Priya, enormous and gentle, and he didn't take a side — he loved them both, Cal and Ella, and he said so — but he told Ella one thing, sitting on her floor with a mug that looked like a thimble in his hand: "He did the thing he does. The vanishing. Except this time he vanished *at* you instead of just away. That's still vanishing. It's the only move he's got when he's scared — go where you can't be reached. He did it to me for two years, just quieter. It's not about you. It's the thing that's wrong with *him*, the thing he's had since his brother — and I only just found out about the brother, today, and it explains the whole of him, every weird year I've known him." He looked at her with his big honest face. "I'm not telling you to forgive him. I'm telling you the diagnosis, because you're a fixer, and a fixer needs the right diagnosis. He's not a man who stopped loving you. He's a man who loves you and got scared and ran *at* you to make you leave so he wouldn't have to wait for it. Whether that's forgivable is your call. But that's what it is. I'd put my forty quid on it."

And Ella, fixer that she was, took the diagnosis and turned it over and found it matched her own read exactly — *he's pointing it at the only person who'll stay, because he can't be angry at the people who actually hurt him* — and the matching of it, the confirmation from the one person who'd known Cal longest, was the last piece she needed. Not to forgive him. To understand that the room she was building had a chance of having an occupant — that the ghost might, if the room were safe enough and true enough, cross it.

So she built. She started that night — the laptop, the blue light on her face, the world assembling. Not a campaign. A *film.* Eight minutes, to premiere at — she'd work out where; somewhere that mattered; somewhere public — that would take the shallow leaked story and tell the deep true one *without ever naming him*, so that it reframed everything and revealed nothing, so that the ghost could step out of it if he chose and stay hidden inside it if he didn't. A room with a door he could walk through or not. The most important thing she'd ever make, for an audience of one, who might never come.

She did not, that night, decide about the offer. But she knew, building the film, that she was choosing — in real time, the way Imogen said you find out who you are — and that the choosing felt, for the first time in her life, like the opposite of carrying. It felt like setting the weight down in the middle, where her mother's hand was on the table, where Priya slept on the floor, where the work was, finally, hers.

## ◆ xiv. Two Climbs, Two Cities

*Cal & Ella*

They were, in the black hour, three hundred miles apart and doing the same thing without knowing it, which is the cruel comedy of people who are right for each other and broken at the same time: each privately deciding to climb, neither able to tell the other, both building toward the same point from opposite ends of the country.

In Catford, on his brother's floor, Cal made a plan he told no one — not even his mother, fully. *Build her a room back. Cross the room where everyone can watch. The same size as the wound.* He didn't know yet that Ella was building a film; he only knew his mother was right, that an apology the size of his betrayal couldn't be a phone call, that he had to do the most ghost-defying thing imaginable — step out of the corner, into the light, in public, on the line — and that somehow it had to be both an apology to Ella and the truth to his father and the thing Jordan never got to do, all at once, in one impossible crossing. He didn't have the *how.* He had only the resolve, forming at the bottom, and Knox's offer ringing in him (*I'll put your name on it the second you say the word*), and the buried voice note, and the fall that he'd finally heard as instruction instead of grief.

In Hulme, on her floor, Ella built the room — the film, the world, the door with no name on it — for the ghost who'd knifed her, knowing he might never come, choosing to build it anyway, because that was who she'd decided to be, the person who builds the true loud thing even when the safe quiet win is sitting folded in her bag.

And between them, in the dark, the break sat total and real. They did not call each other. They did not text. The cruelest message stayed the last message, *congratulations, you win clean after all*, unanswered, a wound with the knife still in it. By every visible measure they were finished — a secret romance detonated, an accusation made and unretracted, two people retreating to the safe scripts they were raised on, his ghost and her strong-one, the exact failure they'd both feared.

But underneath the visible measure, in the place where the real things happen, two climbs had begun. He was going to build her a crossing. She was going to build him a room. Neither knew about the other's. And the whole question of the rest of the story — the only question left at the bottom of the black hour — was whether the room and the crossing would arrive at the same place, at the same time, in front of the same people, or whether they'd miss each other in the dark, two true things built three hundred miles apart by two people too proud and too wounded and too alike to pick up a phone.

That was the reckoning. Not the leak, not the break, not the knife. The reckoning was this: that they had both, finally, at the very bottom, decided to stop being a ghost and a strong-one and to build something true and loud and terrifying — and that they had decided it *separately*, in two cities, in silence, which is the most human and most heartbreaking way two people who love each other ever almost save themselves.

The orange rain came down on both cities. The dryers tumbled in Hulme. The spider plant did its slow green thing in Catford. And two rooms began to be built, three hundred miles apart, toward a point neither builder could yet see — and whether they'd meet there is the next chapter, and the last, and the only one that was ever really in doubt.

*That was Chapter Four — Reckoning. The catch fell, exactly as feared: the offer made enormous by the ruin, the leak through a window, the worst-order exposure, the father's wrecked "don't come to the house," the ghost doing what ghosts do, the knife in the softest place. And then, at the bottom, in two cities, two climbs begun in silence — a room and a crossing, built apart, toward a point not yet visible. Continues, and concludes, in Chapter Five — Midnight in Manchester.*
