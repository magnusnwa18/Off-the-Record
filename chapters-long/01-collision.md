> *Long chapter · target ~45,000 words · built progressively. Scenes marked with ◆.*

## ◆ i. The Loudest Room

*Ella*

The house on Egerton Road had been sweating since ten o'clock, and by midnight the walls had given up pretending to be walls. Bass came up through the floorboards and into the soles of Ella's boots, into her teeth, into the hollow under her ribs where she kept the things she didn't say out loud. Somebody had hung fairy lights from the picture rail and somebody else had pulled half of them down, so the front room flickered like a signal going out, and in the strobing dark of it three years of Fallowfield student life were compressed into one heaving organism — second-years and final-years and a few brave masters students, the union crowd and the society crowd and the crowd that belonged to no crowd, all of them shoulder to shoulder in a terraced house built in 1900 for a family of six and currently containing approximately ninety.

Ella catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, because it was the only way she knew how to stand in a room: by silhouette and palette and the small tells of who each person was trying to be. The boy in the doorway in the deliberately ugly jumper, performing the specific carelessness that takes forty minutes in front of a mirror. The girls from the fashion society reposting each other's stories in real time, building a record of a good night they were too busy documenting to have. The lad from her seminar who had once spent forty minutes explaining her own course to her and was now explaining something to a girl who had the trapped, polite expression of prey. Ella saw all of it. She always saw all of it. It was her gift and it was her exile, the watching — it meant she was never quite *in* a room, always a half-step outside it, the stylist's distance, the eye that frames rather than enters.

"You're doing the face," Priya shouted into her ear.

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing the face that says you're already writing the text you're going to send me tomorrow about how you should have stayed in." Priya pressed a warm can into Ella's hand and beamed at her, lipstick immaculate, eyeliner immaculate, the whole of her assembled with a confidence Ella envied and had, in point of fact, styled half of — the slip dress was Priya's but the layering over it, the boots, the single architectural earring, those were Ella, a Tuesday-night favour that had taken twenty minutes and that Priya had worn to three events since. "One hour. Give me one hour of being a person and then you can go home and be a genius."

"I'm always a person."

"You're always a *project manager*. There's a difference."

That landed harder than Priya meant it to, the way the truest things from your best friend always did, casual as a dropped glass. Ella looked away so it wouldn't show on her face and let the comment slide down into the hollow under her ribs to be examined later, at three in the morning, when she examined everything. *Project manager.* Was it an insult? Priya hadn't meant it as one. But it sat too close to a thing Ella already suspected about herself — that somewhere in the long years of being the eldest, the strong one, the one who handled it, she had quietly stopped being a person who *had* experiences and become a person who *managed* them, who stood at the side of her own life with a clipboard making sure everyone else's went smoothly.

"One hour," she said. "Then I'm gone and you don't get to guilt me."

"Deal." Priya was already turning, already gone, swallowed by the crowd toward the kitchen.

Because that was the thing about this particular party. It was a *crossover* — Priya had started seeing one of the rugby lads, Tom, a lock the approximate dimensions of a wardrobe, who laughed like a faulty boiler and who, to be fair, looked at Priya like she had personally invented the sun. And where Tom went, the academy went, filling doorways like an occupying army, drinking with the focused efficiency of professional athletes on their one sanctioned night off the leash, taking up more room than physics should have allowed. The party was therefore two parties pretending to be one: the art-and-fashion ecosystem Ella moved through, all texture and irony and carefully assembled poverty, and the sport ecosystem, all protein and certainty and the particular loudness of young men who have been told they are special since they were eleven years old.

Ella had opinions about the second category. She'd had the opinions for three years without needing to meet a single specimen up close. Boys who walked through the world like it was a pitch and everyone else was the crowd. She'd grown up around enough manufactured certainty — her cousins, the boys at church, the entire architecture of expectation she'd been raised inside — to recognise the type on sight and to know that the confidence was usually a thing other people had installed in them rather than a thing they had earned.

She found a wall and made it hers, can sweating in her hand, and let the party happen at her like weather.

That was when she clocked him.

It wasn't that he was loud. That was the first thing that snagged, actually — that he wasn't. The kitchen was loud, the boys around him were loud, a great churning engine of noise and shoulder-slaps and someone doing something with a bottle opener that was making everyone roar, and at the dead centre of it he was *still*. Leaning against the counter with a bottle he wasn't drinking from, head tipped, apparently listening to whatever one of his mates was bellowing into the side of his face — but his eyes were going round the room the whole time. Slow. Methodical. Left to right and back, taking inventory, the way Ella's own eyes went round a room. Reading it.

She felt the recognition before she understood it: *that's what I do.* That precise quality of being present in a room and absent from it at the same time, the watcher's exile. She had never once seen it on anyone else. People who weren't watching didn't recognise it; people who were watching were too busy watching to be caught at it. But she caught him, because she was doing the identical thing from the opposite wall, and for one disorienting second it was like looking into a mirror that had been set up across a crowded room.

He had the kind of face the camera was going to love and probably already did — sharp at the jaw, a nose that had been broken at least once and set just imperfectly enough to be interesting, dark hair pushed back off his forehead and curling at the nape where he'd let it grow past whatever regulation governed boys like him. Tall, in the specific way of someone assembled for a purpose: not the gym-built bulk of vanity but the functional architecture of a body that *did* something, shoulders and forearms and a way of holding still that suggested enormous held power, a coiled economy. He wore a plain black tee and dark jeans and absolutely nothing else that asked for attention, which was its own kind of asking, and Ella's stylist brain noted it before her better judgement could intervene: *he knows exactly how he looks and he has chosen to look like he hasn't thought about it.* The hardest look to pull off. The look that costs the most to make appear free.

His gaze finished its circuit of the room and arrived at her.

She didn't look away. She'd learned a long time ago that looking away first was a kind of apology, and she was done apologising for taking up space — she'd done enough apologising for it in her first year, when the accents in seminars had made her feel like a guest in a building she was paying to be in, and she'd promised herself somewhere in second year that she would never again be the one who dropped her eyes first. So she held it, eyebrow lifting a fraction, *and?*, and watched something flicker across his face — surprise, maybe, that she hadn't done the thing that girls apparently did when he looked at them, and then, infuriatingly, the slow arrival of a smile. Not a wide one. A private one. Like she had just confirmed something he'd been wondering about, like she was the answer to a question he'd been asking the whole room and she was the first one to come back right.

He pushed off the counter.

"Oh, no," Ella murmured to her can.

"Don't move," Priya hissed, appearing at her elbow from nowhere, vibrating at a frequency Ella could feel in her fillings. "Don't you *dare* move, that's Cal."

"That's a sentence that assumes I know who Cal is."

"Callum Vance. Fly-half. *The* fly-half. Tom says scouts come to watch him, like actual professional scouts, like he's going to be —" Priya did a complicated two-handed gesture meant to convey enormity, oceans, the future. "He never talks to anyone at these things. That's like his whole thing. He turns up, he stands there being —"

"Aloof?"

"I was going to say *fit*, but —"

He arrived.

Up close he was worse, which Ella registered with a flat sort of internal dismay, the dismay of a woman who has a deadline and a dwindling overdraft and a mother who calls at six o'clock and absolutely no room in the budget of her life for the particular trouble currently walking toward her. Up close she could see that the stillness wasn't an act, or wasn't only an act; there was a real attention behind his eyes, an unsettling focus that he turned on her now — not the slow inventory he'd run over the room but something narrower and more total, as though, having identified her as the one anomaly in his survey, he was now devoting the entire instrument to her alone.

"You're not having a good time," he said.

No hello. No name. Just that, delivered low enough that she had to lean in half an inch to catch it over the bass, which she suspected was precisely the point — the engineered intimacy of forcing someone to come closer to hear you.

"I'm having a fine time."

"You're having a *fine* time against a wall, drinking something you don't like —" his eyes flicked to the can, to her hand around it, the untouched weight of it "— leaving in, what." He glanced at nothing, at the middle distance, performing a calculation. "Forty minutes. Because that's the deal you made with her." He tilted his head a precise few degrees toward Priya, who had developed an expression of such delighted, transparent, project-managing matchmaking that Ella wanted to fold her into a small box and post her somewhere far away. "Am I close?"

It was a good trick. She'd give him that. It was the kind of cold reading that worked because it was ninety percent flattery wearing the costume of insight: *I see you, you're complicated, you're not like the others, no one else in this room gets it but I do.* She'd had it tried on her before, usually by men who wanted credit for noticing she was a person, who treated basic perception as a gift they were bestowing. And the thing that made it dangerous — the thing that made it work on the girls who fell for it — was that it was always *partly true*, and the partly-true part made you forget to interrogate the performance wrapped around it.

So she interrogated the performance.

"That's a lot of certainty," Ella said, "for someone who's known me eleven seconds."

"I'm good at reading rooms."

"You're good at reading the *odds*. There's a difference." She let that sit, watched it land, watched the private smile change shape. "You picked the one girl in the room who isn't already looking at you. That's not insight, that's risk management. If I tell you to get lost, you've lost nothing — you didn't want me, you wanted the read to be right, and being told to get lost still proves you read me correctly, so you win. And if I don't tell you to get lost, you get to be the one man perceptive enough to see something everyone else walked past. It's a clean bet. There's no outcome where you lose. You've structured the whole approach so that you can't." She tilted her head, returning his own gesture to him with interest. "Which means it was never actually about me. It was about being right. You'd have run the exact same play on whichever girl in this room was standing alone looking unimpressed. I'm not a person to you yet. I'm a favourable position."

For a second nothing happened. The party roared on around the small pocket of stillness the two of them had made, a held breath in the middle of the noise, and Priya had gone very quiet in the manner of someone watching a car approach a cliff in slow motion and being unable to decide whether to shout.

Then Cal laughed.

It was a real one — surprised out of him, short and low and warmer than anything else about him had suggested, and it changed his whole face for half a second into someone younger and less guarded and frankly more dangerous, before the shutter came back down over it. People nearby turned at the sound, Ella noticed, the way you turn when a usually silent animal makes a noise. *He never talks to anyone at these things.* He apparently also never laughed at them.

"All right," he said. "Fair."

"That's it? Fair?"

"You're right. So — fair." He set his untouched bottle down on the windowsill behind her, deliberately, a small surrender of the prop, and for one absurd moment they were close enough that she could smell him under the spilled-beer fug of the room: something clean and warm and out of place here, cedar maybe, the expensive kind of understatement, the smell equivalent of the plain black tee. "But you got one thing wrong."

"Did I."

"You said there's no outcome where I lose. There was one." His eyes did the thing again, the narrowing, the full instrument, except this time there was nothing detached in it at all, and Ella felt it go through her like the bass through the floor, unwelcome, undeniable, a physical fact she resented in real time. "The outcome where I came over and you turned out to be exactly as interesting as I thought you'd be from across the room. That one costs me. Because now I have to think about it." A beat. "I knew you'd see through the play. I came over anyway. You can decide for yourself what that tells you."

And then — and this was the part that would keep her up later, replaying it with a kind of furious unwilling admiration — he didn't wait. He didn't lean in for whatever the kill was supposed to be, didn't ask for her number, didn't do any of the things the entire approach had appeared to be building toward. He just nodded, once, the way you nod at someone who has taught you something you didn't expect to learn at a party in Fallowfield, and he turned and went back to the kitchen, back into the noise, back to the boys who immediately swallowed him into their churning orbit — and he did not look at her again.

Not once. She watched the back of his head for a full minute to confirm it, and it never turned, and the not-turning was somehow louder than anything he'd said.

"Okay," Priya breathed, reverent, scandalised. "*Okay.* What was that."

"That was a boy who's used to the last move working." Ella's heart was doing something irritating and arrhythmic and she resented it specifically and personally and filed a complaint with herself about it. "And who changed his entire strategy the second it didn't. In real time. Without getting defensive. That's —" she stopped, because the honest end of the sentence was *rare*, and she did not want to say *rare* out loud in front of Priya, who would build a wedding out of a single adjective.

"That sounds like you liked it."

"It sounds like I noticed it. They're different things. I notice everything, it's my entire personality, you've said so yourself."

"You're doing the face again."

She was. She turned it toward the window so the streetlight could have it instead of Priya, and out on Egerton Road the rain had started, fine and orange under the lamps, the specific Manchester rain that isn't weather so much as a permanent condition of the air, and she thought, with a kind of helpless tenderness she only ever felt at the edges of nights like this: *Manchester.*

She'd come here at eighteen because it was far enough from South London to be hers and near enough that her mother could still phone every evening at six. Three years she'd spent assembling a self out of this grey, glittering, rain-soaked, gloriously unbothered city — the Saturday markets and the warehouse nights and the studios in the Northern Quarter where people made things with their actual hands, the whole alternate universe to the one her family had mapped out for her in straight clean lines. *Law. Or finance, if you must be difficult. Something you can name at a dinner. Something with a building you can point to.* She had four months left of it. One final project. One shot at proving — to a course, to a panel of external assessors, to a future employer, to her mother at six o'clock — that the thing she was good at was a thing you could build a life on, and not a thing you did on the side of a real job until the babies came.

She did not have time for a rugby player who read rooms like he was solving them.

"I'm going home," she told Priya. "You stay. Be a person on my behalf. I love you."

"You're so *boring*," Priya said, with enormous and total affection, and hugged her hard enough to crack a rib, and let her go.

The night air outside was a relief, cold and wet and honest after the furnace of the house, and Ella stood on the step for a second letting it find the sweat at her hairline. Behind her the party throbbed on, muffled now by the closed door, ninety people compressed into a 1900s terrace continuing to have the night she had decided to leave. She pulled her coat tight and started down toward the curry mile, where the neon would still be on at this hour, bleeding into the puddles, the whole long bright strip of it doing its sleepless business — and she did not think about Callum Vance.

She thought about it so deliberately that it amounted to thinking about him constantly, which she noted, and filed, and refused.

*I came over anyway. You can decide what that tells you.*

"It tells me you're trouble," she said out loud, to the rain, to the empty wet street, to nobody.

The rain declined to argue. Behind her, three streets back, the loudest room in Fallowfield kept on roaring, and somewhere inside it a boy who never talked to anyone was standing very still, not drinking, watching the door she'd left through for considerably longer than he would ever admit to anyone, including himself, and trying to work out — with the part of his mind that calculated everything, that read the odds, that had never once in his life been wrong-footed at a party — why his hands wanted to make a beat about a girl whose name he hadn't even asked for, because he'd been too busy losing.

## ◆ ii. Six O'Clock

*Ella*

The flat she shared with Priya was two stops and a long walk away, above a launderette in Hulme, and the rent was due on the first, and the launderette's industrial dryers ran a low heat up through the floorboards that made the front room the warmest place Ella had ever lived and the loudest — a permanent tumbling hum, like the building had a heartbeat, or a stomach, a great slow digestion of other people's washing that went on beneath everything she did there. She had come, against every expectation, to love it. She had come to love a great many things in Manchester that she'd had to first learn weren't beneath her, which was a sentence she would never, ever say to her mother, because her mother had spent twenty-five years climbing specifically so that her children would never have to learn that anything was beneath them by living above it.

She got in at half one, peeled off the wet coat, and stood in the tumbling dark of the front room for a moment without turning the light on, the orange of the streetlamp coming through the thin curtain and laying a bar of it across the carcass of her final project, which covered most of the floor.

The project was — she was prepared to admit at this hour, to no one — not working.

The brief, set by her course, was open to the point of cruelty: *Develop and execute a complete visual identity for a real or fictional brand, artist, or campaign.* Most of her cohort were doing fictional. Safe. Controllable. No client to disappoint, no reality to crash against, a closed system in which the only judge was taste. Ella had decided in September, with the specific magnificent arrogance of someone who needs to prove something to people who aren't in the room, that fictional was cowardice. She was going to do *real*. She was going to find a real artist or a real label and art-direct a real thing into the real world, with real stakes and real failure available, and she was going to put it in front of the assessors and the recruiters at the degree show and make it impossible to file her under *lovely eye, shame about the practicality.*

She'd been pitching for two months. She had a folder of polite nos and a much larger folder of no replies at all, the silence that is its own kind of no. She had a moodboard practice that was, objectively, very good — she could take a sound and build it a world, a palette, a typeface, a set of references that made A&Rs go quiet on calls in the specific way that means *oh.* She'd done it for free for two local artists already, just to have the work to show, and the work was good, and good was not translating into the single thing she actually needed, which was a *yes* attached to a name big enough to matter to a stranger.

She crouched in the orange dark and looked at it all — the printouts, the fabric swatches, the colour studies, the test layouts pinned to a board she'd dragged back from a skip on Stretford Road — and felt the familiar two-part feeling that arrived every night at around this time: first the swell of how much she wanted it, the want so large and unhousebroken it frightened her, and then, right behind it, the cold administrative voice that asked whether wanting it this much was simply going to make the disappointment, when it came, proportionally enormous. The strong one's voice. The voice that had learned, at nine, that it was safer to manage a hope than to have one.

She made tea. She did not turn on the big light. She sat in the warm tumbling dark and let the day end.

Her phone, when she finally looked at it, had the text from her mother that she'd known would be there, sent at 6:02 because her mother considered 6:00 itself too aggressive and 6:05 a capitulation:

*Eleonora. The deadline for the Hargreave scheme is the 15th. I have sent you the link three times. Your cousin Tobi did it and he is at the firm now and his mother could not stop talking at church, it was honestly too much, but the point stands. Two years. You do the two years, you have a name behind you, THEN if you still want to do — and here, Ella could see it even in text, the word held at arm's length with tongs — your fashion, you do it from a position of something. Not from nothing. Your father and I did not cross water so that you could do nothing and call it freedom. Eat something. You sound thin even in your texts. Love, Mummy.*

Ella read it twice in the orange dark, and felt all of it at once — the irritation and the love and the guilt and the old, old exhaustion, the way they were not separate feelings but a single braided one she'd been carrying so long she no longer felt where it attached to her.

Because here was the thing, the thing that made it impossible, the thing that would have been so much easier if her mother were simply a villain: her mother was *right*, about everything except the conclusion. They *had* crossed water. Folake Savage-Oluwa had been a doctor in Lagos and a healthcare assistant in Lewisham for six years before the British system condescended to recognise a fraction of what she already was; Dele had driven minicabs and worked nights and built, brick by patient brick, the modest solid life that now hosted fifty people at a birthday and fed the whole road at Christmas. They had done it by being, as the saying in the house went, *twice as good and half as visible* — by pouring themselves into work that did not love them back and asking, in exchange, only that their children stand somewhere higher and safer than the place they'd started. And Ella, the eldest, the proof, the first British-born, the one the whole project had been *for* — Ella was proposing to take the high safe ground they'd bought with their bodies and use it to do something that, to her mother, looked indistinguishable from the precarious creative striving that had ruined half the men in the family. Uncle Femi and the restaurant. Cousin Side and the music that never came to anything. *Prettiness.* A hobby with delusions.

She understood it. That was the trap. She understood it so completely that she could have written her mother's side of the argument better than her mother could, and the understanding did not change the fact that the only place in her entire life where she was not performing being fine — where she was not the eldest, the strong one, the one who handled it, the project manager of everyone else's feelings — was the place where she sat on a skip-rescued board in a launderette-warm room and built a world out of a sound.

She typed: *I'll look at the link, Mum. I'm eating. Love you.*

She did not say the true thing, which was: *I didn't apply and I'm not going to.* She'd say it eventually. She'd been rehearsing it for weeks. But not at half one in the morning by text, not as an afterthought; the true thing deserved a table and her mother's face and her own steady voice, and she did not have any of those things tonight, so she sent the small lie that kept the peace and hated herself for it the precise, manageable amount.

Then she sat in the dark a while longer, and — because the human mind is a traitor and a comedian — found that the cold administrative voice and the warm wanting voice and the braided exhausted family voice had all, briefly, been crowded out by a fourth thing, an irritating, unwelcome, low-frequency hum underneath all of them, the same frequency as the dryers, the same frequency as the bass through the floor of a house on Egerton Road.

*I came over anyway.*

"No," she told the room.

The room, like the rain, declined to argue. The dryers tumbled. Out on the street the orange light did its work on the puddles, beautiful and slightly ruined, and Ella Savage-Oluwa, who saw everything, did not yet see the shape of the four months coming toward her, or the boy at the centre of them, or the version of herself she was going to have to become to survive both. She finished her tea. She went to bed. And she dreamed, irritatingly, in four notes — three that climbed and one that fell — though she would not remember it in the morning, and could not have known, yet, whose melody it was, or how far it had already travelled to find her.

## ◆ iii. Set Piece

*Cal*

Six a.m. and the pitch was the colour of a bruise, frost burning off it in the floodlights, the breath of fifteen young men hanging in the lit cold like the souls were trying to leave the bodies early, and Cal Vance was running the same line for the fourth time while his father stood at the touchline with his arms folded and said nothing — which was worse than anything he could have said, and his father knew it was worse, and that was the point.

"Again," called Marsh, the academy backs coach, from somewhere off to the left, and Cal was already turning before the word finished, already resetting, because the thing about being the one they came to watch was that the watching never stopped, not at six in the morning, not in the rain, not ever. He took the ball flat off the imaginary scrum-half, drifted two steps across the imaginary line, fixed the imaginary defender with his eyes — *look at the man you're not passing to, that's the whole trick, sell him the lie with your face* — and put the pass into the space where the winger would be. Soft. Weighted. The ball hung in the cold floodlit air for exactly as long as it needed to hang and not a fraction longer, a spiral so clean it looked slow, and dropped into the hands of nobody, because there was no winger, because it was a drill, because at six in the morning the space he was passing into was purely theoretical and he filled it perfectly anyway.

He had thrown ten thousand of those. He could throw it asleep. He sometimes did, in the grey country between waking and dreaming, the ball leaving his hands and never quite landing, hanging forever in a floodlit dark, and he would surface from it with his fingers twitching the shape of the release.

"Better," Marsh said. "That's the one. Why's that one different from the other three?"

"Didn't think about it," Cal said.

"Then stop thinking about the other ones."

Easy to say. Cal jogged back to the mark, breath smoking, and let his eyes go to the touchline for half a second — just half, no more, you never gave it more than half — and his father gave him the nod. The small one. The minimal downward tilt of the chin that had run Cal's entire life since he was six years old and first understood, with a child's terrible clarity, that the nod was a thing you earned and the absence of it was a thing you survived. Gareth Vance had played eleven seasons in the second row for clubs that didn't pay enough and broke him in places that still woke him when it rained, and he had decided, somewhere down in the wreckage of his own finished career, that his son was going to have the one he hadn't. He had decided it the way other men decide to build a house — as a project, with a timeline, brick by brick, no days off, no contingency, no acknowledgement that the materials were a living boy who might have wanted to build something else.

*His son.* Singular. Cal shut that down before it could open into the plural it used to be. Not at six a.m. Not on the pitch. There were rooms inside him that he kept locked through the daylight hours, locked and dark and load-bearing, and one of them had a name on the door, and he had become, over five years, extremely good at the locking — so good that he could run a perfect drill on top of the locked room and never once feel the floor flex.

They ran for another hour. By the end of it the sun was a grey rumour behind the main stand and the frost had gone to mud and three of the younger lads were watching Cal the way he used to watch the seniors when he was their age — with that specific hunger, that *how do I get to be that, what is the secret, is it a thing I can be told* — and Cal gave them the version of himself they needed to see. Easy. Unbothered. A word for each of them on the way off, a hand on a shoulder, a small correction offered as encouragement. *You're good. You're getting there. Watch your support line in the second phase, you're a yard slow folding round.* It cost him nothing and it bought him everything, because that was the entire economy of being Callum Vance in the daylight: you gave them the swagger, you gave them the ease, you let them believe it was free, and in exchange they never once thought to wonder what it was costing you to produce, or whether the still calm centre of you was calm at all, or whether — and this was the unaskable question, the one no teammate had ever come close to — the boy they'd elected the freest among them might in fact be the one holding his breath.

"Pub Thursday," Tom said, falling into step beside him as they came off, steam rising off both of them like a pair of just-finished horses. Tom Bryce was the only one of them Cal would have called a friend without a silent asterisk after the word — too big to be threatened by anyone, too fundamentally kind to bother with the locker-room politics, the rare lad who had worked out somewhere around eighteen that you could simply *like* people, that it was allowed, that it didn't cost you status. "Priya's lot are coming again. The art crowd. You should actually come this time."

"I'm working Thursday."

"You're always working Thursday. You don't even *have* a job, that's the thing that does my head in." Tom said it without heat, just baffled, the bafflement of a man who had spent two years watching his friend vanish after dark and return with shadows under his eyes and no story to account for them. "You disappear, like, four nights a week. You come back looking like you've been up till four. You've got money you don't explain. If you weren't the most boring man alive I'd think you were dealing. Where do you *go*, Cal? Genuinely. I've never once known where you go."

"To bed early," Cal said. "Like a professional. Some of us are trying to make it."

"Aye, and some of us are trying to have a *life*." Tom grinned, unbothered, used to the wall, having long ago decided that whatever was behind it was Cal's business and that loyalty meant not trying the handle. He bumped Cal with a shoulder that could have moved a car. "Anyway. The art crowd. One of Priya's mates was at the thing at Egerton Road. The stylist. Did Priya's whole look for the formal, apparently, you'd have seen it, the one with the —" Tom gestured vaguely at his own ear. "Tall. Sharp. Tom said —" he caught himself, because Tom referring to himself in the third person was a tell that he was about to enjoy something. "*I* said your name to her and she said it back with a *tone.*"

Cal kept his face exactly where it was. This was a skill, and like the flat pass he had built it with ten thousand repetitions until it looked like nothing, until it looked like the absence of effort rather than its mastery. "Which one."

"Don't do that. Don't do the 'which one.' You talked to her. At the party. *Voluntarily.* You stood in a corner with one girl for five minutes and then walked off and stood on your own being weird for the rest of the night, which, for you, is basically a marriage proposal. Her name's Ella." Tom watched him sidelong with the dim, delighted cunning of a very large man who has spotted something small and is pleased with himself for it. "You like her."

"I read the room wrong," Cal said, which was true, and which was very possibly the single most honest sentence he would speak all day.

Because he had. He'd gone over to Ella Savage-Oluwa for exactly the reasons she'd laid out to his face on the table between them — because she was the one not performing, in a room where everyone, himself most of all, was performing, and he was tired, a bone-deep five-year tiredness, of performing. He'd expected to run the play. The read, the flattery, the engineered closeness, the soft landing. He was good at the play; the play worked; the play had never once not worked. And she had taken it apart on the table in front of him, calm as a surgeon, naming each move a half-second before he made it, dismantling not just the approach but the *architecture* of it, the part he'd thought was invisible, the part he'd thought was *him* — and instead of the irritation he should have felt, instead of the small managed embarrassment, he had felt, of all the unwelcome things, *relief.* Like setting something heavy down. Like being caught at something he'd half-wanted to be caught at for years. For one second in that disgusting corner he had wanted to say something true to a stranger, and the wanting had frightened him so badly that he had done the only safe thing available, which was to lose on purpose, admit it, and leave.

He hadn't stopped thinking about it since. He was thinking about it now, in the showers, with the water going from scalding to lukewarm the way it always did when fifteen of them showered at once, and that was a problem — a budget problem — because Cal Vance did not have spare attention to spend. His attention was a fixed quantity and it was fully allocated and had been for years: so much to the rugby, the visible life, the project his father was building; so much to the other thing, the night thing, the locked-room thing; and a thin protective margin held in reserve against the daily labour of keeping those two from ever touching. There was no line item for a girl. There had never needed to be. And now there was a small persistent overdraft in a part of the ledger he hadn't known was open, and it was drawing down the reserve, the protective margin, the very fund that kept the worlds apart — and some animal part of him understood that this was dangerous in a way he couldn't yet name, and the rest of him, the calculating part, the part that read the odds, looked at the danger and did the thing it had never done before with a clear warning in front of it.

It decided it didn't care.

## ◆ iv. The Room With No Sign

*Cal*

The studio was above a vintage shop on the edge of the Northern Quarter, up a staircase that smelled of damp and old paper and the particular cold of a building that has been many things and is currently between purposes. There was no sign on the door, because the whole point was that there was no sign; the absence was the architecture. Cal had taken the lease under a name that wasn't his — well, that was a lie he told even to himself, a careful one. The name *was* his. It was the only name that had ever felt entirely his own, unbuilt by anyone, un-nodded-at, un-coached, the single syllable he'd chosen rather than inherited: **V.** He paid for the room in cash he made from the work he did in it, which made it the one clean closed loop in his entire life — the music paid for the room where he made the music, and no one stood at the edge of it with their arms folded, and there was no project, and there was no nod to earn or survive, and the only judge of whether a thing was good was whether it made the hair stand up on his own forearms, which was a tyrant of a standard but at least it was *his* tyrant.

He let himself in a little after nine, when the city below had already got going — delivery vans double-parked on the cobbles, the metal shutter of the vintage shop rattling up, the first of the coffee places putting their boards out — and he climbed the cold stairs and let himself into the room and did not turn the big lights on. He never turned the big lights on first. He turned on the lamp, and the glow of the screen, and he sat down at the desk in the brown half-dark, and he exhaled for what felt like the first time since the alarm had gone at half five.

This was the part nobody knew. This was the vault.

Cal Vance made beats. Not as a hobby, not as a laugh, not as the thing the lads would have called "messing about on a laptop." He made the records that other people's faces went on. Three tracks on a mixtape that had done a quarter of a million streams and counting. A whole six-track EP for an artist out of Moss Side that the blogs had genuinely lost their minds over — *who produced this, who is this, where did this sound come from* — and the answer to every one of those questions was a single credit that read **V.** and nothing else: no face, no socials, no interviews, no government name. Because Cal Vance the fly-half, the academy prospect, the boy scouts came to watch, could not also be the boy who made the kind of woozy, late, broken, three-in-the-morning sound that the rugby world would have read, at absolute best, as a distraction — and at worst, in the specific cosmology of his father, as the thing. The wasteful thing. The thing with no living in it. The thing that had taken Jordan.

He didn't let the name into the room. Not yet. He let the work do what the name-keeping couldn't, which was hold the exact shape of a feeling without ever letting it out into the air where it could be seen and therefore lost.

He pulled up the session he'd been building for three weeks — a thing for Knox, the only artist he worked with face to face, the only living person who knew that V was Cal. It was nearly there, the track, and nearly-there was the most dangerous place a thing could be, because nearly-there asked you to decide what *there* was. A drum pattern that hung back half a beat, reluctant, like it didn't want to arrive at its own party. A vocal he'd chopped and pitched down until it sounded like a memory of itself, a happiness recalled rather than felt. A low synth that didn't play notes so much as move across them, like weather coming over the rooftops he could see from the high window. He'd been trying, for three weeks, to make it sound like the inside of his own head, which was an insane brief to give yourself, and he had very nearly done it, which was the trouble — because the inside of his own head was not a place he'd ever wanted anyone to hear, and here it was, two minutes and forty seconds of it, with no words at all, and it sounded like six a.m. on a frozen pitch and a nod earned and a room locked and, now, newly, intrusively, in the top of it, in the bright part he hadn't authorised — a girl taking his best move apart on a table and laughing at him and being right.

He took the chopped vocal out. The track got colder, truer, lonelier. He put it back. It got warmer, and the warmth was the lie, and the lie was nicer. He took it out again. He sat with the cold true version for a while, and then — and this was the part he would never have explained to Tom, to anyone — he opened a different file. A clean one. And without quite deciding to, his hands found a slow kick, a heartbeat at rest, and a hi-hat that came in late and reluctant the way he liked them, the way he'd learned them off someone, and over the top of it he laid a sample he'd had sitting on a drive for months, a woman's voice off an old crackling soul record going *oh, oh*, pitched down until it sounded like someone remembering being happy a long time ago — and underneath all of it, four feet down, buried so deep that you would feel it in your chest before you ever heard it in your ears, where no one would ever find it and no one was ever meant to, he laid four notes.

Three that climbed. One that fell.

He listened back to the clean file once, in the lamp-lit dark, with the city going about its indifferent morning business below the high window, and it sounded like the precise sensation of being seen through and crossing the room anyway. He had not set out to make that. He had set out to make nothing; he had opened a clean file the way another man might have gone for a walk. But there it was, made, a thing in the world that hadn't existed an hour ago, and at the centre of it, doing what it always did, holding the structure up from underneath, was his brother — and laid over the top of his brother, in the bright unfamiliar part, was a stranger he'd met for five minutes and lost to on purpose.

He saved it under a name that was just the date. He did not name it her name. He didn't have her name; he'd been too busy losing to ask for it. He would have it by Thursday, as it turned out, though he didn't know that yet, and once he had it he would never be able to give it back, but for now the file sat on the drive called only by the day, and Cal Vance sat in front of it in the dark and understood, with the cold clear part of him that had never once been wrong about a defensive line, that he had just done something he could not undo: he had let a living person into the room where he kept his dead one, and the room had only ever had space for the locking, and now there was something in it that wanted, against every law he had ever made for himself, to be *un*locked.

His phone lit on the desk, face down; he turned it over. Knox. **studio thurs? bringing the team. label want a moodboard person, some art direction girl apparently sick with it, real vision, you'll like the sound of her stuff. trust.**

Cal looked at the message for a long time.

Then he turned the phone back over, face down, the way he always did, the way that meant *not now, the world can wait, this room is for the one true thing* — and he went back to the cold true version of the Knox track, the one with no living person in it, and he worked on it until the light through the high window went from grey to gold to grey again, until his stomach reminded him with a lurch that he had not eaten since the protein thing in the car at half five, until the budget of his attention was finally, blessedly, completely spent and there was nothing left over with which to remember anything at all.

He saved the session under its non-name. He turned off the lamp. He stood at the high window for one minute looking down at the wet cobbled street and the people on it, each of them, he assumed, getting to be only one person, a single self walking around in the daylight with nothing locked — and then he went back down the cold stairs that smelled of old paper, and out into the rain that a South London girl had walked off into the night before, and he put the swagger back on the way you put on a coat at a door, and he went to be Callum Vance, the one they came to watch, and he was so good at it that not one person he passed all day thought to ask who was watching *him.*

Not until Thursday. But he didn't know that yet, and so for one more day the worlds stayed in their boxes, and the locked room stayed locked, and the file with the date for a name sat in the dark holding a dead brother and a living stranger four feet apart, waiting — the way the whole city seemed to be waiting, under its permanent fine orange rain — for the thing that was already coming up the stairs.

## ◆ v. The Architecture of a Locked Room

*Cal*

People thought a double life was a dramatic thing. Cal had learned that it wasn't. A double life was not a thing of disguises and near-misses and pounding hearts; it was a thing of *logistics*, of a thousand small unglamorous decisions made so consistently that they became a kind of weather. You did not get caught living two lives because of one operatic error. You got caught because you got tired, and tiredness made you careless about the small things, and it was always the small things — a tagged location, a session file with the wrong name on it, a producer credit somebody decided to dig into on a slow afternoon, a teammate who looked over your shoulder at the wrong second. So Cal did not allow himself to be careless, ever, which meant he did not allow himself to be tired, which was of course impossible, because he was always tired, and so the whole structure ran on a reserve of vigilance that he topped up by sleeping less, which made him more tired, which required more vigilance. It was a machine that consumed its own fuel. He'd been running it since he was seventeen and he had stopped, somewhere along the way, being able to imagine what it would feel like to switch it off.

The week between the party and the Thursday ran the way every week ran, which was on rails he'd laid himself and now could not get off.

Monday: gym at six, video analysis at eight — an hour of watching himself on a screen while Marsh paused and rewound and said *there, see, you've told the fullback where you're going with your hips, you've got to learn to lie with your hips* — then a seminar he was technically enrolled in, Sport and Exercise Science, where he sat at the back and did not speak and got firsts anyway because the work was easy and he had nothing to spend his attention on but easy work. Monday night: the studio, until two, mixing the Knox track, taking the vocal out and putting it back.

Tuesday: contact session, which meant he came home with a thumb-print bruise blooming on his hip and a graze down one forearm that he'd have to keep covered, because grazes asked questions and he didn't do questions. Tuesday night: the studio, a session with Knox himself, the two of them and a bag of cans and the rare specific joy of making something with the one person alive who knew his real name — Knox draped over the battered sofa freestyling nonsense until the nonsense suddenly wasn't, until a line landed and they both went still, *that, do that again, keep that* — and for a few hours the machine switched off, because Knox required no performance, Knox had met the real one years ago in a youth-club studio before either of them was anyone, and you cannot perform for a person who knew you before the performance existed.

Wednesday: recovery, ice baths, a nutritionist's spreadsheet, a sponsor thing he had to smile through where a man from a protein company called him "the future" four times and Cal said the easy gracious things and watched the man's eyes do the thing eyes did around him now, the *I knew him before* calculation, the small investment of attention people made in the not-yet-famous on the off chance. Wednesday night: not the studio. Wednesday night he made himself sleep, eight hours, professionally, because Saturday was a fixture and the body was the asset and you serviced the asset whether or not you wanted to, especially when you didn't want to.

And under all of it, every day, the locked room. The one with the name on the door.

He let himself near it exactly once that week, and only because the new file made him.

It was late Tuesday, after Knox had gone, the studio dark except for the lamp, and Cal had meant to keep working on the Knox track and had instead opened the clean file — the one with the date for a name, the one with the woman's voice going *oh, oh* and the bright unauthorised part and the four buried notes — and he'd listened to it the whole way through with his eyes shut, and at the end of it he'd done the thing he never did. He'd got out his phone, and he'd opened the message thread he could not delete and could not open, the one that said **Jords** at the top with a photo of his brother pulling a stupid face in a car, and he'd looked at the last message in it without playing it.

A voice note. 0:47. Sent at 23:14 on a Thursday — and wasn't that a thing, that it had been a Thursday, that the worst day of his life had been a Thursday and here he was dreading and craving another one — five years and three months ago. He knew the waveform of it by heart, the little visual mountain range of his brother's last forty-seven seconds, even though he hadn't let it make a sound in over a year. *Listen to this, Cal, listen to the bit at the end.* He knew that was what it said because he'd heard it, once, the day after, when sixteen-year-old Cal had finally looked at his phone and found two-days-old proof that his brother had been alive and excited and wanting to share something at 23:14 on the Thursday, had been a person with a future and a bit at the end he was proud of, right up until a wet road outside Leeds had decided otherwise somewhere around midnight, while Cal was asleep, while the voice note sat unopened because Cal had been at training and then tired and then it could wait, because everything could always wait, because he was sixteen and his brother was permanent.

He didn't play it. He looked at the waveform in the lamp-light, the little mountains of his brother's voice, and then he locked the phone and put it face down and went back to the clean file and listened, instead, to the four notes he'd buried in it — the same four notes, transposed, hidden, encrypted into a place his father would never accidentally find them, the only grave he'd been able to dig that no one could desecrate by visiting. *Three that climb. One that falls.* He'd put them in everything for five years. He'd put his brother in a quarter of a million strangers' headphones without one of them knowing they were carrying a dead boy around in their pocket, playing him on buses, falling asleep to him. It was insane. He knew it was insane. It was also the single most sane thing he did, the one act in his whole logistical machine of a life that was not in service of a project or a performance but was simply, stubbornly, love — secret love, ghost love, the love of a brother who hadn't replied for two days and would spend the rest of his life replying in the only language they'd ever shared, in the one place his father couldn't follow, four feet down, under the drums, where it was safe because it was already buried, because you cannot lose a thing twice.

His mother would have understood the new file. She was the only one who would have. But his mother was in a small flat in Catford with another of Jordan's graves — the literal room, kept exactly as it had been, the decks and the milk crate of records and the spider plant somehow still alive — and Cal did not call her enough, because calling her meant standing in the one relationship that had never required him to perform and discovering, every time, how exhausted the performing had left him, and the exhaustion was easier to manage if he didn't keep measuring it against the one place it lifted. She'd left when he was fourteen. Not because she'd stopped loving them — he understood that now, mostly — but because she couldn't live inside the way Gareth turned grief into a project, couldn't watch the machine being built around the surviving son, and so she had done the only thing left to a person who can't bear to watch a thing happen, which was to leave the room. The family's original ghost. Cal had inherited the gene. He just hadn't admitted it yet, hadn't yet had it named for him by a girl on a chip-shop step, hadn't yet understood that he'd made himself into a ghost on purpose, that the detachment everyone read as cool was a held breath, that *if you're already gone, you can't be left.*

That naming was still weeks away. For now he just worked, and locked the room, and ran the machine, and got tired, and topped up the vigilance, and somewhere underneath the whole structure the new file sat humming with a danger he could feel and couldn't source — the danger of having made, for the first time in five years, a piece of music that wanted to be heard by one specific living person rather than felt, anonymously, by a quarter of a million strangers who'd never know his name.

## ◆ vi. The Grind

*Ella*

Priya came home on the Tuesday evening dropping bags and talking before the door was shut, which was how Priya entered every room, as a weather system with opinions. "Okay so don't be weird, but I have a thing, and you have to not be weird about it, because you do a thing where I tell you something good and you immediately find the catch, and just for once —"

"I don't do that."

"You do the *exact* thing. You're doing the face that's getting ready to do it." Priya dropped onto the floor opposite her in the launderette-warm dark, which she only did for major announcements; minor ones she delivered standing up, walking around, half in the kitchen. The fact that she was sitting made Ella put her laptop down. "But this one's actually big, so. Tom knows everyone, right, because rugby boys somehow know the entire city, it's like a secret civic infrastructure of large men. And one of Tom's mates does sound for people, and he's mates with Knox's lot. *Knox*, Ella. Marcus. The 'Lowlight' one."

Ella's whole body went still. "*That* Knox."

"That Knox. He's making a proper project — a real album, not a mixtape, a real thing, and the label's behind it now, and they want —" Priya did jazz hands, the universal Priya signal for *brace yourself* "— creative direction. A visual world. A whole identity. Somebody with an actual eye, who isn't some forty-five-year-old man who thinks Black British music is a Halloween costume he can put on for a campaign. And Tom mentioned you. And Knox's people went and looked at the stuff you put up — the 'Lowlight' reimagining you did for *free*, for fun, the one with the orange and the wrong film stock — and apparently Knox himself saw it and said the word *yes* out loud, which according to the sound guy basically never happens, and they want to meet you. As a real candidate. For the real job. At their studio. This Thursday."

The dryers tumbled beneath them. Ella did not say anything for so long that Priya's whole face began the slow collapse of someone whose surprise party has gone wrong.

"Say something. Is it not — I thought it was — it's good, isn't it? It's hard to tell with you, you've got the same face for 'I've won an award' and 'someone has died.'"

"Priya." Ella's voice came out strange, too careful, the voice she used to hold something heavy without letting it show in her arms. "Do you actually understand what this is."

"It's... a job? A good job?"

"It's *the* project." Ella heard her own voice climb and made it stay level, the discipline of not wanting a thing too loudly in case the wanting jinxed it. "It's a real artist — a real one, the kids at Tio's school have his lyrics in their bios — with a real label, a real release, a real budget. If I art-direct Knox's album, then I don't have to *find* a brand for my final project. The project *becomes* the final project. The thing I'd be making for a living becomes the thing I put in the degree show, in front of the external assessors, in front of the recruiters who come round on the last day with their lanyards deciding whose number to take. It's not a fictional brand in a folder. It's a thing on the actual radio with my actual name in the actual credits." She pressed both hands flat over her face. "Priya. It's everything I've been pitching for, for two months, two months of polite nos and silence, and it just — landed. In our front room. Above a launderette. Because your boyfriend is enormous and knows everyone."

"So that's the good face?" Priya ventured. "I can never tell with you, you're the most opaque person I've ever —"

"It's the good face." Ella lowered her hands, and she was grinning now, properly, the unmanaged one, the one that got loose maybe twice a year. "I could kiss Tom. I could kiss his enormous stupid head."

"Please don't, he's mine, but I'll pass it on."

And then the second feeling came, right behind the joy, the way it always did, the cold administrative tenant who lived under all of Ella's hopes and charged rent on them. *Don't want it this much. If you want it this much and it goes wrong, the size of the wreckage is the size of the want, and you have a degree to finish and rent due on the first and a mother at six o'clock, and you cannot afford a want this size, you have never been able to afford a want any size, that's the whole point of you, you're the one who manages the wants so the others can have theirs.* She felt it move through her, familiar, almost comforting in its familiarity, and for once — maybe because Priya was sitting on the floor, which she only did for the big ones; maybe because the orange light was doing the thing it did; maybe because some animal part of her had already started dreaming in four notes — Ella looked the cold tenant in the eye and said, internally, *not this one. This one I'm allowed to want.*

"I need to build a deck," she said, already on her feet, already moving, the tiredness burned clean off her by the only fuel that had ever fully worked on her, which was a real chance. "A proper one. Not a moodboard, not free-work — a pitch. I need to listen to everything he's ever made between now and Thursday and find the world that's already inside it, and walk into that room with the world *already built*, so completely built that they can't imagine the project without me in it. So that saying no to me feels like demolishing something instead of just declining something."

"It's eight o'clock on a Tuesday."

"Then I've got till Thursday minus eight o'clock on a Tuesday." She looked at Priya, and Priya saw the face — not the opaque one, the other one, the one Ella only ever showed the work, the one that was naked in a way Ella never let herself be naked in front of people. "I'm not going to be safe, Pri. I've been safe my whole life. I've been the safe one *for everyone else.* I'm going to be *good*. And I'm going to be so good in that room on Thursday that they cannot say no to me."

She did not sleep much that week either. She built a world. She listened to every track Knox had ever put out — the early stuff that was all hunger and rough edges, the middle stuff where he'd found the voice, the recent things produced by the mysterious *V* that had a different quality entirely, a depth, a late-night ache, a sound that wasn't slick but *intimate*, like overhearing someone's actual interior — and she built around it the thing she would later describe to a label as *a secret that got out.* She did not sleep, and she did not look at the Hargreave link, and she texted her mother *eating well, working hard, love you* at increasingly creative hours, and exactly once, on the Wednesday, at three in the morning, she found herself thinking — entirely unbidden, with real annoyance — about a boy who'd said *I came over anyway* and then walked off and won by losing, and she shut the thought in the same drawer as the cold tenant and went back to the work, because the work was real and the boy was a complication and she had no room in the budget of her life for a complication, and the studio she was going to on Thursday was just a room, and the ghost producer behind the boards was just a man with a mystique, and she would probably never even meet him.

She was wrong about exactly one of those things. But on the Wednesday night, with the deck nearly built and the world nearly real and her whole future apparently fitting through a door in the Northern Quarter on a Thursday afternoon, she didn't know that yet, and she let herself, for once, sleep four whole hours, and she dreamed again in four notes she didn't recognise — three that climbed and one that fell — and woke not remembering them, only with the strange sensation, as she made her coffee in the grey Hulme morning, of being homesick for somewhere she had not yet been.

## ◆ vii. The People You Can't See

*Cal*

On the Wednesday — the night Cal made himself sleep, the professional eight hours, the servicing of the asset — he did not, in fact, sleep, because his agent called at half nine, and Marcus Webb did not call to chat.

"You've got eyes on you," Webb said, with the particular relish of a man whose entire value proposition was knowing things first. Webb was forty, slick, dressed like money that wanted you to know it was money but had read an article about restraint; he'd signed Cal at nineteen on the strength of a single school-of-rugby fixture and had spent three years since being right about him, which made him insufferable in a way Cal had decided to tolerate because being right was the whole job. "Saturday. The fixture. I'm not going to tell you who because I don't want you tightening up, you play your best loose, but there'll be men in that stand whose phone calls change lives. So. Two things. One: you do the basics perfectly and you let the special stuff happen, you don't *chase* the special stuff. Two —" and here Webb's voice changed, dropped into the register he used for the things he actually cared about, which were never the things you'd expect "— is there anything I don't know."

"Like what."

"Like anything, Cal. Like a girlfriend who's going to put a story on her account that ends up screenshotted. Like a mate who sells a quote. Like a — I don't know what I'm asking about because if I knew I wouldn't have to ask, that's the point of the question. At the level you're about to be at, son, there are no small secrets. There are only secrets that haven't been monetised yet. Everybody's got something. The ones who make it are the ones who either have nothing or have already told me everything so I can manage it. So." A pause, down the line, expensive and patient. "Which are you. Nothing, or everything-already-told?"

And Cal stood in his small bare professional bedroom — bare because he spent no time in it, because the studio was where he lived and this was where he slept the body — and looked at the question, the way he looked at a defensive line, and saw exactly how it was structured. Webb wasn't fishing idly. Webb had felt something. Agents were like Sasha, like Ella, like Cal himself: they read rooms for a living, and the room they read most carefully was their client, and Webb had picked up the static of a secret the way a man picks up the smell of smoke before he sees the fire.

"Nothing," Cal said. Even. Easy. The flat pass of a lie, weighted perfectly, hung in the air exactly as long as it needed to. "I sleep, I train, I do my degree, I'm boring. Ask Tom, he complains about it weekly."

"Mm," said Webb, and the *mm* contained an entire essay about how little he believed it and how he'd decided, for now, not to push. "Boring's good. Boring's bankable. Stay boring through Saturday and we'll talk about your whole life on Sunday, because your whole life might be different on Sunday." He paused. "One more thing, and then I'll let you sleep. The special stuff. The thing that makes you different — and you are different, I've watched four hundred fly-halves and you've got a thing maybe three of them had — I don't know what it is and I don't need to. Some lads it's anger. Some it's fear of their dad. Some it's God. Whatever yours is, the thing that gives you *time* on the ball when everyone else is rushed — protect it. Don't let Marsh or me or anyone coach it flat in the name of consistency, because consistency's worth money but the *thing* is worth everything, and the thing is always the part nobody can see. All right. Sleep. Be boring. Change your life Saturday."

He rang off. Cal lay in the dark and did not sleep, because Webb had, without knowing it, named the danger from a second angle. *There are no small secrets at this level. Protect the thing nobody can see.* And the thing nobody could see and the secret that hadn't been monetised yet were the same thing — were both, when you traced them down to the root, four notes that climbed and fell, a brother in a car on a wet road, a locked room with a name on the door. The thing that gave him time on the ball was the same thing that made the sound. Webb thought they were two topics. Ella, in three weeks, would tell him to his face that they were one. But that was three weeks away, and tonight Cal lay in the bare room servicing a body that wouldn't sleep, doing the arithmetic he could never make come out, which was: the bigger I get, the more eyes there are; the more eyes there are, the harder the locked room is to keep locked; and the harder it is to keep locked, the more it costs to keep it, and I am already paying everything I have. He was a man holding his breath who had just been told he was about to be asked to swim further.

He got up at four and went to the studio and worked until the alarm for the Thursday told him to stop, and he did not open the clean file with the date for a name, because he didn't trust himself with it on no sleep, and he did not play the voice note, and he locked everything, the files and the room and the breath, and went to be the future a man from a protein company had called him four times, and he was, as instructed, boring, and bankable, and nobody could see the thing, and that was, he told himself, exactly how he wanted it.

He was lying to himself. He was extremely good at it. It was, after all, just another flat pass, weighted perfectly, sold with the face — and the only person who had ever read his hips instead of his eyes was, at that exact hour, two miles south in a launderette-warm flat, building a world out of his music and not knowing it was his, getting ready to walk up a staircase that smelled of old paper and call his bluff with her whole life.

## ◆ viii. The Eve

*Ella*

Tioluwa called on the Wednesday night, which she was not supposed to do on a school night, which was exactly why she did it.

"Don't tell Mum I'm up," Tio said, by way of hello, her voice low, the duvet-fort acoustics of a seventeen-year-old breaking the rules in a quiet house. "I can't sleep. I've got mocks and I hate all of it and I needed to talk to someone who isn't going to turn it into a lecture about how lucky I am to even *have* the mocks."

"I would never," Ella said, settling back against the skip-rescued board, the deck for Knox glowing on the laptop beside her, the dryers tumbling beneath. "I am a lecture-free zone. What's wrong?"

"Everything's fine, that's what's wrong. Everything's fine and I'm supposed to be grateful and I am grateful and I'm also losing my mind, and I can't say that to Mum because she'll do the —" Tio dropped into a startlingly accurate impression of their mother "— *do you know what I was doing at your age, Tioluwa* — and I can't say it to my friends because their version of stress is whether a boy texted back, and I just —" Ella heard her sister exhale, seventeen and overloaded. "How did you do it. Sixth form. All of it. You never seemed stressed. You were just — sorted. You were always sorted. Mum still holds you up like a — you're like a saint they pray to. Saint Eleonora of the Good Grades and the Sensible Choices."

Ella laughed, but it caught on something on the way out. "Tio. I wasn't sorted. I was *performing* sorted. There's a difference and I'm only now learning how big it is."

"What does that mean."

Ella looked at the orange bar of streetlight across the floor and decided, because it was Wednesday night and her sister was crying quietly in a duvet fort and the truest things get said in exactly those conditions, to tell her the real thing. "It means I learned really young that if I looked like I had it handled, then nobody had to worry about me, and if nobody had to worry about me, then Mum and Dad had a bit more room to worry about the things they actually couldn't fix. The money, mostly. When we were small. There was a year, you were tiny, you won't remember — Dad lost the first proper job, the one with the security, and it was bad, Tio, it was midnight-at-the-kitchen-table bad, and I was nine and I decided, I literally remember deciding it, like flicking a switch: *I will never be a thing they have to worry about. I'll be the easy one. I'll handle myself so completely that I'm a relief instead of a weight.*" She swallowed. "And it worked. That's the thing. It worked so well that everyone — including me — forgot it was a performance. I became the strong one so convincingly that I stopped being able to find the seam between the strong one and me. So when you ask how I did sixth form looking sorted — I did it by deciding, at nine, that being a person with needs was a luxury for other people. That's not a recipe, Tio. Don't copy it. It's not strength. It's just fear wearing a really good outfit."

There was a long silence down the line. The dryers tumbled. Somewhere in Lewisham, a seventeen-year-old sat very still in a fort made of duvet.

"That's the most you've ever said to me," Tio said finally. "Like — ever. You don't *do* this. You do the lecture-free zone and the 'you've got this' and then you change the subject. You've never once told me you were scared of anything."

"I know."

"Why now?"

Ella considered it. "Because I think I'm about to stop performing," she said slowly, surprising herself with the truth of it as it arrived. "I've got a — there's a thing tomorrow. A real chance. The kind I've been pretending I don't want as much as I want, because wanting it out loud is dangerous. And I think if I get it, I'm going to have to finally tell Mum and Dad the truth about what I'm actually doing with my life, and stop managing them, and stop being the easy one — and that's so terrifying that I think it's, like, leaking. The honesty. It's leaking out onto you because you called at the exact moment I couldn't hold it in anymore." She laughed, wetly. "Sorry. You called for comfort and got my entire breakdown."

"No," Tio said fiercely, instantly, the way only a little sister can. "No, this is *better* than comfort. Comfort is when you tell me it's fine. This is when you tell me it's *not* fine and you're scared too and we're both just — doing it anyway. That's so much better. Comfort is lonely. This isn't." A pause. "What's the thing tomorrow?"

"I can't say yet. I'll jinx it."

"Saint Eleonora is *superstitious*, this is incredible, I'm telling everyone."

"Go to *sleep*, Tio. You've got mocks."

"You go to sleep, you've got your secret thing."

"I will."

"You won't. You're going to work on it all night, I can hear the laptop fan." Tio's voice softened, went younger. "Ella. Whatever the thing is tomorrow. Get it. Not for Mum, not so you can prove anything to anyone at church. Get it for *you.* Because I've spent my whole life watching you be the easy one and I always thought you were just — like that, naturally, born sorted — and now you're telling me you decided it at nine to take weight off everyone else, and I just — somebody should get to take some weight off *you*, for once. So get the thing. And then let it be yours. Don't even tell Mum if you don't want to. Have one thing that's just yours and not a performance for anyone."

Ella sat in the orange dark with her phone hot against her ear and her sister's voice in it and the deck glowing beside her and the dryers tumbling beneath, and felt something she did not have a clean name for — the specific, dangerous warmth of being seen by someone she'd spent her whole life making sure didn't have to see her. The flinch came, right on schedule, the burn-near-a-hand reflex: *you can't accept this, accepting it means you're a person with needs, and you're not, you're the one who carries.* And for the second time that week, with the second person, she made herself not flinch. She let her little sister hand her something with no invoice attached.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it all the way down. "Go to sleep. I love you. I'll tell you everything on Sunday."

"You better. Night, Saint Eleonora."

"Night, you menace."

She did, in fact, work on it all night. Tio had been right about the laptop fan. She built the world until it was so complete that she could walk around inside it, until she knew exactly what she'd say and the order she'd say it in and which slide she'd let the silence sit on, until the deck stopped being a pitch and became a *place*, somewhere that already existed and just needed someone to agree to move in. And somewhere around four in the morning, with the city outside its lowest and quietest, the orange light steady, the dryers finally still, Ella Savage-Oluwa sat back from the finished thing and let herself feel, fully, undefended, for one unguarded minute, exactly how much she wanted it — and she did not manage the feeling, did not file it, did not charge it rent. She just wanted it, hugely, the way she hadn't let herself want anything since she was nine years old and flicked a switch.

Tomorrow she would carry the deck up a staircase that smelled of old paper. Tomorrow she would find out that the ghost behind the boards had a face she'd already memorised across a loud room. Tomorrow the two boxes of her life and the two boxes of his would be set down on the same table and neither of them would ever get them fully closed again.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only a girl and a finished world and a wanting she'd finally let herself feel, and outside, the first of the morning's fine orange rain beginning again on the empty Hulme street, beautiful and slightly ruined, the way she'd taught herself to love it.

She slept, finally, just before five. She did not dream in four notes this time. She did not dream at all. She slept the deep dreamless sleep of someone who has, after a very long time, set something down — not knowing it was the last full night's sleep she'd get before her whole carefully managed life cracked open along a seam she'd forgotten was there, and the light got in.

## ◆ ix. The Loudest Room (The Other Side)

*Cal*

Here is what the party had looked like from where Cal was standing, which was nowhere anyone could see him properly, which was how he liked it.

He hadn't wanted to come. He never wanted to come. But Tom had asked, and Tom asked for so little — Tom, who covered for Cal's disappearances without ever being told what he was covering, who had decided two years ago that whatever Cal was hiding was Cal's to hide and had simply gone on liking him through the locked door — and the currency of a friendship like that was that when Tom finally asked for the small thing, you paid it. So Cal had come to a terrace on Egerton Road and stood in the kitchen with a bottle he wasn't drinking and let the night happen at him, doing the only thing he knew how to do in a room, which was read it.

He read it the way he read a defensive line. Not for pleasure — for *threats and gaps.* Where were the people who might want something from him; where were the conversations he'd have to perform through; where was the exit. He clocked the lad who'd cornered a girl and was explaining something at her, and filed him under *avoid.* He clocked two of his own teammates getting to the stage of drunk where they'd want him to do a thing, a shot, a story, a performance of being one of the lads, and he calculated the angles to not be where they were looking when the wanting peaked. He clocked the fashion-society girls documenting the night instead of having it, and felt the small recognition he always felt around people who'd turned their own lives into a thing to be observed, because he'd done the same, hadn't he, turned his whole self into footage, into a highlight reel a protein company called the future.

And then his eyes, finishing their circuit, snagged on a wall, and a girl against it, and the snag was so unexpected that he actually lost his place in the read.

Because she was doing it too.

She was standing exactly the way he was standing — present and absent at once, in the room and behind glass, her eyes moving over the crowd in the slow methodical sweep of someone taking inventory rather than someone looking for fun. He knew that sweep. He'd never once seen it on another human being, because the people doing it were, by definition, the ones you didn't notice, that was the whole point of the sweep, it made you furniture — and yet he'd noticed her, the way you notice the one other person in a foreign country reading a book in your language. *That's mine,* some animal part of him said, with no romance in it at all, just the shock of recognition. *That's the thing I do. She does the thing I do.*

He should have left it there. The smart play, the only play that protected the locked room and the held breath and the budget of his attention, was to finish the read, find Tom, do his time, and go. He knew that. He calculated it in real time. And then the girl's sweep arrived at him — caught him mid-read, caught the watcher watching — and instead of the flick-away that everyone did, the small social apology of *sorry, didn't mean to look,* she held it. Eyebrow up. A challenge with no fear in it. *And?*

And Cal Vance, who calculated everything, did a thing he could not afterward account for, which was push off the counter and cross the room toward a stranger for no reason that survived contact with his own logic.

He'd told himself, walking over, that he was running the play — the read, the soft landing, the bit he could do in his sleep — because telling himself that was easier than admitting he was crossing a room because he was *lonely*, a word he did not allow himself, a word with no place in the swagger, a word that belonged to the locked room. And she'd known. That was the thing that undid him, standing there in the spilled-beer fug while she dismantled him on the table between them, calm as a surgeon. *You picked the one girl who isn't already looking at you. It's a clean bet. It was never about me. I'm a favourable position.* She'd read not just the move but the *architecture* of the move, the scaffolding he'd thought was invisible, the load-bearing fear underneath the charm — and she'd done it in eleven seconds, and she'd been *right*, and being seen that accurately should have felt like exposure, like a hand closing around the held breath, and instead —

Instead it had felt like the clean file. Like opening a session in the dark with the lamp on. Like the one place in his life where the machine switched off.

He'd laughed. Actually laughed, the real one, the one that turned heads because the boys had never heard it, and he'd heard himself laugh and thought, with the cold clear part of him, *that's a tell, that's a crack in the wall, walk it back* — and he hadn't been able to walk it back, because she'd earned it, and the cold clear part of him had a rule that you paid what you owed at a table, even a sticky one in Fallowfield, even to a stranger. *Fair.*

And then he'd done the thing he was proudest of and least able to explain. He hadn't pressed the advantage. He hadn't, having been caught, tried to recover by being charming about being caught, which was the next move, the recovery move, the one that turned exposure back into seduction. He'd just — told her one true thing. *I knew you'd see through the play. I came over anyway.* And then he'd left, because the true thing had cost him everything he had and he'd had nothing left to spend, and because leaving first was the only move he had ever fully trusted, the family move, the *get out before it can get out of you* move, except this time it hadn't felt like the ghost thing, it had felt like the opposite, like setting down a thing he wanted to carry because his arms weren't strong enough yet and he needed to come back when they were.

He'd watched the door she left through for forty minutes. Tom had asked twice if he was all right. He'd said he was tired. He'd gone home and not slept and opened a clean file and put a stranger in the bright top of a song where his brother lived in the dark bottom of it, and he had understood, lying awake, the precise size of the danger: that he had met, at a party he hadn't wanted to attend, the first person in five years capable of reading his hips instead of his eyes — and that a man whose entire life depended on no one reading his hips had just gone out of his way to be read, and had liked it, and was going to have to spend the next however-long pretending, to her and to himself, that he hadn't.

He did not know her name. He'd been too busy losing to ask. He turned it over now, in the bare professional bedroom, the not-knowing — *Tom said her name with a tone, the stylist, Ella* — Tom had given it to him this morning at training and Cal had filed it with elaborate carelessness, *which one,* as if he hadn't already built a clean file around the shape of her. Ella. He let himself have the name once, in the dark, the way you let yourself have one cigarette you swore you'd quit.

Then he locked it, with everything else, and ran the machine, and told himself it was nothing, a party, a stranger, a five-minute thing — and somewhere two miles south the stranger was telling herself the identical lie in a launderette-warm room, and neither of them knew that the lie had a deadline, and the deadline was a Thursday, and the Thursday was nearly here.

## ◆ x. Brother (Five Years Back)

*Cal*

The thing people never understood about grief — the thing Cal had stopped trying to explain to anyone, because explaining it required showing them the room — was that it wasn't the absence that wrecked you. The absence you could almost manage; the absence was a fact, flat, daily, survivable. What wrecked you was the *presence.* The way a dead person stayed exactly as alive as they'd been, in you, forever, while never doing one new thing. Jordan was twenty-two in Cal's head and would be twenty-two when Cal was forty, frozen, perfect, pulling a stupid face in a car, saying *listen to the bit at the end* — and that frozen perfect presence was the thing that made Cal make music, because music was the only place where Jordan could still do something new. A track was the one machine Cal had ever found that let his dead brother keep working.

He'd been eleven the first time he understood Jordan was different.

It was the box room in the old house, before their mother left, before the wet road, before any of it — a box room their father had grudgingly let Jordan have for "the music thing," in a tone that already, even then, contained the whole future, the *no living in it*, the patience that was really disappointment wearing patience's clothes. Jordan was seventeen and had a setup he'd built out of nothing: a borrowed laptop, a controller bought with a summer of car-washing, a milk crate of charity-shop records, foam tiles on the walls that did nothing acoustically and everything spiritually. And eleven-year-old Cal had sat on the floor of that box room, where he was not technically allowed, while his big brother made the air into shapes.

"Listen," Jordan would say. Always the same word, the word that came back five years later as the last word. *Listen.* "You hear that? That's not the kick. Listen *under* the kick." And he'd play it again, and Cal would screw his face up, and then — sometimes, on the good days — he'd hear it, the thing under the thing, the ghost note, the part of the music that wasn't being played but was being *implied*, the space the kick left that your body filled in for itself. "There," Jordan would breathe, watching Cal's face change. "You got it. That's the whole game, little man. Anyone can play you a note. The trick is making them hear the note you *didn't* play. That's where the feeling lives. In the gap."

*In the gap.* Cal had carried that his whole life without knowing he was carrying it. It was why his passing was different. Marsh thought it was vision, timing, some coachable quantity — *you've got time on the ball, son* — and it wasn't any of those things, it was Jordan, it was a seventeen-year-old in a box room teaching an eleven-year-old that the realest thing in any sequence is the part you leave out, the implied man, the space the defender fills with the wrong guess because you sold him the gap with your hips. The flat pass and the ghost note were the same lesson. Cal had learned to play rugby from his brother's beats and had never told a living soul, because the soul he'd have told was the one who'd taught him, and that soul was twenty-two forever in a frozen car.

Their father had never once come into the box room.

That was the detail Cal kept returning to, the one that explained everything if you let it. Gareth Vance had come to every one of Cal's matches from age six. He had stood at every touchline in the rain. He had driven hours. He had built a whole machine around the son who played. And he had never, not once, in seven years, climbed the stairs and put his head into the box room where his other son was making something genuinely extraordinary out of nothing — because that room contained a thing Gareth couldn't see the use of, and a man who has decided the world is divided into useful and wasteful cannot afford to look directly at the wasteful, in case it turns out to be beautiful, in case the beauty indicts the whole system of usefulness he's built his life on. So he didn't look. For seven years he didn't look. And Jordan, who was seventeen and then eighteen and then nineteen, making better and better things in a smaller and smaller room, learned the lesson that the gap teaches when it's a person and not a kick drum: that the thing your father won't look at, you start to believe doesn't exist. That you start, slowly, to not look at it yourself. That you get smaller to fit the size of the attention available.

He didn't fall apart loud. Cal had said that to Ella, weeks from now, and it would be the truest sentence about Jordan he'd ever speak. *He fell apart quiet. He got smaller.* He stopped playing Cal stuff. He started driving further for sessions, Leeds and Sheffield and once all the way to Bristol, chasing the recognition that the box room couldn't give him and the touchline-father would never give him, tired all the time, *they said tired*, and the night it happened he'd been coming back from Leeds at midnight on a wet road having made, presumably, the thing he wanted Cal to listen to, the bit at the end, the thing he'd cracked — and the voice note sat unopened in a sixteen-year-old's phone for two days because the sixteen-year-old was at training, building, under the touchline, becoming the useful son, while the wasteful son's last new thing went unheard in a dead phone in a wrecked car in a yard in Leeds.

Two days. Cal had done a lot of terrible arithmetic in five years and that was the sum that never came out. Two days he could have replied. Two days the bit at the end existed and he didn't listen. And the cruelty of it — the specific, surgical, lifelong cruelty — was that the lesson Jordan had spent seven years teaching him was *listen under the kick, listen for the thing that isn't being played* — and when his brother had finally, literally, said *listen*, Cal hadn't. He'd been the gap. He'd been the note that didn't get played. And he'd spent every track since trying to play it, four notes, three that climb and one that falls, the bit at the end, over and over, into the headphones of a quarter of a million strangers, *listen, listen under the kick, my brother is down there, you're carrying him, you don't even know* — because if enough people heard the note Cal had failed to hear, then maybe, by some accounting Cal didn't believe in but performed anyway, the note would finally have been heard, and Jordan would finally have been listened to, and the sum would come out.

It never came out. That was grief. Not the absence. The arithmetic that never resolves, run nightly, forever, in a locked room with a name on the door.

He'd been seventeen when he made the first beat with a buried note in it. He hadn't planned the burying; it had just happened, his hands finding Jordan's four notes the way they found the flat pass, and he'd pitched them down and tucked them under the drums because hearing them clearly was unbearable and not having them at all was worse, and somewhere in that compromise — audible but hidden, present but safe, there but un-loseable — he had invented, without a word for it, the entire philosophy of the rest of his life. Bury the true thing where it can't be taken. Be a ghost so you can't be left. Make the realest part the part nobody can see.

It had worked for five years. It was, by every measure that mattered to the machine, a success: the streams, the EP, the academy contract coming, the future a man called him four times. And it had cost him the ability to be in a single room as a single whole person, which was a cost he'd considered acceptable, because the alternative — being seen, all of him, in the daylight, the pitch and the box room at once, in front of the father who wouldn't climb the stairs — was not survivable, he was certain, he had run the odds, the odds said it would kill him.

The odds had never met Ella Savage-Oluwa.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only a man in a bare room, twenty-two, holding his breath, doing the arithmetic, with a clean file on a drive that for the first time in five years had something *living* in the bright top of it — and downstairs in the dark, on a drive in his chest he never opened, the bit at the end, still unheard, still waiting, the way it had waited for two days that became five years that were, though he could not yet imagine it, about to end — because a girl was going to hum the four notes by accident in a studio in three weeks, not knowing what they were, and Cal was going to do the one thing he had sworn for five years he would never do, which was let someone listen under the kick.

He slept eventually, badly, the way he always did. And in the grey before the alarm he surfaced once from the recurring dream — the ball leaving his hands, hanging forever in a floodlit dark, never landing — except this time, just before he woke, it landed. Soft. Weighted. Into the hands of someone he couldn't see, on the far side of a room he hadn't crossed yet.

He woke not remembering it. But his hands were twitching the shape of the release, and there was a Thursday coming, and the whole grey city was waiting under its fine orange rain for the thing that was already, finally, climbing the stairs.

## ◆ xi. The Switch (A Kitchen, Long Ago)

*Ella*

Ella did not have a box room and a brother and a wet road. Her wound was quieter than Cal's, and older, and she'd carried it so long that she'd stopped experiencing it as a wound at all and started experiencing it as simply the shape of her — the way a tree grows around a fence until the fence is inside the wood and the tree would not know how to be a tree without it.

She was nine. The flat in Lewisham then was smaller than the house they'd later build a community inside; it was the in-between place, the rented place, the place with the damp patch in the bathroom shaped like a country no one could name and the boiler that had to be hit in a specific spot. Tioluwa was a baby. The boys weren't born yet. And there was a year — Ella could not have told you which year on a calendar, only that she was nine, only that it was the year of the damp-country and the hittable boiler — when her father lost the job.

Not the minicab years; those came after, and were hard but were at least *motion*, her father out in the world doing a thing. This was before that, the gap between, the bad gap, when the first proper job, the one with the security and the lanyard and the pension her parents had crossed water to earn the right to, simply ended — a restructure, a word a nine-year-old learned to fear without understanding — and there was, for a stretch of months, no money coming in and a baby and a damp country on the wall and a silence in the flat that was louder than any party.

Her parents did not fight. That was the thing Ella would try, much later, to make people understand about her family, and would mostly fail. They didn't fight, didn't break things, didn't raise voices. They were dignified the way people are dignified when dignity is the only asset that can't be repossessed. What happened instead was the kitchen table at midnight.

Ella was supposed to be asleep. But the flat was small and the damp made her chest tight and she'd got up for water, padding down the cold hall in her socks, and she'd stopped at the kitchen door because the light was on and her mother was at the table and her mother was — not crying. That was the thing. Folake Savage-Oluwa, who had been a doctor in a country that respected her and a healthcare assistant in one that didn't, sat at a kitchen table at midnight over a spread of letters and bills, and she was holding herself so still and so straight and breathing so carefully, in for four and out for four, that nine-year-old Ella understood, in the wordless way children understand the enormous things, that her mother was *not crying on purpose*, was performing not-crying with the entire force of her will, because somewhere in the flat were children who might wake and pad down a cold hall, and those children must never, ever see.

And her father came in, in his vest, unable to sleep either, and he did not say *it'll be all right* because they did not lie to each other, and he did not say *I'm sorry* because there was nothing to be sorry for, a restructure is not a sin, and instead he sat down across from her mother at the midnight table and put his big hand flat on the table between them — not holding her hand, just there, a thing in the world that was on her side — and her mother put her own hand flat next to it, not touching, just there, and the two of them sat in the kitchen light over the letters breathing in for four and out for four, two people carrying a thing too heavy to lift and refusing, with everything they had, to let it crush the sleeping children in the next room.

Ella watched from the dark hall for a long time. And somewhere in that watching, she flicked the switch.

She didn't have words for it then; she had them now. *I will never be a thing they have to carry. There are two of them and the weight is already too heavy and there is a baby and I am nine and the most useful thing — the only useful thing — I can do is be the one they never have to worry about. I will be easy. I will be sorted. I will handle myself so completely that I am a relief and not a weight. I will take the part of me that needs things and I will fold it up small and put it somewhere they'll never find it, so that when they do the midnight arithmetic, I am not in the sum. I will subtract myself from the cost of their lives.*

And she did. That was the thing. A nine-year-old made a vow in a cold hall and *kept it*, for twelve years, with a discipline that would have frightened her parents far more than any tantrum, because tantrums are children being children and what Ella did was stop, deliberately, being a child at all. She got the good grades without being asked. She translated the letters that came in difficult English. She learned the boiler's hittable spot and hit it so her father wouldn't have to come home to a cold flat. She held the baby. She became, by twelve, so thoroughly the easy one that her parents' relief calcified into expectation and the expectation calcified into identity and somewhere in there the seam between Eleanora-who-handled-it and Eleonora-underneath sealed over so completely that even Ella couldn't find it with her fingernails.

The cost did not present itself as a cost for years. That was the trap of it. Folding the needing-part away didn't hurt; it felt like *strength*, felt like virtue, got rewarded constantly — *so mature, so sorted, your sister is a credit to you, omo mi* — and a thing that feels like strength and gets you praised does not announce itself as the slow amputation it actually is. It was only later, at eighteen, at nineteen, in a city far enough to be hers, that Ella started to notice the absence: that she did not know how to be looked after, flinched from it like a hand near a burn; that she experienced a friend buying her a drink as the opening of a debt; that she stood at the side of every room she entered with a clipboard, project-managing other people's nights, because being *in* a night, undefended, present, needing things, was a country she'd emigrated from at nine and forgotten the language of.

And it was only this week — this strange cracked-open week of a real chance and a boy who read hips — that she'd started to suspect the deepest thing, the thing under the kick: that the reason she wanted creative direction so badly, more than was reasonable, more than was safe, was that building a world for a sound was the one and only activity in her entire life where the needing-part got to come out of its fold. Where she wasn't managing anyone's feelings. Where she wasn't being a relief. Where she got to want something hugely and out loud and just for herself and call it work so that wanting it was allowed. The studio — though she didn't know yet that there'd be a studio, didn't know yet whose it was — was going to be for her exactly what the box room had been for Jordan and the unmarked room was for Cal: the one place the switch flicked back on. The one room where the held breath came out.

She just didn't know it yet. On the Thursday morning, surfacing from her first dreamless sleep in weeks, she knew only that she had a deck, and a chance, and a staircase to climb, and a switch she'd flicked at nine that had no idea it was about to meet a boy who'd been holding his breath since sixteen — two people who'd each, separately, in a cold hall and a box room, learned the identical lesson at the hands of love and fear, and folded themselves away, and become legendary to everyone around them for a strength that was really just a wound with excellent posture.

She got up. She made coffee. She chose what to wear with the specific care of a woman who knows that what you wear is the first sentence you say in a room. And she did not know — could not have known, standing in the grey Hulme light pulling on the coat — that by the end of the day someone was finally going to read her hips, and that the reading was going to feel, after twelve years of perfect posture, like the first full breath of her adult life.

## ◆ xii. The Approach

*Ella*

The Northern Quarter on a Thursday afternoon was the version of Manchester that Ella loved most and trusted least — loved because it was beautiful, the red brick and the iron and the murals and the record shops and the coffee places where people who made things drank flat whites and pretended they weren't networking; trusted least because it was the version most easily faked, the version that had been photographed onto so many moodboards (including, she could admit, some of hers) that it was always one bad investment away from becoming a theme park of itself. She walked up from the bus through the cold bright afternoon with the leather folder under her arm — the deck lived on her laptop but she'd printed the world too, the physical artefact, because she'd learned that an A&R will scroll past a screen and *hold* a page, and holding is closer to owning — and she let the walk do what walks did for her, which was settle the noise.

She was nervous. She let herself be nervous; she'd learned that pretending not to be nervous spent more energy than the nerves themselves. But under the nerves was the thing that had got loose at four in the morning and not gone back in its fold: the wanting. Big and undefended. *This one I'm allowed to want.*

She found the address Knox's sound guy had sent — a doorway between a vintage shop and a place that sold expensive plants, no buzzer label, no sign, a door that announced its own importance through refusing to announce anything, which made Ella's stylist brain note, with grudging approval and a flicker of irritation, *the whole no-sign thing is a brand and they know it's a brand and pretending it isn't a brand is itself the brand.* She stood in front of it for a moment, in the cold, in the orange-tinged afternoon light that even the sun in this city seemed to apologise for, and she did the thing she always did before a room that mattered: she put her hand flat against her own sternum, in for four, out for four — her mother's breath, the midnight-table breath, the not-crying breath, inherited and repurposed — and she made the wanting sit down without folding it away.

*You built a world,* she told herself. *Walk in there like you already live in it.*

She did not feel, climbing the stairs that smelled of damp and old paper and the cold of a building between purposes, any premonition. There was no shiver, no four-note melody surfacing from the dreamless dark, no sense of the two halves of her life about to be set on one table. She felt only the ordinary enormous nervousness of a twenty-one-year-old about to pitch for the thing that could change everything, and the stairs were just stairs, steep and narrow and worn in the middle where a hundred years of feet had worn them, and the door at the top was just a door, scuffed, with a handle polished bright by use and no sign on it, and behind it she could hear — faintly, through the cheap hollow wood — music.

She stopped on the top step to listen, because listening was the job, because you could learn more about a room from ten seconds of the sound coming out of it than from ten minutes inside it. And what she heard, muffled through the door, was a track she didn't know — a Knox track, she assumed, an unreleased one, the new project — and it was the *V* sound, unmistakably, the depth and the late-night ache, the drums that hung back half a beat like they didn't want to arrive: the sound she'd spent a sleepless week building a world around, the sound she could have picked out of any lineup, the sound she was, in a sense, in love with already, in the uncomplicated way you can love a sound before you know it has a face.

And under it — so deep she felt it in her chest before she heard it in her ears, the way you're meant to, the way it was designed — four notes.

Three that climbed. One that fell.

Something moved at the very back of Ella's mind, a small dark fish under ice, a sense of having heard this before, of a dream she couldn't retrieve, of being homesick for somewhere she'd never been — and it was gone before she could name it, surfaced and dived, leaving only a faint ache and the cold afternoon and the scuffed door and her own hand, she realised, already raised to knock.

She knocked.

The music stopped. A chair scraped. Voices — more than she'd expected, a whole room of them, Knox's laugh bright and unmistakable above the rest, someone saying *that'll be her*. Feet. The bright-polished handle turned.

And Eleonora Savage-Oluwa, who saw everything, who had built a world and climbed a staircase and let herself want a thing out loud for the first time since she was nine years old, stood on the worn top step with her printed world under her arm and her mother's breath in her chest, and waited for the door of the rest of her life to open — not knowing, not yet, that the ghost behind the boards had a face she'd already memorised across the loudest room in Fallowfield, and that the four notes she'd just half-recognised through the door belonged to a boy in a frozen car, and that the deal she was about to strike to save her future was going to be, without either of them planning it, the beginning of the only thing that ever finally made both of them set the held breath down.

The door opened.

## ◆ xiii. The Collision

*Cal*

Knox had arrived at the studio the way Knox arrived everywhere, which was as a weather system — door banging, hood down, a carrier bag of cans swinging from one hand and his phone held out in front of his mouth like a microphone while he finished a voice note to someone, and three people trailing in his wake whom Cal catalogued before any of them had spoken, because cataloguing the room was the first thing Cal did in any room and the studio was *his* room, which made the cataloguing both instinctive and territorial. A videographer with a camera already half-raised, filming the staircase, the door, the texture, building the rollout's behind-the-scenes before there was anything to be behind. A label A&R in box-fresh trainers who kept touching things — the desk, the foam, the spines of Cal's records — with the proprietary curiosity of someone working out what it was all worth. And a woman in expensive understatement, late twenties, who didn't touch anything, who stood just inside the door and let her eyes do what Cal's eyes did, the slow inventory, and who Cal therefore identified instantly as the most dangerous person in the room, the way you always recognise your own kind fastest. Sasha, Knox called her. She smiled at Cal with her whole face and exactly none of her eyes, and he added a line to her file before she'd said three words: *reads rooms. reads people. reads me. careful.*

"V," Knox said, dropping the cans, pulling Cal into a one-armed thing that no one else in the world was allowed to do. He was the only living person who used the name to Cal's face, and he used it like a secret they were both keeping, which it was, which was the whole basis of the only uncomplicated relationship in Cal's life. "Play them the thing. Play them the thing, man, they don't believe me, I've been telling them all week and they think I'm gassing."

"Sit down. You're making the room nervous."

"I *am* the room," Knox said, with total accuracy, and dropped onto the battered sofa under the high window, sprawling, a king on a found throne, and the others arranged themselves, and the A&R said something about scheduling and Q2 and the woman Sasha said something smooth and weightless about *the bigger picture, the rollout, the story, it's all about the story now, the music's the easy part* — and Cal, who did not want to talk about the story, who wanted to talk about nothing, who in an ideal world would have had no one in this room at all, turned to the desk and did the one thing that quieted everything, which was play them the music and let it argue on his behalf.

He played the track he'd been killing himself over for three weeks. The one that sounded like the inside of his head. He'd taken the living girl back out of the bright top of it that morning — couldn't risk anyone hearing a thing he didn't understand yet — so it was the cold true version, the lonely one, drums hanging back half a beat, the chopped vocal pitched into a memory of itself, the low synth moving like weather, and four feet down, where only the body could find it, his brother. He played it loud, on the good monitors, and he did the thing he always did, which was watch the room instead of listening to the music, because he knew the music, the music was the one thing in his life he was certain of, and the only new information available was in the faces.

And he watched it land.

He watched the A&R stop scrolling. Watched the videographer, unprompted, lower the camera to just *listen*, which is the highest compliment a person who films everything can pay a thing. Watched Sasha's professional smile slip, for one unguarded bar, into something genuine and almost hungry, the look of a person who deals in stories recognising a story bigger than she'd been promised. The track ended. The room held the particular silence that good work makes, the silence Cal lived for, the only applause he trusted.

"That," said Sasha, into it. "That is the record. Marcus, I don't care what else is on the project, that's the first single, that's the whole campaign, that's —" she shook her head, and for a second she wasn't a fixer, she was just a person who'd heard a thing. "Who *are* you," she said to Cal, lightly, smiling again, the eyes back in their professional dark. "Genuinely. Two years I've been hearing about V. No face, no name, no socials. You know what that is, at this level? That's not shyness. Shyness doesn't make a record like that. That's a *strategy.* And I love a strategy. I'd love to know whose it is."

"It's the work's strategy," Cal said, even, easy, the flat pass. "The work doesn't need a face. Faces just give people something to talk about instead of the work."

"Mm," said Sasha, and the *mm* was Webb's *mm*, the *mm* of a professional choosing not to push yet, filing it, and Cal understood with total clarity that he had just been identified as the most interesting unsolved problem in the room by exactly the kind of person whose job was solving such problems, and that this was a thing he would have to manage, starting now, forever.

"We need to talk about who builds the world for this," Sasha went on, all business. "The visual identity. I am not putting a sound like *that* out with a stock-template rollout and a man in a hoodie standing in front of a brick wall. We need an eye. A real one, that costs less than the eyes that cost forty grand, which means young, which means hungry, which means we find them now before they know what they're worth. Marcus has a candidate."

"My boy Tom knows her," Knox said, from the sofa. "Well — Tom knows everyone, Tom's like the rugby internet. She did a thing for 'Lowlight,' just for the love, put it online, no one paid her, and it's —" he kissed his fingers, a chef in an advert. "She *gets* it. Not the look. The *feeling.* She's coming now, should be —"

The buzzer went.

And Cal — who had hit that door release a thousand times, half-listening, his mind always somewhere in a mix — got up and crossed to the little panel by the door and pressed the button without thinking, the way you do the small automatic things, his attention still down in the track wondering whether the chopped vocal wanted to come back in the second eight, and he heard the street door go below and feet on the stairs that smelled of old paper, climbing, and he turned back toward the desk to pull up the session for her to hear, *some art direction girl, real vision, you'll like the sound of her stuff* —

The studio door opened.

And Ella Savage-Oluwa walked in out of the grey Northern Quarter light with a leather folder under her arm and her face composed into pure, expensive, weaponised professionalism — *good afternoon, thank you so much for seeing me* — already scanning the room the way she'd scanned the party, the way Cal scanned every room, the slow methodical inventory, and her eyes went round it: the sofa, Knox, the A&R, Sasha, the half-raised camera, the high window, the monitors. And arrived at the desk.

At him.

Cal watched it happen in real time — the scan reaching him, the recognition firing, the entire architecture of her afternoon reorganising itself behind her eyes in the space of a single held breath — and to her enormous and instantly admirable credit, not one muscle in her face moved. The professionalism stayed welded on. But he knew her now; he'd spent a week not thinking about her with the dedication of a man thinking about someone constantly; he'd built a clean file around the shape of her; and so he saw the thing underneath the welded calm, the thing she'd done at the party, the holding-of-the-gaze-instead-of-the-apology — except now, behind it, fractionally, he saw the exact thing he was feeling, the thing he'd have bet money she was feeling, the small internal free-fall that in his head sounded like: *oh, no.*

And, half a beat behind it, its terrible twin, which they would both spend weeks pretending hadn't also been there: *oh.*

Because here it was. The collision he hadn't seen coming, that no read could have predicted, that the odds said was absurd — the one person in the city who could read his hips, the stranger he'd put in the bright top of a song, walking into the one room where the locked door had no lock, the room where he was V, in front of a label and a fixer and the artist who knew the secret, holding a folder with a world in it, about to discover with everyone watching that the ghost she'd been hired to dress had a face she'd already taken apart on a table in Fallowfield.

The whole shape of both their lives wobbled on the head of a pin for one long bar of silence.

"We've met," Ella said.

It was, Cal thought, with the helpless surge of admiration he'd felt at the party, the single most perfect move available. *Truthful* — so she couldn't be caught later in a lie, in a room where one of these people filmed everything and another solved people for a living. *Vague* — so it surrendered nothing, named nothing, gave the camera and the fixer no story to seize. And it threw the next word to *him* — made him choose, in front of everyone, exactly how much to spend, exactly which version of the truth to release into a room that absolutely could not, under any circumstances, learn that "V" was a fly-half whose father stood at a touchline at six in the morning. She'd handed him the ball flat and weighted and let him decide where to put it, and she'd done it in two words, and the two words had bought them both the only thing that mattered, which was time.

"At a party," Cal said. Even. Easy. The daylight voice. The flat pass, sold with the face. "Friend of Tom's, right. Few weeks back."

"Right." Ella crossed the room and put out her hand and he took it and they shook — firm, brief, professional, two strangers who'd met once in passing — and her hand was warm and dry and her grip told him nothing because she'd built it to tell him nothing, and her eyes, for the half-second they were close enough that only he could read them, told him an entire paragraph her mouth wasn't: *I don't know what you are. I don't know what this is. But I need this room more than I need to understand it, and you need it quiet, so we are both going to be very, very careful, and you are going to follow my lead, because I have just done you the largest favour of your life and you know it.* He let go of her hand half a second after he should have. He saw Sasha see it.

"Small world," Sasha said pleasantly, watching them both with the patient total attention of a person whose entire profession was noticing the things people tried not to show. Cal added another line to her file. *Saw the half-second. Filing it. Dangerous.*

"Manchester's a village pretending to be a city," Ella said smoothly, without missing a beat, turning the line into nothing, turning herself fully toward Knox and Sasha and the actual room — and *dropping Cal entirely*, cutting him from her attention with a completeness that was the right move, the only move, and that stung him in a place he had absolutely no business being stung. "Can I play you something? Well — I built a deck, it's on here —" she touched the leather folder "— but honestly the deck's secondary. I'd rather you heard the world first and then I'll show you how I'd build it. Can we —" she gestured, with beautiful nerve, at the desk, at the monitors, at *him*, at the producer she'd met at a party, "— can we play the single again? And I'll talk over it. I work better out loud."

"You haven't heard the single," the A&R said. "It's not out. It's not anywhere. You can't have —"

"I don't need to have heard *this* one to know what it's doing," Ella said. "I've heard everything else he's made. I've spent a week inside it. I could draw you the shape of this single with my eyes shut." She looked, finally, briefly, at Cal — at the producer, at V, a candidate addressing the magic she'd been hired to dress — and there was nothing in her face but the work. "May I?"

And Cal looked at her, and she looked at him, and the room did not know it was watching the third move of a game whose first two moves had happened against a wall on Egerton Road. He reached over and cued the track. He pressed play.

And Ella Savage-Oluwa stood in the middle of the unmarked studio above the vintage shop and talked over his music — *his* music, the inside of his own head, the cold true lonely version, the one with his brother four feet down — and what she said about it took the floor clean out from under him, slow, like a held breath finally going.

She didn't talk about it the way a stylist sells a service. She didn't reach for adjectives or references or the names of other campaigns. She talked about it like she had been *inside* it, like she'd been living in the track for a week, which she had. "This sound is nostalgic for a present it's still living in," she said, walking the room slowly, her eyes half-closed, her hand drifting as if she were touching the air the sound moved through. "It's homesick for *now.* That's the whole feeling and everything has to serve it. The drums are late on purpose — hear it, they're reluctant, they don't want to arrive, like someone putting off going home. It's the sound of a person who is brilliant at something and resents being watched doing it." She had no idea. She was reading the music the way she'd read the room, the way she'd read *him*, naming the architecture, the load-bearing fear under the charm — and she did not know that the man whose interior she was describing aloud to a record label was standing four feet away with his hand still on the desk, not breathing. "So the world can't be slick. Slick would be a lie, and this track does not tell a single lie, which is rare, which is the whole reason it's good. It has to look like the inside of a night bus at three in the morning — that orange streetlight, condensation on the glass, everything beautiful and slightly ruined and just past being yours. I'd shoot all of it on the wrong film stock, the cheap expired stuff, so the colour bleeds. I'd make the type look like it got left out in the rain and dried wrong. The whole identity should feel like —" she opened her eyes, and they were bright, and she said the thing that nearly ended him "— like a secret that got out. Like something private that everyone can suddenly see, and it's beautiful, and it's also a kind of grief, because the second a secret gets out it stops being yours."

The room was silent. Even Sasha. The track played on under her words, the drums refusing to arrive, and four feet down, where no one knew to listen, three notes climbed and one fell.

*A secret that got out,* Cal thought, and did not trust himself to make a single sound.

"That," said Knox, very quietly, sitting up off the sofa for the first time, "is exactly the thing. That's exactly *it*, that's what I've been trying to say all week and couldn't." He looked at Sasha. Sasha looked at the A&R. The A&R, slowly, nodded.

"Show us the deck," Sasha said. "Though I think we already know."

Ella opened the leather folder.

And Cal Vance — who had spent five years building a wall between the boy at the touchline and the ghost behind the boards, who had organised his entire existence around the single principle that no one would ever be allowed to stand in both his rooms at once, who had made himself into a held breath specifically so that the two halves of him could never be in the same frame — watched the one girl who'd ever read his hips walk straight through the wall as if it weren't there and set up her work on the far side of it, in front of witnesses, and he understood, with the cold clean clarity he normally reserved for reading a defensive line a half-second before it broke, that the wall was already gone. Not breached. *Gone.* It had stopped existing the moment she walked in, because a wall only works if no one can see both sides, and now there was a person in the world who could see both sides at once, who'd seen the ghost and the swagger and read the same fear under each, and you cannot rebuild a wall in front of the one person who has already seen around it.

She got the job. Of course she got the job. There was never, from the moment the door opened, any version of the afternoon where she didn't. The deck was extraordinary — Cal watched the A&R hold the printed pages instead of scrolling, exactly as she'd known he would — but the deck was almost beside the point; she'd won the room when she described the inside of his head to his face and called it a brief. Sasha said the words *we'd love to bring you on* before Ella had reached the third page, and started talking timelines and day-rates that made Ella's professional mask flicker for one human instant into a twenty-one-year-old who'd just changed her own life, and Knox came off the sofa and hugged her, and the videographer filmed it, and the A&R shook her hand, and the room filled up with the warm chaos of a thing decided.

And through all of it Cal stood at his desk in the room where he kept the only true part of himself, and watched a stranger he'd lost to at a party become, in the space of forty minutes, the keeper of his secret, the architect of his world, and the single greatest threat to the life he'd spent five years building — and felt, underneath the cold clear understanding of how much trouble he was in, a thing he had not felt since a box room and a brother and the word *listen*, a thing he had no business feeling, a thing that frightened him more than Sasha and Webb and his father and the whole machine combined.

He felt seen. And he didn't want it to stop.
