> *Long chapter · target ~45,000 words · built progressively. Scenes marked with ◆.*

## ◆ i. The Loudest Room

*Ella*

The house on Egerton Road had been sweating since ten o'clock, and by midnight the walls had given up pretending to be walls. Bass came up through the floorboards and into the soles of Ella's boots, into her teeth, into the hollow under her ribs where she kept the things she didn't say out loud. Somebody had hung fairy lights from the picture rail and somebody else had pulled half of them down, so the front room flickered like a signal going out, and in the strobing dark of it three years of Fallowfield student life were compressed into one heaving organism — second-years and final-years and a few brave masters students, the union crowd and the society crowd and the crowd that belonged to no crowd, all of them shoulder to shoulder in a terraced house built in 1900 for a family of six and currently containing approximately ninety.

Ella catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, because it was the only way she knew how to stand in a room: by silhouette and palette and the small tells of who each person was trying to be. The boy in the doorway in the deliberately ugly jumper, performing the specific carelessness that takes forty minutes in front of a mirror. The girls from the fashion society reposting each other's stories in real time, building a record of a good night they were too busy documenting to have. The lad from her seminar who had once spent forty minutes explaining her own course to her and was now explaining something to a girl who had the trapped, polite expression of prey. Ella saw all of it. She always saw all of it. It was her gift and it was her exile, the watching — it meant she was never quite *in* a room, always a half-step outside it, the stylist's distance, the eye that frames rather than enters.

"You're doing the face," Priya shouted into her ear.

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing the face that says you're already writing the text you're going to send me tomorrow about how you should have stayed in." Priya pressed a warm can into Ella's hand and beamed at her, lipstick immaculate, eyeliner immaculate, the whole of her assembled with a confidence Ella envied and had, in point of fact, styled half of — the slip dress was Priya's but the layering over it, the boots, the single architectural earring, those were Ella, a Tuesday-night favour that had taken twenty minutes and that Priya had worn to three events since. "One hour. Give me one hour of being a person and then you can go home and be a genius."

"I'm always a person."

"You're always a *project manager*. There's a difference."

That landed harder than Priya meant it to, the way the truest things from your best friend always did, casual as a dropped glass. Ella looked away so it wouldn't show on her face and let the comment slide down into the hollow under her ribs to be examined later, at three in the morning, when she examined everything. *Project manager.* Was it an insult? Priya hadn't meant it as one. But it sat too close to a thing Ella already suspected about herself — that somewhere in the long years of being the eldest, the strong one, the one who handled it, she had quietly stopped being a person who *had* experiences and become a person who *managed* them, who stood at the side of her own life with a clipboard making sure everyone else's went smoothly.

"One hour," she said. "Then I'm gone and you don't get to guilt me."

"Deal." Priya was already turning, already gone, swallowed by the crowd toward the kitchen.

Because that was the thing about this particular party. It was a *crossover* — Priya had started seeing one of the rugby lads, Tom, a lock the approximate dimensions of a wardrobe, who laughed like a faulty boiler and who, to be fair, looked at Priya like she had personally invented the sun. And where Tom went, the academy went, filling doorways like an occupying army, drinking with the focused efficiency of professional athletes on their one sanctioned night off the leash, taking up more room than physics should have allowed. The party was therefore two parties pretending to be one: the art-and-fashion ecosystem Ella moved through, all texture and irony and carefully assembled poverty, and the sport ecosystem, all protein and certainty and the particular loudness of young men who have been told they are special since they were eleven years old.

Ella had opinions about the second category. She'd had the opinions for three years without needing to meet a single specimen up close. Boys who walked through the world like it was a pitch and everyone else was the crowd. She'd grown up around enough manufactured certainty — her cousins, the boys at church, the entire architecture of expectation she'd been raised inside — to recognise the type on sight and to know that the confidence was usually a thing other people had installed in them rather than a thing they had earned.

She found a wall and made it hers, can sweating in her hand, and let the party happen at her like weather.

That was when she clocked him.

It wasn't that he was loud. That was the first thing that snagged, actually — that he wasn't. The kitchen was loud, the boys around him were loud, a great churning engine of noise and shoulder-slaps and someone doing something with a bottle opener that was making everyone roar, and at the dead centre of it he was *still*. Leaning against the counter with a bottle he wasn't drinking from, head tipped, apparently listening to whatever one of his mates was bellowing into the side of his face — but his eyes were going round the room the whole time. Slow. Methodical. Left to right and back, taking inventory, the way Ella's own eyes went round a room. Reading it.

She felt the recognition before she understood it: *that's what I do.* That precise quality of being present in a room and absent from it at the same time, the watcher's exile. She had never once seen it on anyone else. People who weren't watching didn't recognise it; people who were watching were too busy watching to be caught at it. But she caught him, because she was doing the identical thing from the opposite wall, and for one disorienting second it was like looking into a mirror that had been set up across a crowded room.

He had the kind of face the camera was going to love and probably already did — sharp at the jaw, a nose that had been broken at least once and set just imperfectly enough to be interesting, dark hair pushed back off his forehead and curling at the nape where he'd let it grow past whatever regulation governed boys like him. Tall, in the specific way of someone assembled for a purpose: not the gym-built bulk of vanity but the functional architecture of a body that *did* something, shoulders and forearms and a way of holding still that suggested enormous held power, a coiled economy. He wore a plain black tee and dark jeans and absolutely nothing else that asked for attention, which was its own kind of asking, and Ella's stylist brain noted it before her better judgement could intervene: *he knows exactly how he looks and he has chosen to look like he hasn't thought about it.* The hardest look to pull off. The look that costs the most to make appear free.

His gaze finished its circuit of the room and arrived at her.

She didn't look away. She'd learned a long time ago that looking away first was a kind of apology, and she was done apologising for taking up space — she'd done enough apologising for it in her first year, when the accents in seminars had made her feel like a guest in a building she was paying to be in, and she'd promised herself somewhere in second year that she would never again be the one who dropped her eyes first. So she held it, eyebrow lifting a fraction, *and?*, and watched something flicker across his face — surprise, maybe, that she hadn't done the thing that girls apparently did when he looked at them, and then, infuriatingly, the slow arrival of a smile. Not a wide one. A private one. Like she had just confirmed something he'd been wondering about, like she was the answer to a question he'd been asking the whole room and she was the first one to come back right.

He pushed off the counter.

"Oh, no," Ella murmured to her can.

"Don't move," Priya hissed, appearing at her elbow from nowhere, vibrating at a frequency Ella could feel in her fillings. "Don't you *dare* move, that's Cal."

"That's a sentence that assumes I know who Cal is."

"Callum Vance. Fly-half. *The* fly-half. Tom says scouts come to watch him, like actual professional scouts, like he's going to be —" Priya did a complicated two-handed gesture meant to convey enormity, oceans, the future. "He never talks to anyone at these things. That's like his whole thing. He turns up, he stands there being —"

"Aloof?"

"I was going to say *fit*, but —"

He arrived.

Up close he was worse, which Ella registered with a flat sort of internal dismay, the dismay of a woman who has a deadline and a dwindling overdraft and a mother who calls at six o'clock and absolutely no room in the budget of her life for the particular trouble currently walking toward her. Up close she could see that the stillness wasn't an act, or wasn't only an act; there was a real attention behind his eyes, an unsettling focus that he turned on her now — not the slow inventory he'd run over the room but something narrower and more total, as though, having identified her as the one anomaly in his survey, he was now devoting the entire instrument to her alone.

"You're not having a good time," he said.

No hello. No name. Just that, delivered low enough that she had to lean in half an inch to catch it over the bass, which she suspected was precisely the point — the engineered intimacy of forcing someone to come closer to hear you.

"I'm having a fine time."

"You're having a *fine* time against a wall, drinking something you don't like —" his eyes flicked to the can, to her hand around it, the untouched weight of it "— leaving in, what." He glanced at nothing, at the middle distance, performing a calculation. "Forty minutes. Because that's the deal you made with her." He tilted his head a precise few degrees toward Priya, who had developed an expression of such delighted, transparent, project-managing matchmaking that Ella wanted to fold her into a small box and post her somewhere far away. "Am I close?"

It was a good trick. She'd give him that. It was the kind of cold reading that worked because it was ninety percent flattery wearing the costume of insight: *I see you, you're complicated, you're not like the others, no one else in this room gets it but I do.* She'd had it tried on her before, usually by men who wanted credit for noticing she was a person, who treated basic perception as a gift they were bestowing. And the thing that made it dangerous — the thing that made it work on the girls who fell for it — was that it was always *partly true*, and the partly-true part made you forget to interrogate the performance wrapped around it.

So she interrogated the performance.

"That's a lot of certainty," Ella said, "for someone who's known me eleven seconds."

"I'm good at reading rooms."

"You're good at reading the *odds*. There's a difference." She let that sit, watched it land, watched the private smile change shape. "You picked the one girl in the room who isn't already looking at you. That's not insight, that's risk management. If I tell you to get lost, you've lost nothing — you didn't want me, you wanted the read to be right, and being told to get lost still proves you read me correctly, so you win. And if I don't tell you to get lost, you get to be the one man perceptive enough to see something everyone else walked past. It's a clean bet. There's no outcome where you lose. You've structured the whole approach so that you can't." She tilted her head, returning his own gesture to him with interest. "Which means it was never actually about me. It was about being right. You'd have run the exact same play on whichever girl in this room was standing alone looking unimpressed. I'm not a person to you yet. I'm a favourable position."

For a second nothing happened. The party roared on around the small pocket of stillness the two of them had made, a held breath in the middle of the noise, and Priya had gone very quiet in the manner of someone watching a car approach a cliff in slow motion and being unable to decide whether to shout.

Then Cal laughed.

It was a real one — surprised out of him, short and low and warmer than anything else about him had suggested, and it changed his whole face for half a second into someone younger and less guarded and frankly more dangerous, before the shutter came back down over it. People nearby turned at the sound, Ella noticed, the way you turn when a usually silent animal makes a noise. *He never talks to anyone at these things.* He apparently also never laughed at them.

"All right," he said. "Fair."

"That's it? Fair?"

"You're right. So — fair." He set his untouched bottle down on the windowsill behind her, deliberately, a small surrender of the prop, and for one absurd moment they were close enough that she could smell him under the spilled-beer fug of the room: something clean and warm and out of place here, cedar maybe, the expensive kind of understatement, the smell equivalent of the plain black tee. "But you got one thing wrong."

"Did I."

"You said there's no outcome where I lose. There was one." His eyes did the thing again, the narrowing, the full instrument, except this time there was nothing detached in it at all, and Ella felt it go through her like the bass through the floor, unwelcome, undeniable, a physical fact she resented in real time. "The outcome where I came over and you turned out to be exactly as interesting as I thought you'd be from across the room. That one costs me. Because now I have to think about it." A beat. "I knew you'd see through the play. I came over anyway. You can decide for yourself what that tells you."

And then — and this was the part that would keep her up later, replaying it with a kind of furious unwilling admiration — he didn't wait. He didn't lean in for whatever the kill was supposed to be, didn't ask for her number, didn't do any of the things the entire approach had appeared to be building toward. He just nodded, once, the way you nod at someone who has taught you something you didn't expect to learn at a party in Fallowfield, and he turned and went back to the kitchen, back into the noise, back to the boys who immediately swallowed him into their churning orbit — and he did not look at her again.

Not once. She watched the back of his head for a full minute to confirm it, and it never turned, and the not-turning was somehow louder than anything he'd said.

"Okay," Priya breathed, reverent, scandalised. "*Okay.* What was that."

"That was a boy who's used to the last move working." Ella's heart was doing something irritating and arrhythmic and she resented it specifically and personally and filed a complaint with herself about it. "And who changed his entire strategy the second it didn't. In real time. Without getting defensive. That's —" she stopped, because the honest end of the sentence was *rare*, and she did not want to say *rare* out loud in front of Priya, who would build a wedding out of a single adjective.

"That sounds like you liked it."

"It sounds like I noticed it. They're different things. I notice everything, it's my entire personality, you've said so yourself."

"You're doing the face again."

She was. She turned it toward the window so the streetlight could have it instead of Priya, and out on Egerton Road the rain had started, fine and orange under the lamps, the specific Manchester rain that isn't weather so much as a permanent condition of the air, and she thought, with a kind of helpless tenderness she only ever felt at the edges of nights like this: *Manchester.*

She'd come here at eighteen because it was far enough from South London to be hers and near enough that her mother could still phone every evening at six. Three years she'd spent assembling a self out of this grey, glittering, rain-soaked, gloriously unbothered city — the Saturday markets and the warehouse nights and the studios in the Northern Quarter where people made things with their actual hands, the whole alternate universe to the one her family had mapped out for her in straight clean lines. *Law. Or finance, if you must be difficult. Something you can name at a dinner. Something with a building you can point to.* She had four months left of it. One final project. One shot at proving — to a course, to a panel of external assessors, to a future employer, to her mother at six o'clock — that the thing she was good at was a thing you could build a life on, and not a thing you did on the side of a real job until the babies came.

She did not have time for a rugby player who read rooms like he was solving them.

"I'm going home," she told Priya. "You stay. Be a person on my behalf. I love you."

"You're so *boring*," Priya said, with enormous and total affection, and hugged her hard enough to crack a rib, and let her go.

The night air outside was a relief, cold and wet and honest after the furnace of the house, and Ella stood on the step for a second letting it find the sweat at her hairline. Behind her the party throbbed on, muffled now by the closed door, ninety people compressed into a 1900s terrace continuing to have the night she had decided to leave. She pulled her coat tight and started down toward the curry mile, where the neon would still be on at this hour, bleeding into the puddles, the whole long bright strip of it doing its sleepless business — and she did not think about Callum Vance.

She thought about it so deliberately that it amounted to thinking about him constantly, which she noted, and filed, and refused.

*I came over anyway. You can decide what that tells you.*

"It tells me you're trouble," she said out loud, to the rain, to the empty wet street, to nobody.

The rain declined to argue. Behind her, three streets back, the loudest room in Fallowfield kept on roaring, and somewhere inside it a boy who never talked to anyone was standing very still, not drinking, watching the door she'd left through for considerably longer than he would ever admit to anyone, including himself, and trying to work out — with the part of his mind that calculated everything, that read the odds, that had never once in his life been wrong-footed at a party — why his hands wanted to make a beat about a girl whose name he hadn't even asked for, because he'd been too busy losing.

## ◆ ii. Six O'Clock

*Ella*

The flat she shared with Priya was two stops and a long walk away, above a launderette in Hulme, and the rent was due on the first, and the launderette's industrial dryers ran a low heat up through the floorboards that made the front room the warmest place Ella had ever lived and the loudest — a permanent tumbling hum, like the building had a heartbeat, or a stomach, a great slow digestion of other people's washing that went on beneath everything she did there. She had come, against every expectation, to love it. She had come to love a great many things in Manchester that she'd had to first learn weren't beneath her, which was a sentence she would never, ever say to her mother, because her mother had spent twenty-five years climbing specifically so that her children would never have to learn that anything was beneath them by living above it.

She got in at half one, peeled off the wet coat, and stood in the tumbling dark of the front room for a moment without turning the light on, the orange of the streetlamp coming through the thin curtain and laying a bar of it across the carcass of her final project, which covered most of the floor.

The project was — she was prepared to admit at this hour, to no one — not working.

The brief, set by her course, was open to the point of cruelty: *Develop and execute a complete visual identity for a real or fictional brand, artist, or campaign.* Most of her cohort were doing fictional. Safe. Controllable. No client to disappoint, no reality to crash against, a closed system in which the only judge was taste. Ella had decided in September, with the specific magnificent arrogance of someone who needs to prove something to people who aren't in the room, that fictional was cowardice. She was going to do *real*. She was going to find a real artist or a real label and art-direct a real thing into the real world, with real stakes and real failure available, and she was going to put it in front of the assessors and the recruiters at the degree show and make it impossible to file her under *lovely eye, shame about the practicality.*

She'd been pitching for two months. She had a folder of polite nos and a much larger folder of no replies at all, the silence that is its own kind of no. She had a moodboard practice that was, objectively, very good — she could take a sound and build it a world, a palette, a typeface, a set of references that made A&Rs go quiet on calls in the specific way that means *oh.* She'd done it for free for two local artists already, just to have the work to show, and the work was good, and good was not translating into the single thing she actually needed, which was a *yes* attached to a name big enough to matter to a stranger.

She crouched in the orange dark and looked at it all — the printouts, the fabric swatches, the colour studies, the test layouts pinned to a board she'd dragged back from a skip on Stretford Road — and felt the familiar two-part feeling that arrived every night at around this time: first the swell of how much she wanted it, the want so large and unhousebroken it frightened her, and then, right behind it, the cold administrative voice that asked whether wanting it this much was simply going to make the disappointment, when it came, proportionally enormous. The strong one's voice. The voice that had learned, at nine, that it was safer to manage a hope than to have one.

She made tea. She did not turn on the big light. She sat in the warm tumbling dark and let the day end.

Her phone, when she finally looked at it, had the text from her mother that she'd known would be there, sent at 6:02 because her mother considered 6:00 itself too aggressive and 6:05 a capitulation:

*Eleonora. The deadline for the Hargreave scheme is the 15th. I have sent you the link three times. Your cousin Tobi did it and he is at the firm now and his mother could not stop talking at church, it was honestly too much, but the point stands. Two years. You do the two years, you have a name behind you, THEN if you still want to do — and here, Ella could see it even in text, the word held at arm's length with tongs — your fashion, you do it from a position of something. Not from nothing. Your father and I did not cross water so that you could do nothing and call it freedom. Eat something. You sound thin even in your texts. Love, Mummy.*

Ella read it twice in the orange dark, and felt all of it at once — the irritation and the love and the guilt and the old, old exhaustion, the way they were not separate feelings but a single braided one she'd been carrying so long she no longer felt where it attached to her.

Because here was the thing, the thing that made it impossible, the thing that would have been so much easier if her mother were simply a villain: her mother was *right*, about everything except the conclusion. They *had* crossed water. Folake Savage-Oluwa had been a doctor in Lagos and a healthcare assistant in Lewisham for six years before the British system condescended to recognise a fraction of what she already was; Dele had driven minicabs and worked nights and built, brick by patient brick, the modest solid life that now hosted fifty people at a birthday and fed the whole road at Christmas. They had done it by being, as the saying in the house went, *twice as good and half as visible* — by pouring themselves into work that did not love them back and asking, in exchange, only that their children stand somewhere higher and safer than the place they'd started. And Ella, the eldest, the proof, the first British-born, the one the whole project had been *for* — Ella was proposing to take the high safe ground they'd bought with their bodies and use it to do something that, to her mother, looked indistinguishable from the precarious creative striving that had ruined half the men in the family. Uncle Femi and the restaurant. Cousin Side and the music that never came to anything. *Prettiness.* A hobby with delusions.

She understood it. That was the trap. She understood it so completely that she could have written her mother's side of the argument better than her mother could, and the understanding did not change the fact that the only place in her entire life where she was not performing being fine — where she was not the eldest, the strong one, the one who handled it, the project manager of everyone else's feelings — was the place where she sat on a skip-rescued board in a launderette-warm room and built a world out of a sound.

She typed: *I'll look at the link, Mum. I'm eating. Love you.*

She did not say the true thing, which was: *I didn't apply and I'm not going to.* She'd say it eventually. She'd been rehearsing it for weeks. But not at half one in the morning by text, not as an afterthought; the true thing deserved a table and her mother's face and her own steady voice, and she did not have any of those things tonight, so she sent the small lie that kept the peace and hated herself for it the precise, manageable amount.

Then she sat in the dark a while longer, and — because the human mind is a traitor and a comedian — found that the cold administrative voice and the warm wanting voice and the braided exhausted family voice had all, briefly, been crowded out by a fourth thing, an irritating, unwelcome, low-frequency hum underneath all of them, the same frequency as the dryers, the same frequency as the bass through the floor of a house on Egerton Road.

*I came over anyway.*

"No," she told the room.

The room, like the rain, declined to argue. The dryers tumbled. Out on the street the orange light did its work on the puddles, beautiful and slightly ruined, and Ella Savage-Oluwa, who saw everything, did not yet see the shape of the four months coming toward her, or the boy at the centre of them, or the version of herself she was going to have to become to survive both. She finished her tea. She went to bed. And she dreamed, irritatingly, in four notes — three that climbed and one that fell — though she would not remember it in the morning, and could not have known, yet, whose melody it was, or how far it had already travelled to find her.

## ◆ iii. Set Piece

*Cal*

Six a.m. and the pitch was the colour of a bruise, frost burning off it in the floodlights, the breath of fifteen young men hanging in the lit cold like the souls were trying to leave the bodies early, and Cal Vance was running the same line for the fourth time while his father stood at the touchline with his arms folded and said nothing — which was worse than anything he could have said, and his father knew it was worse, and that was the point.

"Again," called Marsh, the academy backs coach, from somewhere off to the left, and Cal was already turning before the word finished, already resetting, because the thing about being the one they came to watch was that the watching never stopped, not at six in the morning, not in the rain, not ever. He took the ball flat off the imaginary scrum-half, drifted two steps across the imaginary line, fixed the imaginary defender with his eyes — *look at the man you're not passing to, that's the whole trick, sell him the lie with your face* — and put the pass into the space where the winger would be. Soft. Weighted. The ball hung in the cold floodlit air for exactly as long as it needed to hang and not a fraction longer, a spiral so clean it looked slow, and dropped into the hands of nobody, because there was no winger, because it was a drill, because at six in the morning the space he was passing into was purely theoretical and he filled it perfectly anyway.

He had thrown ten thousand of those. He could throw it asleep. He sometimes did, in the grey country between waking and dreaming, the ball leaving his hands and never quite landing, hanging forever in a floodlit dark, and he would surface from it with his fingers twitching the shape of the release.

"Better," Marsh said. "That's the one. Why's that one different from the other three?"

"Didn't think about it," Cal said.

"Then stop thinking about the other ones."

Easy to say. Cal jogged back to the mark, breath smoking, and let his eyes go to the touchline for half a second — just half, no more, you never gave it more than half — and his father gave him the nod. The small one. The minimal downward tilt of the chin that had run Cal's entire life since he was six years old and first understood, with a child's terrible clarity, that the nod was a thing you earned and the absence of it was a thing you survived. Gareth Vance had played eleven seasons in the second row for clubs that didn't pay enough and broke him in places that still woke him when it rained, and he had decided, somewhere down in the wreckage of his own finished career, that his son was going to have the one he hadn't. He had decided it the way other men decide to build a house — as a project, with a timeline, brick by brick, no days off, no contingency, no acknowledgement that the materials were a living boy who might have wanted to build something else.

*His son.* Singular. Cal shut that down before it could open into the plural it used to be. Not at six a.m. Not on the pitch. There were rooms inside him that he kept locked through the daylight hours, locked and dark and load-bearing, and one of them had a name on the door, and he had become, over five years, extremely good at the locking — so good that he could run a perfect drill on top of the locked room and never once feel the floor flex.

They ran for another hour. By the end of it the sun was a grey rumour behind the main stand and the frost had gone to mud and three of the younger lads were watching Cal the way he used to watch the seniors when he was their age — with that specific hunger, that *how do I get to be that, what is the secret, is it a thing I can be told* — and Cal gave them the version of himself they needed to see. Easy. Unbothered. A word for each of them on the way off, a hand on a shoulder, a small correction offered as encouragement. *You're good. You're getting there. Watch your support line in the second phase, you're a yard slow folding round.* It cost him nothing and it bought him everything, because that was the entire economy of being Callum Vance in the daylight: you gave them the swagger, you gave them the ease, you let them believe it was free, and in exchange they never once thought to wonder what it was costing you to produce, or whether the still calm centre of you was calm at all, or whether — and this was the unaskable question, the one no teammate had ever come close to — the boy they'd elected the freest among them might in fact be the one holding his breath.

"Pub Thursday," Tom said, falling into step beside him as they came off, steam rising off both of them like a pair of just-finished horses. Tom Bryce was the only one of them Cal would have called a friend without a silent asterisk after the word — too big to be threatened by anyone, too fundamentally kind to bother with the locker-room politics, the rare lad who had worked out somewhere around eighteen that you could simply *like* people, that it was allowed, that it didn't cost you status. "Priya's lot are coming again. The art crowd. You should actually come this time."

"I'm working Thursday."

"You're always working Thursday. You don't even *have* a job, that's the thing that does my head in." Tom said it without heat, just baffled, the bafflement of a man who had spent two years watching his friend vanish after dark and return with shadows under his eyes and no story to account for them. "You disappear, like, four nights a week. You come back looking like you've been up till four. You've got money you don't explain. If you weren't the most boring man alive I'd think you were dealing. Where do you *go*, Cal? Genuinely. I've never once known where you go."

"To bed early," Cal said. "Like a professional. Some of us are trying to make it."

"Aye, and some of us are trying to have a *life*." Tom grinned, unbothered, used to the wall, having long ago decided that whatever was behind it was Cal's business and that loyalty meant not trying the handle. He bumped Cal with a shoulder that could have moved a car. "Anyway. The art crowd. One of Priya's mates was at the thing at Egerton Road. The stylist. Did Priya's whole look for the formal, apparently, you'd have seen it, the one with the —" Tom gestured vaguely at his own ear. "Tall. Sharp. Tom said —" he caught himself, because Tom referring to himself in the third person was a tell that he was about to enjoy something. "*I* said your name to her and she said it back with a *tone.*"

Cal kept his face exactly where it was. This was a skill, and like the flat pass he had built it with ten thousand repetitions until it looked like nothing, until it looked like the absence of effort rather than its mastery. "Which one."

"Don't do that. Don't do the 'which one.' You talked to her. At the party. *Voluntarily.* You stood in a corner with one girl for five minutes and then walked off and stood on your own being weird for the rest of the night, which, for you, is basically a marriage proposal. Her name's Ella." Tom watched him sidelong with the dim, delighted cunning of a very large man who has spotted something small and is pleased with himself for it. "You like her."

"I read the room wrong," Cal said, which was true, and which was very possibly the single most honest sentence he would speak all day.

Because he had. He'd gone over to Ella Savage-Oluwa for exactly the reasons she'd laid out to his face on the table between them — because she was the one not performing, in a room where everyone, himself most of all, was performing, and he was tired, a bone-deep five-year tiredness, of performing. He'd expected to run the play. The read, the flattery, the engineered closeness, the soft landing. He was good at the play; the play worked; the play had never once not worked. And she had taken it apart on the table in front of him, calm as a surgeon, naming each move a half-second before he made it, dismantling not just the approach but the *architecture* of it, the part he'd thought was invisible, the part he'd thought was *him* — and instead of the irritation he should have felt, instead of the small managed embarrassment, he had felt, of all the unwelcome things, *relief.* Like setting something heavy down. Like being caught at something he'd half-wanted to be caught at for years. For one second in that disgusting corner he had wanted to say something true to a stranger, and the wanting had frightened him so badly that he had done the only safe thing available, which was to lose on purpose, admit it, and leave.

He hadn't stopped thinking about it since. He was thinking about it now, in the showers, with the water going from scalding to lukewarm the way it always did when fifteen of them showered at once, and that was a problem — a budget problem — because Cal Vance did not have spare attention to spend. His attention was a fixed quantity and it was fully allocated and had been for years: so much to the rugby, the visible life, the project his father was building; so much to the other thing, the night thing, the locked-room thing; and a thin protective margin held in reserve against the daily labour of keeping those two from ever touching. There was no line item for a girl. There had never needed to be. And now there was a small persistent overdraft in a part of the ledger he hadn't known was open, and it was drawing down the reserve, the protective margin, the very fund that kept the worlds apart — and some animal part of him understood that this was dangerous in a way he couldn't yet name, and the rest of him, the calculating part, the part that read the odds, looked at the danger and did the thing it had never done before with a clear warning in front of it.

It decided it didn't care.

## ◆ iv. The Room With No Sign

*Cal*

The studio was above a vintage shop on the edge of the Northern Quarter, up a staircase that smelled of damp and old paper and the particular cold of a building that has been many things and is currently between purposes. There was no sign on the door, because the whole point was that there was no sign; the absence was the architecture. Cal had taken the lease under a name that wasn't his — well, that was a lie he told even to himself, a careful one. The name *was* his. It was the only name that had ever felt entirely his own, unbuilt by anyone, un-nodded-at, un-coached, the single syllable he'd chosen rather than inherited: **V.** He paid for the room in cash he made from the work he did in it, which made it the one clean closed loop in his entire life — the music paid for the room where he made the music, and no one stood at the edge of it with their arms folded, and there was no project, and there was no nod to earn or survive, and the only judge of whether a thing was good was whether it made the hair stand up on his own forearms, which was a tyrant of a standard but at least it was *his* tyrant.

He let himself in a little after nine, when the city below had already got going — delivery vans double-parked on the cobbles, the metal shutter of the vintage shop rattling up, the first of the coffee places putting their boards out — and he climbed the cold stairs and let himself into the room and did not turn the big lights on. He never turned the big lights on first. He turned on the lamp, and the glow of the screen, and he sat down at the desk in the brown half-dark, and he exhaled for what felt like the first time since the alarm had gone at half five.

This was the part nobody knew. This was the vault.

Cal Vance made beats. Not as a hobby, not as a laugh, not as the thing the lads would have called "messing about on a laptop." He made the records that other people's faces went on. Three tracks on a mixtape that had done a quarter of a million streams and counting. A whole six-track EP for an artist out of Moss Side that the blogs had genuinely lost their minds over — *who produced this, who is this, where did this sound come from* — and the answer to every one of those questions was a single credit that read **V.** and nothing else: no face, no socials, no interviews, no government name. Because Cal Vance the fly-half, the academy prospect, the boy scouts came to watch, could not also be the boy who made the kind of woozy, late, broken, three-in-the-morning sound that the rugby world would have read, at absolute best, as a distraction — and at worst, in the specific cosmology of his father, as the thing. The wasteful thing. The thing with no living in it. The thing that had taken Jordan.

He didn't let the name into the room. Not yet. He let the work do what the name-keeping couldn't, which was hold the exact shape of a feeling without ever letting it out into the air where it could be seen and therefore lost.

He pulled up the session he'd been building for three weeks — a thing for Knox, the only artist he worked with face to face, the only living person who knew that V was Cal. It was nearly there, the track, and nearly-there was the most dangerous place a thing could be, because nearly-there asked you to decide what *there* was. A drum pattern that hung back half a beat, reluctant, like it didn't want to arrive at its own party. A vocal he'd chopped and pitched down until it sounded like a memory of itself, a happiness recalled rather than felt. A low synth that didn't play notes so much as move across them, like weather coming over the rooftops he could see from the high window. He'd been trying, for three weeks, to make it sound like the inside of his own head, which was an insane brief to give yourself, and he had very nearly done it, which was the trouble — because the inside of his own head was not a place he'd ever wanted anyone to hear, and here it was, two minutes and forty seconds of it, with no words at all, and it sounded like six a.m. on a frozen pitch and a nod earned and a room locked and, now, newly, intrusively, in the top of it, in the bright part he hadn't authorised — a girl taking his best move apart on a table and laughing at him and being right.

He took the chopped vocal out. The track got colder, truer, lonelier. He put it back. It got warmer, and the warmth was the lie, and the lie was nicer. He took it out again. He sat with the cold true version for a while, and then — and this was the part he would never have explained to Tom, to anyone — he opened a different file. A clean one. And without quite deciding to, his hands found a slow kick, a heartbeat at rest, and a hi-hat that came in late and reluctant the way he liked them, the way he'd learned them off someone, and over the top of it he laid a sample he'd had sitting on a drive for months, a woman's voice off an old crackling soul record going *oh, oh*, pitched down until it sounded like someone remembering being happy a long time ago — and underneath all of it, four feet down, buried so deep that you would feel it in your chest before you ever heard it in your ears, where no one would ever find it and no one was ever meant to, he laid four notes.

Three that climbed. One that fell.

He listened back to the clean file once, in the lamp-lit dark, with the city going about its indifferent morning business below the high window, and it sounded like the precise sensation of being seen through and crossing the room anyway. He had not set out to make that. He had set out to make nothing; he had opened a clean file the way another man might have gone for a walk. But there it was, made, a thing in the world that hadn't existed an hour ago, and at the centre of it, doing what it always did, holding the structure up from underneath, was his brother — and laid over the top of his brother, in the bright unfamiliar part, was a stranger he'd met for five minutes and lost to on purpose.

He saved it under a name that was just the date. He did not name it her name. He didn't have her name; he'd been too busy losing to ask for it. He would have it by Thursday, as it turned out, though he didn't know that yet, and once he had it he would never be able to give it back, but for now the file sat on the drive called only by the day, and Cal Vance sat in front of it in the dark and understood, with the cold clear part of him that had never once been wrong about a defensive line, that he had just done something he could not undo: he had let a living person into the room where he kept his dead one, and the room had only ever had space for the locking, and now there was something in it that wanted, against every law he had ever made for himself, to be *un*locked.

His phone lit on the desk, face down; he turned it over. Knox. **studio thurs? bringing the team. label want a moodboard person, some art direction girl apparently sick with it, real vision, you'll like the sound of her stuff. trust.**

Cal looked at the message for a long time.

Then he turned the phone back over, face down, the way he always did, the way that meant *not now, the world can wait, this room is for the one true thing* — and he went back to the cold true version of the Knox track, the one with no living person in it, and he worked on it until the light through the high window went from grey to gold to grey again, until his stomach reminded him with a lurch that he had not eaten since the protein thing in the car at half five, until the budget of his attention was finally, blessedly, completely spent and there was nothing left over with which to remember anything at all.

He saved the session under its non-name. He turned off the lamp. He stood at the high window for one minute looking down at the wet cobbled street and the people on it, each of them, he assumed, getting to be only one person, a single self walking around in the daylight with nothing locked — and then he went back down the cold stairs that smelled of old paper, and out into the rain that a South London girl had walked off into the night before, and he put the swagger back on the way you put on a coat at a door, and he went to be Callum Vance, the one they came to watch, and he was so good at it that not one person he passed all day thought to ask who was watching *him.*

Not until Thursday. But he didn't know that yet, and so for one more day the worlds stayed in their boxes, and the locked room stayed locked, and the file with the date for a name sat in the dark holding a dead brother and a living stranger four feet apart, waiting — the way the whole city seemed to be waiting, under its permanent fine orange rain — for the thing that was already coming up the stairs.

## ◆ v. The Architecture of a Locked Room

*Cal*

People thought a double life was a dramatic thing. Cal had learned that it wasn't. A double life was not a thing of disguises and near-misses and pounding hearts; it was a thing of *logistics*, of a thousand small unglamorous decisions made so consistently that they became a kind of weather. You did not get caught living two lives because of one operatic error. You got caught because you got tired, and tiredness made you careless about the small things, and it was always the small things — a tagged location, a session file with the wrong name on it, a producer credit somebody decided to dig into on a slow afternoon, a teammate who looked over your shoulder at the wrong second. So Cal did not allow himself to be careless, ever, which meant he did not allow himself to be tired, which was of course impossible, because he was always tired, and so the whole structure ran on a reserve of vigilance that he topped up by sleeping less, which made him more tired, which required more vigilance. It was a machine that consumed its own fuel. He'd been running it since he was seventeen and he had stopped, somewhere along the way, being able to imagine what it would feel like to switch it off.

The week between the party and the Thursday ran the way every week ran, which was on rails he'd laid himself and now could not get off.

Monday: gym at six, video analysis at eight — an hour of watching himself on a screen while Marsh paused and rewound and said *there, see, you've told the fullback where you're going with your hips, you've got to learn to lie with your hips* — then a seminar he was technically enrolled in, Sport and Exercise Science, where he sat at the back and did not speak and got firsts anyway because the work was easy and he had nothing to spend his attention on but easy work. Monday night: the studio, until two, mixing the Knox track, taking the vocal out and putting it back.

Tuesday: contact session, which meant he came home with a thumb-print bruise blooming on his hip and a graze down one forearm that he'd have to keep covered, because grazes asked questions and he didn't do questions. Tuesday night: the studio, a session with Knox himself, the two of them and a bag of cans and the rare specific joy of making something with the one person alive who knew his real name — Knox draped over the battered sofa freestyling nonsense until the nonsense suddenly wasn't, until a line landed and they both went still, *that, do that again, keep that* — and for a few hours the machine switched off, because Knox required no performance, Knox had met the real one years ago in a youth-club studio before either of them was anyone, and you cannot perform for a person who knew you before the performance existed.

Wednesday: recovery, ice baths, a nutritionist's spreadsheet, a sponsor thing he had to smile through where a man from a protein company called him "the future" four times and Cal said the easy gracious things and watched the man's eyes do the thing eyes did around him now, the *I knew him before* calculation, the small investment of attention people made in the not-yet-famous on the off chance. Wednesday night: not the studio. Wednesday night he made himself sleep, eight hours, professionally, because Saturday was a fixture and the body was the asset and you serviced the asset whether or not you wanted to, especially when you didn't want to.

And under all of it, every day, the locked room. The one with the name on the door.

He let himself near it exactly once that week, and only because the new file made him.

It was late Tuesday, after Knox had gone, the studio dark except for the lamp, and Cal had meant to keep working on the Knox track and had instead opened the clean file — the one with the date for a name, the one with the woman's voice going *oh, oh* and the bright unauthorised part and the four buried notes — and he'd listened to it the whole way through with his eyes shut, and at the end of it he'd done the thing he never did. He'd got out his phone, and he'd opened the message thread he could not delete and could not open, the one that said **Jords** at the top with a photo of his brother pulling a stupid face in a car, and he'd looked at the last message in it without playing it.

A voice note. 0:47. Sent at 23:14 on a Thursday — and wasn't that a thing, that it had been a Thursday, that the worst day of his life had been a Thursday and here he was dreading and craving another one — five years and three months ago. He knew the waveform of it by heart, the little visual mountain range of his brother's last forty-seven seconds, even though he hadn't let it make a sound in over a year. *Listen to this, Cal, listen to the bit at the end.* He knew that was what it said because he'd heard it, once, the day after, when sixteen-year-old Cal had finally looked at his phone and found two-days-old proof that his brother had been alive and excited and wanting to share something at 23:14 on the Thursday, had been a person with a future and a bit at the end he was proud of, right up until a wet road outside Leeds had decided otherwise somewhere around midnight, while Cal was asleep, while the voice note sat unopened because Cal had been at training and then tired and then it could wait, because everything could always wait, because he was sixteen and his brother was permanent.

He didn't play it. He looked at the waveform in the lamp-light, the little mountains of his brother's voice, and then he locked the phone and put it face down and went back to the clean file and listened, instead, to the four notes he'd buried in it — the same four notes, transposed, hidden, encrypted into a place his father would never accidentally find them, the only grave he'd been able to dig that no one could desecrate by visiting. *Three that climb. One that falls.* He'd put them in everything for five years. He'd put his brother in a quarter of a million strangers' headphones without one of them knowing they were carrying a dead boy around in their pocket, playing him on buses, falling asleep to him. It was insane. He knew it was insane. It was also the single most sane thing he did, the one act in his whole logistical machine of a life that was not in service of a project or a performance but was simply, stubbornly, love — secret love, ghost love, the love of a brother who hadn't replied for two days and would spend the rest of his life replying in the only language they'd ever shared, in the one place his father couldn't follow, four feet down, under the drums, where it was safe because it was already buried, because you cannot lose a thing twice.

His mother would have understood the new file. She was the only one who would have. But his mother was in a small flat in Catford with another of Jordan's graves — the literal room, kept exactly as it had been, the decks and the milk crate of records and the spider plant somehow still alive — and Cal did not call her enough, because calling her meant standing in the one relationship that had never required him to perform and discovering, every time, how exhausted the performing had left him, and the exhaustion was easier to manage if he didn't keep measuring it against the one place it lifted. She'd left when he was fourteen. Not because she'd stopped loving them — he understood that now, mostly — but because she couldn't live inside the way Gareth turned grief into a project, couldn't watch the machine being built around the surviving son, and so she had done the only thing left to a person who can't bear to watch a thing happen, which was to leave the room. The family's original ghost. Cal had inherited the gene. He just hadn't admitted it yet, hadn't yet had it named for him by a girl on a chip-shop step, hadn't yet understood that he'd made himself into a ghost on purpose, that the detachment everyone read as cool was a held breath, that *if you're already gone, you can't be left.*

That naming was still weeks away. For now he just worked, and locked the room, and ran the machine, and got tired, and topped up the vigilance, and somewhere underneath the whole structure the new file sat humming with a danger he could feel and couldn't source — the danger of having made, for the first time in five years, a piece of music that wanted to be heard by one specific living person rather than felt, anonymously, by a quarter of a million strangers who'd never know his name.

## ◆ vi. The Grind

*Ella*

Priya came home on the Tuesday evening dropping bags and talking before the door was shut, which was how Priya entered every room, as a weather system with opinions. "Okay so don't be weird, but I have a thing, and you have to not be weird about it, because you do a thing where I tell you something good and you immediately find the catch, and just for once —"

"I don't do that."

"You do the *exact* thing. You're doing the face that's getting ready to do it." Priya dropped onto the floor opposite her in the launderette-warm dark, which she only did for major announcements; minor ones she delivered standing up, walking around, half in the kitchen. The fact that she was sitting made Ella put her laptop down. "But this one's actually big, so. Tom knows everyone, right, because rugby boys somehow know the entire city, it's like a secret civic infrastructure of large men. And one of Tom's mates does sound for people, and he's mates with Knox's lot. *Knox*, Ella. Marcus. The 'Lowlight' one."

Ella's whole body went still. "*That* Knox."

"That Knox. He's making a proper project — a real album, not a mixtape, a real thing, and the label's behind it now, and they want —" Priya did jazz hands, the universal Priya signal for *brace yourself* "— creative direction. A visual world. A whole identity. Somebody with an actual eye, who isn't some forty-five-year-old man who thinks Black British music is a Halloween costume he can put on for a campaign. And Tom mentioned you. And Knox's people went and looked at the stuff you put up — the 'Lowlight' reimagining you did for *free*, for fun, the one with the orange and the wrong film stock — and apparently Knox himself saw it and said the word *yes* out loud, which according to the sound guy basically never happens, and they want to meet you. As a real candidate. For the real job. At their studio. This Thursday."

The dryers tumbled beneath them. Ella did not say anything for so long that Priya's whole face began the slow collapse of someone whose surprise party has gone wrong.

"Say something. Is it not — I thought it was — it's good, isn't it? It's hard to tell with you, you've got the same face for 'I've won an award' and 'someone has died.'"

"Priya." Ella's voice came out strange, too careful, the voice she used to hold something heavy without letting it show in her arms. "Do you actually understand what this is."

"It's... a job? A good job?"

"It's *the* project." Ella heard her own voice climb and made it stay level, the discipline of not wanting a thing too loudly in case the wanting jinxed it. "It's a real artist — a real one, the kids at Tio's school have his lyrics in their bios — with a real label, a real release, a real budget. If I art-direct Knox's album, then I don't have to *find* a brand for my final project. The project *becomes* the final project. The thing I'd be making for a living becomes the thing I put in the degree show, in front of the external assessors, in front of the recruiters who come round on the last day with their lanyards deciding whose number to take. It's not a fictional brand in a folder. It's a thing on the actual radio with my actual name in the actual credits." She pressed both hands flat over her face. "Priya. It's everything I've been pitching for, for two months, two months of polite nos and silence, and it just — landed. In our front room. Above a launderette. Because your boyfriend is enormous and knows everyone."

"So that's the good face?" Priya ventured. "I can never tell with you, you're the most opaque person I've ever —"

"It's the good face." Ella lowered her hands, and she was grinning now, properly, the unmanaged one, the one that got loose maybe twice a year. "I could kiss Tom. I could kiss his enormous stupid head."

"Please don't, he's mine, but I'll pass it on."

And then the second feeling came, right behind the joy, the way it always did, the cold administrative tenant who lived under all of Ella's hopes and charged rent on them. *Don't want it this much. If you want it this much and it goes wrong, the size of the wreckage is the size of the want, and you have a degree to finish and rent due on the first and a mother at six o'clock, and you cannot afford a want this size, you have never been able to afford a want any size, that's the whole point of you, you're the one who manages the wants so the others can have theirs.* She felt it move through her, familiar, almost comforting in its familiarity, and for once — maybe because Priya was sitting on the floor, which she only did for the big ones; maybe because the orange light was doing the thing it did; maybe because some animal part of her had already started dreaming in four notes — Ella looked the cold tenant in the eye and said, internally, *not this one. This one I'm allowed to want.*

"I need to build a deck," she said, already on her feet, already moving, the tiredness burned clean off her by the only fuel that had ever fully worked on her, which was a real chance. "A proper one. Not a moodboard, not free-work — a pitch. I need to listen to everything he's ever made between now and Thursday and find the world that's already inside it, and walk into that room with the world *already built*, so completely built that they can't imagine the project without me in it. So that saying no to me feels like demolishing something instead of just declining something."

"It's eight o'clock on a Tuesday."

"Then I've got till Thursday minus eight o'clock on a Tuesday." She looked at Priya, and Priya saw the face — not the opaque one, the other one, the one Ella only ever showed the work, the one that was naked in a way Ella never let herself be naked in front of people. "I'm not going to be safe, Pri. I've been safe my whole life. I've been the safe one *for everyone else.* I'm going to be *good*. And I'm going to be so good in that room on Thursday that they cannot say no to me."

She did not sleep much that week either. She built a world. She listened to every track Knox had ever put out — the early stuff that was all hunger and rough edges, the middle stuff where he'd found the voice, the recent things produced by the mysterious *V* that had a different quality entirely, a depth, a late-night ache, a sound that wasn't slick but *intimate*, like overhearing someone's actual interior — and she built around it the thing she would later describe to a label as *a secret that got out.* She did not sleep, and she did not look at the Hargreave link, and she texted her mother *eating well, working hard, love you* at increasingly creative hours, and exactly once, on the Wednesday, at three in the morning, she found herself thinking — entirely unbidden, with real annoyance — about a boy who'd said *I came over anyway* and then walked off and won by losing, and she shut the thought in the same drawer as the cold tenant and went back to the work, because the work was real and the boy was a complication and she had no room in the budget of her life for a complication, and the studio she was going to on Thursday was just a room, and the ghost producer behind the boards was just a man with a mystique, and she would probably never even meet him.

She was wrong about exactly one of those things. But on the Wednesday night, with the deck nearly built and the world nearly real and her whole future apparently fitting through a door in the Northern Quarter on a Thursday afternoon, she didn't know that yet, and she let herself, for once, sleep four whole hours, and she dreamed again in four notes she didn't recognise — three that climbed and one that fell — and woke not remembering them, only with the strange sensation, as she made her coffee in the grey Hulme morning, of being homesick for somewhere she had not yet been.
